# The Second Sun

*A short story of the Colossus of Rhodes*

---

## I. The dowry (304 BC)

Kleio was nine when the city promised to pay for her wedding.

Her father had died on the harbour wall in the first summer of the siege, the summer Demetrius brought his towers in on ships. Afterwards the assembly voted that the daughters of the fallen would be dowered from the public purse. A clerk with ink ground into his thumb wrote her name on a tablet, and her mother cried in a way Kleio had not heard before, as if something had been handed back to her.

"The city will marry you," her mother said. "The city owes us that."

That year the city owed a great many things. When the Besieger finally made his peace and sailed away, he left his engines behind in the churned ground outside the walls: the stone-throwers, the rams, and the great tower they called *Helepolis*, the City-Taker. It had nine storeys and eight iron-rimmed wheels, and it had taken three thousand men to push it. The children went out to look at it the way children go to look at a dead whale. Its iron skin was buckled and scorched where the fire-missiles had struck all night, and through the gaps you could see charred wood. Someone had already scratched a rude picture of Demetrius on one wheel.

"They're going to sell every nail of it," said Timon, whose father cast bronze in the potters' quarter. He was ten and knew everything. "And with the money they're going to make a god."

"Which god?"

Timon squinted and pointed straight up, where the sun sat white over the sea.

"That one."

## II. The hill that grew (c. 292 to 280 BC)

Chares of Lindos was a small man, which the workshop found endlessly funny. He had trained under Lysippos, who made Alexander's portraits, and he talked about Lysippos the way old sailors talk about a storm they came through.

By the time the feet were set on their white marble base, Timon worked for him and was Kleio's husband. The public dowry had bought them two rooms near the harbour, a loom, and a clay stove. Timon came home every evening grey with charcoal dust, with green under his fingernails.

"Seventy cubits," he told her in the first year, lying on his back in the dark. "Seventy. Eleven men standing on each other's heads."

The workshop had a joke it never told where Chares could hear. The Rhodians, it went, had asked Chares what a statue would cost, and then what one twice as tall would cost, and he had said *double*. Every caster in the yard knew what he should have said. Make a thing twice as tall and it is also twice as wide and twice as deep, so it needs eight times the bronze. The men laughed at it the way you laugh at a knife you have to walk past every day.

Kleio never saw the god being made. Nobody did. What the city saw was a hill.

You could not lift a bronze thigh the height of a house, Timon said, so they did not try. They cast the god where he stood, one course on top of the last, like building a wall. As each course was finished they heaped earth around it, so that the next could be poured from what was once again ground level. The hill rose ring by ring. There was a ramp and a line of donkeys with baskets, and at the top, always, a crown of smoke, a raw bright edge of new metal, and men in leather aprons shouting over the roar of the furnaces. Inside the rising bronze, other men stacked cut stone and bolted it with iron bars as thick as a girl's wrist, so the god would have something to stand on from within.

"He has bones," Timon said. "Stone bones. Chares says the bronze is only the skin."

For twelve years the hill grew. Kleio had three children while it grew. Her daughter learned to walk on the ramp's lower slope, among the donkey droppings and broken moulds. The wine jars that came into port that decade were stamped with the year in the old way, *in the priesthood of So-and-so*, which meant the year that man served as priest of the Sun. Kleio could not read, but she learned the look of the stamps. She liked to think each one marked a year the hill had risen a little more.

Then, in the twelfth year, they took the hill away.

It took months. The same donkeys carried the same earth down again, and as the soil came off, the god came up out of it. It was not like watching a statue unveiled. It was like watching a man wade out of the sea. First the crown of sunbeams that had stood alone on the summit all that last season. Then the face, then the curling hair, then the shoulders, broad enough for a ship to rest on. One arm was raised, the hand held flat against the brow as though the god were shading his eyes to look at something far off. Kleio stood among the crowd on the day the last basket came off his feet, and she understood what he was looking at. He was looking at his brother, the other sun, in the sky. He looked back at it as an equal.

The people of Rhodes carved a verse on the base. A man read it aloud for those who could not read it themselves:

> *To thy very self, O Sun, did the people of Dorian Rhodes raise high to heaven this colossus, then, when having laid to rest the brazen wave of war, they crowned their country with the spoils of their foes.*

"Spoils of their foes," Timon muttered. His hands were ruined by then, burned and knotted. "Spoils of their foes and twelve years of my knees."

But he was smiling. That evening at the harbour, when the light went gold and long, the god caught it and blazed, and the whole city stopped for a moment to watch. Kleio held her daughter's hand. She thought of the clerk with the inky thumb, and of a tower rotting in the mud, and of how a city that could not afford to lose a war had somehow afforded this.

"There," she said to the girl. "That is what your grandfather bought."

## III. Broken at the knees (c. 226 BC)

Kleio was very old when the ground moved.

She was so old that she had outlived Timon, and Chares, and two of her three children. She had become the woman the neighbourhood called *Grandmother* whether they were related to her or not. Her great-granddaughter Nikarete slept on a pallet beside her, because Kleio had begun to wake in the night and forget which decade she was in, and a child's warm back reminded her.

The earthquake came before dawn. First a sound like a cart on a stone road, but underneath everything. Then the pots walking off the shelves. Then a noise Nikarete would try to describe for the rest of her life and never manage: a single enormous note, lower than any bell, which went on and on and then stopped.

In the grey light they went out with everyone else. Walls were down along the harbour. The dockyards, where it was death to go without leave, stood open to the street. And the god was not in the sky.

He lay across the ground where he had fallen, snapped at the knees. The lower legs still stood on their marble base like two bronze towers, and the rest of him stretched away in a long, terrible line of bronze and stone. His face was turned to one side. The raised hand had come down with him.

People were weeping. A priest was tearing his clothes. Nikarete clung to her great-grandmother's hand and waited for the old woman to cry too.

Kleio laughed.

It was a small, dry laugh, and people turned to stare. "He's tired," she said. "Seventy years is a long time to stand. I should know."

---

What happened next, Nikarete learned slowly, the way children learn what adults are really doing.

The city sent ambassadors to every king in the world, and the ambassadors did not play down the disaster. They made it enormous. They wept in foreign courts, and the kings wept back and sent gifts. Syracuse sent silver and cauldrons, and put up statues in the market showing Syracuse placing a crown on the head of Rhodes. The king in Egypt promised grain by the million measures, timber for twenty warships, and three thousand talents of silver to raise the god again, ten times what he had first cost. He even promised a hundred master builders and three hundred and fifty masons to do the work.

And the city consulted an oracle. The oracle said (and here the story always went a little vague in the telling) that the god should not be raised.

So the Rhodians took the grain, and the timber, and the silver, and the masons, and they rebuilt their walls and their dockyards and their gymnasium, and they left the Sun lying where he fell.

"Isn't that stealing?" Nikarete asked. She was twelve by then and had strong opinions about fairness.

Kleio was nearly blind now. She sat in the doorway with her face turned toward the warmth.

"When I was a girl," she said, "your grandfather's grandfather died on the wall, and the city promised to pay for my wedding. It paid for two rooms and a loom and a stove. It did not pay for your great-grandfather. I chose him myself, and he was worth far more than the city ever gave." She felt for the girl's hand. "A city takes what it's given and spends it on what it needs. The god doesn't mind. He's done his work. Let him rest."

Nikarete did not think that was a real answer. But years later, when she climbed with other children into the broken end of one bronze leg, she understood a little better. It was dark in there, and cool, and full of the great rough blocks of stone Chares had set as the god's bones. She put her palm flat on a block that her great-grandfather might have lifted. She could feel, in the quiet, that the god was not dead, only lying down.

## IV. The thumb (the 70s AD)

Three hundred years later, a Roman official on his way to somewhere more important stopped at Rhodes. He had read about the famous ruin and asked to be shown it.

The Rhodians were polite about it, the way Rhodians were about everything. They walked sedately, did not strut in the street, and applauded at the theatre by clicking their tongues instead of shouting. A girl selling figs near the ruin offered to be his guide, for a small coin.

He wrote it down that night, more or less as he found it. The fallen giant was still a wonder even lying on the ground. Where the limbs had broken off, enormous cavities yawned, and inside you could see the great masses of rock the artist had used to steady it. The fingers were larger than most statues.

And the thumb! The fig-seller showed him the game every child in the city played. You pressed yourself against the thumb, stretched both arms around it, and tried to make your fingers meet on the other side.

Almost nobody could.

The Roman tried, laughing, with his toga bunched under his arms, and came up a hand's breadth short. The fig-seller tried and came up shorter. Then she shrugged and said something in her own dialect that he did not catch, and patted the bronze. The gesture was absent and affectionate, the way you would pat an old dog asleep in the sun.

He asked her what she had said.

"Only what my grandmother used to say," she told him. "He's resting. He was the second sun. He stood up for seventy years so we wouldn't have to kneel. Now it's our turn to stand."

She held out her hand for the coin.

### A note on what is true

The frame is invented: Kleio, Timon, Nikarete and the fig-seller never existed. Most of what happens around them is sourced, and this repo's research notes (`research/01-*`, `research/02-*`, `research/jamie-03-colossus.md`) carry the citations.

- **The dowries** for the daughters of the fallen were voted during the siege (Diodorus 20.84). The **Helepolis**, with its nine storeys, eight iron-rimmed wheels, 3,400 pushers and night of fire-missiles, is from Diodorus 20.91-97.
- **The money** came from selling Demetrius's abandoned engines, 300 talents, and the work took **twelve years** (Pliny, *Natural History* 34.41). The height was **seventy cubits** (Strabo 14.2.5).
- **"Double, not eight times"** is Sextus Empiricus's anecdote about Chares. It is a lesson about cubic scaling, not history.
- **The earth mound and casting in courses** come from pseudo-Philo, a late-antique writer. Scholars are split: Vedder rejects the method, and Badoud (2024) thinks it probably authentic. **Stone blocks bolted with iron** inside the figure are in both Philo and Pliny. The phrase **"a second Helios facing the first"** is Philo's.
- **The pose.** A crowned, nude youth with curling hair matches Rhodian coins. The hand shading the eyes comes from a Rhodian relief of Helios and is a plausible guess. There was no torch (a 1572 invention) and no straddling of the harbour (a medieval fantasy). Nobody knows exactly where the statue stood, so the story does not say.
- **The dedication** is Anthologia Palatina 6.171, in Paton's translation. **Amphorae** were dated by the priest of Helios.
- **The fall.** It was broken at the knees and not raised, "in accordance with a certain oracle" (Strabo). Later tradition names Delphi.
- **The relief effort** is from Polybius 5.88-89, including the 3,000 talents, the 100 builders and the 350 masons, and his cynical remark that the Rhodians talked the disaster up.
- **The thumb, the cavities and the rocks** are Pliny's eyewitness details. The **quiet walking and tongue-clicking applause** are Dio Chrysostom's (Oration 31), from the same Roman century.
- **Left out on purpose:** the story that the scrap was sold in AD 654 and carried off on 900 camels. It is an 8th-century fabrication.

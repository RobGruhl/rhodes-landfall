# In the Priesthood of the Sun

*One day in the life of a young Rhodian, about 255 BC: some twenty-five years after the Colossus was finished, and some twenty-five before it fell*

---

## Before dawn

Timokles woke to the sound of the cistern bucket.

That meant Manes was up. Manes was the household's slave, a Phrygian who had been old when Timokles was born. He always drew the first water of the day, before the street was awake. The rope creaked against the stone lip, and the bucket slapped into the dark water below. Rain from the whole winter was stored down there under the courtyard. Timokles lay still and listened to the house come alive: his mother's voice in the storeroom, his sister Kleino's loom starting its dull knock, and the donkey next door complaining about everything.

He was nineteen. He was a citizen of Rhodes, of the tribe of the Lindians and a deme whose name nobody outside the island had ever heard. He believed, the way you believe the ground is under you, that he lived in the best-run city in the world. Strangers said so. The city itself said so, in stone, on the base of its god.

He pulled on his tunic and went out into the courtyard. The sky was the colour of ash, and the tiled roofs of the block stood black against it. There were three houses to a block, all the same size. His grandfather used to say the old men had drawn the city on a wax tablet before a single wall went up, with every street straight, every block equal, and every family given the same ground to start from. The main streets were wide enough for two carts to pass. The side streets, like theirs, were narrower, with a covered drain running down the middle that took the rain and the rest of it down to the sea.

Breakfast was bread dipped in watered wine, and a few olives from a jar. His father was already sitting in the doorway, looking toward the east.

"Wait," the old man said, without turning round.

They waited. Then, somewhere above the rooftops, a point of light struck. It was the crown. The first sun of the day always found the god's crown before it found anything else in the city: the spikes of bronze rays around his head lit up like a lamp, while the streets below were still grey. The light slid down the face and the curling hair, down the raised arm, the shoulders and the chest. Only when it reached the knees did the sun itself clear the hills across the water in Karia and reach the roofs.

His father watched it every morning. He said it was the one thing in his life he had never got tired of.

"Seventy cubits," the old man said, as he did most mornings. "I watched it go up. Twelve years. I was already grey when they finished it." He tapped his own chest. "He's ours, you know. Not a king's. No king paid a single obol for him. The Besieger left his engines outside our walls, and we sold them and made a god."

Timokles had heard it a thousand times. He said, as he always did, "I know, Father."

Hanging on the wall inside the door was a bronze breastplate, dull brown and never polished. It was the reason his father told the story every morning. When the Besieger came with his towers and his forty thousand men, Timokles's grandfather had died on the walls. The city had voted that the sons of the fallen would be raised at public cost. When they came of age, each would be crowned in the theatre at the festival of Dionysos, in front of everyone, and given a full suit of armour. His father had been one of those boys. The breastplate was what the city had given him.

"The city keeps its promises," his father said. It was his other morning sentence. Then he got up, knees cracking, to go and sit on the council. It was his turn this month. At Rhodes the citizens took turns: a man governed for some months of the year and was an ordinary citizen for the rest.

## Morning: the stamps

The family workshop lay outside the walls, where there was room for the kilns and the clay pits, and where the smoke would not bother anybody who mattered. It made wine jars, the tall, narrow-necked Rhodian kind with two handles, which went out by the shipload to Alexandria and everywhere else ships went.

Timokles's job this morning was the stamps.

Every Rhodian jar carried two of them, one pressed into the top of each handle while the clay was still soft. One stamp named the workshop: his father's name and a little rose, the flower of the island. The other named the year, and Rhodes had no numbers for its years. A year was known by the man who served that year as priest of the Sun. Every contract, every decree and every jar said the same thing: *in the priesthood of So-and-so*, and then the month.

The priest had changed. There was a new man in the office, from an old ship-owning family, the kind of man whose grandfather's name was carved on things. So the old stamp had gone into a basket in the corner with the others, a whole basket of dead years. The new one had come back from the die-cutter the day before. Timokles turned it over in his fingers. It was a little rectangle cut in reverse: the Sun's face, the priest's name, and the month.

"Careful," said Manes, who had come out with him and was already stripped to the waist at the wedging bench. "You press it crooked, the clerk at the harbour says we're cheats."

"Nobody reads the stamps, Manes."

"Somebody reads them," Manes said. "Somebody always reads them. In a hundred years somebody will pick up your crooked handle and say, *This fool couldn't press a stamp straight in the priesthood of whoever it was.*"

Timokles laughed and pressed the next handle straight. He did forty jars before the sun was halfway up the sky. Each one said, in small backwards letters now facing the right way round, which year of the Sun it had been born in. The god's face on the stamp was the same face he could see from the workshop door, standing over the city with one hand raised.

The wine they would hold was not much good. Everyone knew it. Rhodian wine was the wine you drank when the good wine had run out. But the jars were beautiful, and they were honest. The city made sure they held what they said they held, and dated them, and the whole sea trusted them for it.

## Midday: the harbour

He walked the jars down to the harbour on the cart with Manes, back through the gate and along the waterfront.

The great harbour was the loudest place he knew. Grain ships from Egypt lay three deep along the moles, sitting low in the water, their holds full of wheat from the Nile. Half of them would unload here and sell here. Rhodes was the house every ship stopped at between Egypt and the Aegean. The city grew fat on the harbour dues, which it sold every year to a company of contractors, who then stood on the quay with their scales and clerks and took their cut of everything that crossed it. The rest of the ships would take on water and wine and news, and sail on.

The merchant who was buying their jars was a Rhodian who lived half the year in Alexandria. He was in a foul mood.

"Storm off Crete," he said, before Timokles had even said good day. "We had to lighten the ship. Three hundred jars of oil over the side. Not mine!" He held up a finger. "Not mine. But the law says what goes over the side to save the ship is paid for by everyone whose goods were saved. So I pay a share of another man's oil. It's fair. I hate it, but it's fair." He spat into the water. "It's a Rhodian law. Every sailor from here to Carthage knows it. What is given up for all is made good by all."

Timokles thought about the breastplate on the wall at home. His grandfather had been given up for all, and the city had made it good. It was the same law.

He counted the coins twice. They were silver, and on every one was the god's face again, full on, with his rays, and the rose on the other side. *You're everywhere,* Timokles thought, looking up. From the harbour the Colossus stood over the town, enormous and calm. He had lived under it his whole life and still could not quite make his eyes accept the size of it. A man at its feet would not come up to its ankle bone. People said a man could barely reach his arms around its thumb. He had never been up close enough to try. You did not climb on a god.

On the way back he passed the military harbour. There were no stalls and no crowds there, only the long roofed sheds where the warships lay out of the water. There were guards on the walls, and a notice everybody knew by heart. Anyone who went into the dockyards without leave would die. The city did not joke about its ships. Its ships were the reason it had never been swallowed.

His friend Lysistratos was sitting on the harbour wall outside, eating an onion.

"We sail in ten days," Lysistratos said. "Pirate patrol, down toward Karpathos. You're on my bench."

Timokles grinned. He had rowed last season and would row again. The fleet was rowed by citizens, which made Rhodes different from most places. One of the fast Rhodian ships carried a hundred and twenty men at the oars. The whole fleet needed thousands, which was most of the men in the city. So the rich fed the poor. That was the old custom. The great families paid for ships and for grain handouts and for public works, and in return there were always strong men for the benches. Strangers called it charity. Rhodians called it good sense. A hungry rower was no use to anybody.

"My mother says you're to eat properly before we go," Timokles said. "She says you look like an oar."

"Your mother," said Lysistratos with dignity, "is the finest woman in the city."

## Afternoon: the gymnasium

In the heat of the day, when sensible people slept, the young men went up the hill.

The gymnasium stood among the sanctuaries above the town, near the long stadium where the Games of the Sun were run every summer. The racecourse was straight, over two hundred metres long, with stone seats down its sides. Timokles stripped, rubbed himself with oil from the flask he carried, and wrestled with Lysistratos until they were both grey with dust and oil. Then they scraped themselves clean with bronze strigils and lay in the shade of the colonnade like two lizards.

Below them the whole city lay spread out in its grid, straight streets running down to the two harbours, and the sea beyond, dark blue and wrinkled with wind. Across the strait the mountains of Karia were blue with distance. The Colossus stood out of all of it like a man wading out of the roofs.

"When the Games come," Lysistratos said, "you should run."

"I'm too slow."

"You're not too slow. You're too lazy."

The Games of the Sun were the greatest thing that happened all year. There was a procession to the god, a sacrifice, and races for runners, wrestlers and chariots. There were contests for singers and lyre players. Envoys came from the kings, and victors from Olympia and Delphi came to compete, because the Rhodian games were worth winning now. The prize was a wreath of white poplar, whose leaves shone silver in the wind like the god's own light. At the end of it all came the part Timokles loved best. The city dedicated a four-horse chariot to the Sun and threw it into the sea. The Sun drove his chariot round the world every day and went down into the sea every night, so once a year Rhodes gave him a new one, the same way.

"If I won," Timokles said slowly, "they'd put my name on a stone."

"They'd put your name on a stone," Lysistratos agreed. "In the priesthood of Whoever. And every jar your father makes that year, you'll know."

## Evening: the club

In the evening his father's club met for dinner.

Rhodes was full of clubs. Some said there were hundreds of them: burial clubs, clubs for particular gods, clubs for men from the same town overseas, clubs for men who simply liked eating together. Each club had its own treasurer, its own decrees and its own honours, like a tiny city inside the city. His father's club was a mixture. There were citizens like themselves, and men who had lived on the island for thirty years without ever becoming citizens: a Syrian dyer, a man from Alexandria who dealt in papyrus, and a freedman who had once been somebody's slave and was now one of the richest men at the table. Nobody pretended they were all equal outside. Inside, they paid the same dues, drank from the same mixing-bowl and would be buried in the same plot. The club had bought the plot together, each man giving what he promised, and their names were cut on a stone above it.

They ate fish grilled over coals, barley cakes, cheese and figs. They poured a little wine on the floor for the gods, and argued about the price of grain, the new priest, and whether the king in Egypt was a friend or merely a good customer. The papyrus dealer told a story about a ship full of elephants that nobody believed. The freedman made a speech proposing that the club honour its treasurer with a crown for his honesty. The motion passed. Honesty at Rhodes was the kind of thing that got you a crown.

His father spoke once, late, when the lamps were low.

"I sat through a whole morning today," he said, "while the council argued about a harbour wall. A whole morning. Four men made the same speech." The table laughed. "But I'll tell you what I thought, sitting there. I thought: my father died on that wall. He died so that we could sit there and argue about it. Nobody from outside tells us what to do. There's no garrison, no king's man in the harbour. We collect our own dues and waste our own mornings." He lifted his cup. "To wasted mornings."

"To wasted mornings!"

## Night

Timokles walked home the long way, past the god.

He had not meant to. His feet took him there. The streets were dark between the blocks, with here and there a lamp in a doorway and a dog barking. Then the street opened out onto the Sun's sacred ground, and there was the pedestal: white marble, taller than a house. Above it, black against the stars, rose the bronze legs, and then more and more of him, up out of sight into the dark.

He put his hand on the marble. It was still warm from the day. The letters of the dedication ran across it at shoulder height, too dark to read now, but he knew them. Every Rhodian child learned them:

> *To thy very self, O Sun, did the people of Dorian Rhodes raise high to heaven this colossus, then, when having laid to rest the brazen wave of war, they crowned their country with the spoils of their foes.*

*The people.* Not a king. Not a priest. The people had raised him, paid for him and built him, and they would stand under him for ever.

That was what Timokles thought, at nineteen, with his hand on the warm stone. He thought the god would stand for ever. He thought his name might go on a stone someday, and his sons would press stamps in the priesthood of men not yet born, and look up every morning to watch the first light find the crown.

He went home. Manes had left the door on the latch. The breastplate hung dull in the dark. Upstairs, Kleino's loom was quiet at last. Timokles lay down and listened to the city breathing: the dogs, the far-off harbour, the wind coming off the strait. Over all of it, invisible, the god stood with his hand raised against the light, waiting for morning.

He had twenty-eight years left of mornings like that. He did not know it. Nobody did.

### A note on what is true

Timokles, his family, Manes, Lysistratos, the merchant, the club members and the new priest are invented. Their world is sourced as closely as I could manage from this repo's research notes (`research/jamie-01-polis.md`, `jamie-11-work-money.md`, `jamie-04-myth-in-life.md`, `jamie-03-colossus.md`, `16-rhodes-rules-the-waves-*`, `01-*`).

**The date.** The Colossus was finished about 280 BC and fell in the earthquake of about 227/226 BC. A story set about 255 BC falls roughly halfway, which is why the last line gives twenty-eight years.

**The city**
- The city was a planned grid, founded 408/7 BC, with equal blocks of about 47 x 26 m, three houses to a block, and drains and a water supply. Strabo praised its harbours, streets and walls above any other city's.
- The citizens belonged to three tribes (Lindians, Ialysians, Kamirians), and each tribe was divided into demes.

**The siege and the breastplate**
- During Demetrius's siege (305–304 BC) the city voted that the fallen would be buried at public expense and their families supported.
- Their daughters would get public dowries. Their sons would be crowned in the theatre at the Dionysia and given a full suit of armour (Diodorus 20.84).

**The Colossus**
- It was paid for by selling Demetrius's abandoned engines (Pliny).
- The dedication verse is Anthologia Palatina 6.171.
- The crown of rays matches Rhodian coins, which carry Helios's facing head on one side and a rose on the other.
- The raised hand shading the eyes is a plausible guess, based on a Rhodian relief of Helios.
- The god's thumb that "a man could barely reach his arms around" is Pliny's detail. Pliny saw the statue lying on the ground after it fell, so in the story it is only a rumour Timokles has heard.
- Nobody knows exactly where the statue stood, so the story says only "the Sun's sacred ground" and does not name a hill.

**Government**
- Citizens took turns serving on the council (Cicero, *De Republica* 3.48).
- Strabo says the rich fed the poor "for the manning of the fleets".
- It was death to enter the naval dockyards without leave (Strabo 14.2.5).
- "No garrison" and "our own revenue" echo the peace terms of 304 BC.

**Jars and dates**
- Rhodian wine jars carried two stamps. One named the workshop (often with a rose). The other named the year's priest of Helios, with the month, sometimes with the Sun's head.
- Rhodes named its years after that priest.
- Hundreds of thousands of stamped handles survive, and archaeologists use them to date sites across the Mediterranean. Manes was right.
- Rhodian wine was not thought very good.

**The harbour and the sea**
- Rhodes was a stopping place for Egyptian grain ships.
- The harbour dues were sold to tax contractors (Polybius 30.31).
- The rule that goods thrown overboard to save a ship are paid for by everyone aboard is the "Rhodian law of jettison". It survives in Roman law (Digest 14.2.1), and the story puts it about two centuries before that text was written down.
- Rhodes fought pirates at sea.
- A Rhodian trihemiolia, a fast warship, carried 120 rowers.

**The Games of the Sun**
- The Halieia had a procession, a sacrifice, athletic, musical and chariot contests, and victors from the great Panhellenic games.
- Festus records the four-horse chariot thrown into the sea each year.
- The white-poplar wreath comes from later handbooks rather than an ancient text.
- The stadium on the acropolis is 210 m long and dates from the 3rd century BC.

**Clubs**
- Hellenistic Rhodes had some 200 attested private associations mixing citizens, foreigners and freed slaves.
- Some clubs bought shared grave plots by subscription. One from 185 BC at Ialysos had 36 members.
- Clubs honoured their own officers with crowns.

**Guesses I've made**
- The workshop's site: many Rhodian amphora kilns were in the countryside, so "outside the walls" is a guess.
- The month of the priest's change, and exactly how the Halieia fell in the year, are unsettled in the scholarship, so the story avoids naming months.

# Jamie 03: The Colossus, told properly, and what it says about how Rhodians organised a city

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** research/01 and research/02 already carry the spine of the story (Diodorus on the siege and the Helepolis, Pliny's 300 talents and the thumb, Philo's earth mound, the epigram, Strabo's oracle, Polybius's relief, Theophanes's camels, the four location theories, the 2008 and 2015 rebuild schemes). Do not repeat them at length. This file supplies what Jamie asked for that those files lack: the Colossus as a *social* object. A merchant republic of perhaps a few tens of thousands of people, ten years out of a siege, votes to spend the price of a war fleet on a bronze of its patron god, and to build it for twelve years. Who decided, who paid, who cast it, why a sun god, and how the same god ran the calendar, the wine trade, the games and the foreign policy. The through-line is Pindar's myth of Helios choosing Rhodes: written in 464 BC, carved (the Rhodians said) in gold at Lindos, stamped on the coins from the founding of the city, dated on every wine jar for 260 years, and then cast at thirty-three metres. The statue was the myth made load-bearing. Everything below is sourced; contested points are flagged.

---

## Tell at stop 1 (cruise berth) and stop 5 (Marine Gate): the siege as a social contract

research/01 gives Diodorus's numbers. What matters for Jamie is the *shape* of the mobilisation, because it is the same society that will later vote the statue.

- Diodorus 20.84: about 6,000 citizens and up to 1,000 metics and foreigners took arms; slaves who fought well were promised freedom; the state undertook to bury the fallen at public expense, support their parents and children, and give "dowries at the public cost" to their daughters. That last clause is the tell: the city guarantees the *marriage market* of its dead. A Rhodian household's future was underwritten by the polis in exchange for the household's men.
- The peace of 304 BC (Diod. 20.99.3) made Rhodes "autonomous and ungarrisoned", keeping "its own revenue", with 100 citizen hostages handed to Demetrius. Sovereignty, in the Hellenistic sense, was precisely that bundle: no foreign garrison, your own taxes, your own foreign policy except against one named friend.
- Immediately afterwards the assembly institutionalised gratitude. They asked the oracle of Ammon whether to honour Ptolemy as a god and built the Ptolemaeum, a square precinct with stoas a stade long (Diod. 20.100.3-4). Pausanias 1.8.6 (Jones tr.): "the son of Lagus is called Soter, a name given him by the Rhodians." The cult title "Saviour" that the Ptolemaic dynasty used for three centuries was, on Pausanias's account, a Rhodian decree. Small city, big naming rights.

So before a single talent went to Chares, the demos had already spent on two other cult objects: the widows' dowries and Ptolemy's temple. The Colossus is the third instalment of the same post-war settlement.

## Tell at stop 17 (Temple of Aphrodite) and stop 9 (Palace forecourt): why a sun god

### The myth, in Pindar's words

Olympian 7 was written for Diagoras of Ialysos, Olympic boxing champion of 464 BC. According to a scholiast citing Gorgon, the Rhodian historian, "a copy of this ode, in letters of gold, was preserved in the temple of Athena at Lindus" (Wikipedia, Olympian 7, citing the scholia; whether on the wall or on a gilded scroll is unknown). Three scenes from it are the civic scripture. Ernest Myers's translation (Wikisource):

1. **The fireless sacrifice and the gold.** The sons of Helios were told to be first to build Athena an altar when she was born; they hurried up the acropolis and forgot fire. Zeus rewarded the intention anyway: "For them Zeus brought a yellow cloud into the sky and rained much gold upon the land; and Glaukopis herself gave them to excel the dwellers upon earth in every art of handicraft." Diodorus 5.56-57 rationalises this: the Heliadae sacrificed without fire, and the Rhodian rite to Athena stayed fireless while the Athenians (Cecrops) later used fire. A Rhodian in 290 BC believed his ancestors had been given craft *as a divine gift*, and that gift now paid the sculptors. Diodorus 5.55 adds the older layer: the Telchines, "the first to fashion statues of gods", wizards who could "summon clouds and rain and hail". A city whose founding myth is *we were the first statue-makers* will eventually build the biggest statue.

2. **Helios absent at the division of the earth.** "And for that Helios was otherwhere, none drew a lot for him ... he saw a certain land waxing from its root in earth ... he bade her of the golden fillet, Lachesis, to stretch her hands on high ... promise him that the isle ... should be thenceforth a title of himself alone." Then: "There sprang up from the watery main an island, and the father who begetteth the keen rays of day hath the dominion thereof." Note what this does politically. Rhodes is not conquered, not colonised, not allotted by Zeus: it rises from the sea already *belonging* to one god, by a sworn oath of Fate. That is a sovereignty charter. The epigram on the Colossus base repeats it: "for to those who spring from the race of Heracles dominion is a heritage both on land and sea" (Anth. Pal. 6.171, Paton tr.).

3. **The seven sons and the three cities.** "There sometime having lain with Rhodos he begat seven sons ... One begat Kameiros, and Ialysos his eldest, and Lindos: and they held each apart their shares of cities, making threefold division of their father's land." Diodorus 5.57 names the intermediate father as Kerkaphos and adds that the Heliadae "surpassed [others] in learning and especially in astrology" and "established the division of the day into hours". The sun's children invent the clock. Homer already knew the three cities and the money: Iliad 2.653-670 ends the Tlepolemus entry with Zeus pouring "wondrous wealth" on the Rhodians; Philo of Byzantium, describing the Colossus 800 years later, borrows the exact phrase, asking whether Zeus poured "heavenly wealth" on Rhodes so that they might spend it honouring Helios (Dickie 1996, p. 255, citing Philo p. 30 Hercher).

### The myth becomes the constitution (408/7 BC)

In 408/7 BC Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros merged into one state and built the new city at the north tip, on a Hippodamian grid (Strabo 14.2.5: "by the same architect, as they say, who founded the Peiraeus"). The three old cities became the three *phylai* of the new state, "under the names Lindia, Kamiris and Ialysia" (Thomsen 2020, ch. 4), and competed against each other as tribes at state festivals in the *asty* (Thomsen's argument as summarised; the exact wording was not reachable this session). Pindar's threefold division of the father's land was now the electoral map. Under the tribes sat demes and, older still, *ktoinai*, "an older form of territorial organization that was superceded but not replaced by the deme system".

The federal god had to be one none of the three cities owned. Lindos had Athena Lindia, Ialysos and Kameiros their own cults. Helios belonged to all and to none: the synoecism marked "a pivotal religious shift" with "Helios's rise as a unifying deity", a "supra-demotic deity" whose cult ran across the countryside and the Peraia on the Anatolian mainland (Zachhuber 2024, via BMCR 2025). From the synoecism onward the coins carry the facing head of Helios on one side and the rose (*rhodon*, the nymph Rhodos) on the other (the exact start date of the type, 408/7 or a few years later, I could not pin to a numismatic source this session; say "from the founding of the city"). Every transaction restated the marriage in Pindar's poem.

### The priest of Helios dates the year

This is the detail Jamie will like best. The eponymous magistrate of Rhodes, the man whose name labelled the year in every decree, was the priest of Halios (Doric for Helios). Because Rhodian wine amphorae were stamped "in the priesthood of X, month Y", the sequence survives in clay. The *Lexicon of Eponym Dies on Rhodian Amphora Stamps* records 258-259 priests, "to these should be added eight further eponyms", making 266, who "must have officiated between ca. 303/300 B.C.E. and 36/33 B.C.E."; the corpus runs to 5,772 distinct dies and "over 140,000 stamped amphora handles" in Alexandria alone (AJA review, Oct 2019). In other words the year on a Rhodian wine jar dug up in Jerusalem or Alexandria reads "the year So-and-so was priest of the Sun". The Rhodian calendar was a religious calendar, exported by the shipload. When the Colossus was voted (c. 292 BC), the eponym list had begun about a decade earlier: the statue and the dating system are the same generation's idea of Helios.

Who held the priesthood? A one-year office in rotation, drawn from the families who also captained the fleet. Thomsen argues a "magisterial elite" dominated by capturing magistracies; one man is attested in twenty-three associations. Vincent Gabrielsen's earlier label for the same class is "the naval aristocracy of Hellenistic Rhodes". Government was a council (*boula*) and assembly of "the whole people" (*ho sympas damos*), with five *prytaneis* holding executive office (standard handbooks give six-month terms; I could not reach a primary citation this session, so treat the six months as unverified). Strabo is blunt: "The Rhodians are concerned for the people in general, although their rule is not democratic."

## Tell at stop 19 (deer columns) and stop 20 (Fort St Nicholas), reprise at stop 21 (the drink): the Halieia, or a chariot driven into the sea

The Colossus was a votive in a cult that had a festival, and the festival had the one ritual every guide remembers and few source.

- **The source.** Festus, *De verborum significatu* s.v. *October equus* (p. 190 Lindsay), explaining why Romans sacrifice a horse in October, lists foreign parallels: *"Et Rhodi, qui quotannis quadrigas soli consecratas in mare iaciunt, quod is tali curriculo fertur circumvehi mundum"* ("And the Rhodians, who every year throw into the sea four-horse chariots consecrated to the Sun, because he is said to be carried round the world in such a chariot", Crawford tr., UCLA Digital Roman Forum). That is the whole of it. Festus does not say the horses were white, alive, or driven in by a charioteer; those are modern embroideries (Wikipedia's "four white horses" is footnoted only to 19th-century handbooks, Smith, Torr, Seyffert and Gardner-Jevons; Festus, the one ancient text, gives no colour). What Festus gives is the logic: the sun sinks into the sea every evening, so once a year the city gives the god a chariot the same way.
- **When.** A scholiast on Pindar Ol. 7.147c, probably drawing on Istros's lost *On the Games of Helios*, says the Halieia "finished on the 24th day of the month of Gorpiaios, six days after the Nemea" (Iversen, *The Antikythera Mechanism, Rhodes, and Epeiros*, p. 23 n. 11); that puts them in high summer, roughly July. Inscriptions distinguish "the Great and the Small Halieia" (IK RhodPer 555, mid-2nd c. BC); the Great Halieia were pentaeteric (every four years by our count), attested in IG XII,1 730 at four-year intervals, with one apparent cancellation Iversen puts at the Mithridatic War of 85 BC (Iversen p. 24 n. 12).
- **The Antikythera Mechanism.** The games dial of the Antikythera Mechanism lists Isthmia, Olympia, Nemea, Pythia, the Naa of Dodona and, in the year-4 slot, ΑΛΙΕΙΑ: Iversen's 2017 reading. "35 of the 37 epigraphical attestations of the spelling Ἁλίεια are found on inscriptions from the island of Rhodes or the Rhodian Peraia" (p. 25), which is his strongest evidence the device was made on Rhodes. A bronze computer built on the Sun's island keeps the Sun's games on its dial. Say this at Mandraki; it is the best single line about Rhodian time-keeping.
- **What happened at the festival.** Smith's *Dictionary* (s.v. Halia): annual gymnastic and musical contests, "a grand procession", white wreaths for victors (later sources say white poplar, Helios's tree; medium confidence). A 2nd-century AD novel set a scene there: in Xenophon of Ephesus's *Ephesiaca* the lovers, landing at Rhodes, dedicate a golden panoply to Helios with an inscription naming them "citizens of sacred Ephesus", and in Book 5 the heroine, back on Rhodes "during the festival of the sun", dedicates a lock of hair and is recognised by it (Smith's cites *Ephes.* 5.11; Wikipedia synopsis). Fiction, but fiction that expected readers to know that at Rhodes you went to Helios's temple, hung up your offering, and were seen.
- **The god's own chariot in bronze.** Lysippus, Chares's teacher, made a Helios in a four-horse chariot for Rhodes: Pliny 34.63 praises him "most of all for his Chariot with the Sun belonging to Rhodes". It outlived the Colossus by centuries. When Cassius plundered the city in 42 BC "he appropriated their ships, money, and public and sacred treasures, with the exception of the chariot of the Sun" (Cassius Dio 47.33.4). Even a Roman warlord stripping the treasuries left Helios his quadriga. Dio Chrysostom, a century later, tells the Rhodians that to erase a name from a statue is as bad as if "any person should remove a part of the Chariot" (Or. 31.86, Loeb, whose note says this "probably refers to" Lysippus's group).
- **Continuity into Christian custom.** I looked for a documented survival of the sea-chariot or the Halieia into Byzantine or modern Rhodian practice and found none in reachable sources. Do not claim one. The honest continuity is structural: a midsummer festival, a procession to the shore, an offering to the water. Say "the pattern survives; the god's name did not" and leave it there.

## Tell at stop 9 (Palace, Badoud's Helios sanctuary) and stop 8 (St John's): commissioning a wonder

### The money, in human units

Pliny (NH 34.41): twelve years, 300 talents, "money realized from the engines of war belonging to King Demetrius which he had abandoned". A talent is 6,000 drachmas, so 1,800,000 drachmas. The nearest datable wage comparator is the Erechtheion accounts at Athens of 408/7 BC (IG I3 476, the year Rhodes was founded): "citizens, metics and slaves ... were paid at the same rate for the same job", the standard rate being 1 drachma a day, and the architect drew the same (Attic Inscriptions Online). At that rate 300 talents is 1.8 million man-days, roughly 5,000 man-years, or a 400-strong workforce for the twelve years Pliny gives. That is arithmetic, not evidence, but it tells you the sum is a *labour* figure, not a bronze figure. Philo's "500 talents of bronze and 300 of iron" (13 t and 8 t) are far below what a 33 m statue needs; Dickie (1996) concludes Philo's construction account "is almost certainly worthless" as technical evidence, while Badoud (2024) defends casting in courses. Genuinely open.

For the scale of the *second* statue budget: after the earthquake Ptolemy III offered "three thousand talents for the repair of the Colossus; a hundred master builders with three hundred and fifty workmen, and fourteen talents yearly to pay their wages" (Polybius 5.89, Shuckburgh tr.). Fourteen talents is 84,000 drachmas a year for 450 men: about half a drachma a day each, which is a plausible skilled-labour wage for the 220s. So we have a royal quote for the workforce of a colossus: 450 men, one in five a master. Chares's original crew was probably of that order.

### Who the workers were

No Rhodian building account survives for the Colossus, so reason from analogy and from Philo's imagination:

- Philo imagines the audience's question: "what sort of tongs, what size of anvil-bases, what strength of assistants" could have forged such masses (Dickie p. 255, quoting Philo p. 30 Hercher). The iron-and-stone interior he calls a hidden labour "greater than the visible". The workforce he pictures is smiths and hauliers, not sculptors.
- The Erechtheion model, the only detailed Greek building payroll: citizens registered by deme, metics "living in" a named district, slaves by their owner's name, all on the same day-rate, the enslaved Somenes fluting a column beside his owner Ameiniades, who is himself a metic ("Ameiniades living in Koile"), at the same 18 or 20 drachmas per column as the citizens on the next capital. Rhodes in the 290s had the same three statuses (Diodorus counts them separately for the siege) and a big metal industry; Pliny says the city held "a hundred other colossal statues" and five colossal gods by Bryaxis (34.42), and "there are still 3000 statues at Rhodes" in his day (34.36). Bronze-casting on that scale is a standing industry with pits, furnaces and slave gangs, not a one-off.
- Chares himself: from Lindos, pupil of Lysippus, the court sculptor who Pliny says made 1,500 works and put a gold coin aside for each (34.37), and whose 40-cubit Zeus at Tarentum "can be moved by the hand" yet stood in storms (34.40). A Lindian trained in the greatest bronze workshop of the age, working for the state whose *phyle* Lindia he belonged to. The Sextus Empiricus anecdote (*Against the Logicians* 1.107-108): the Rhodians asked Chares his price, then asked what a statue twice the size would cost; he asked double, spent the whole sum on the preliminaries, and killed himself, and only afterwards did the craftsmen realise he should have asked eight times as much. It is a rhetorician's example about cubic scaling, not history (Sextus does not name Laches; the tradition that Laches of Lindos finished the work is a separate and weaker one); but it preserves a real anxiety about how you *price* a wonder.

### What kind of object they thought they were making

Two things the demos dedicated: a votive and a claim. The epigram says "the people of Dorian Rhodes" raised the statue "when having laid to rest the brazen wave of war"; it is the people, not a king, not a priest, and the ethnic is Dorian, the Heraclid line of Tlepolemus. Badoud goes further: the statue "fixed Helios at the center of the new territories to ensure that they would never escape Rhodian domination", and the word *kolossos* originally meant "a magical statue, not defined by its size, that could encase a god or mortal" (Archaeology Magazine, 2025). That reading rests on the Cyrene inscriptions, where wax *kolossoi* are burned as the settlers swear their oath, and on Vernant's idea of the *kolossos* as a ritual double. Be careful: Dickie (1996) showed that *kolossos* already meant a very large statue in Hellenistic prose (Polybius uses it for a 10-cubit Attalus at Sicyon and a 30-cubit Roman People at Rhodes) and that "there is no reason to think" the Rhodian statue changed the word's meaning. Present both: to an anthropologist the interesting fact is that the *same* word covered a wax doll melted in an oath and a bronze god the height of a ship's mast.

Where it stood matters for this reading. Badoud's site is the small hill under the Palace: "an inscription mentioning a sacred area of Helios", "dedications made by his priests" and "a marble head of the god have been found there", all now under the 14th-century fortress. Standing in the forecourt at stop 9, Jamie is on the leading candidate for the sanctuary. Note the disagreement: Zachhuber (2024), following Hoepfner, locates Helios's sanctuary on the acropolis (Monte Smith), which would make Vedder's site the temple and leave the Palace hill for something else. Nobody has dug either.

## Tell at stop 6 (Hospital / Archaeological Museum): the thumb, the head and the statue economy

### Pliny's thumb

"Few people can make their arms meet round the thumb of the figure, and the fingers are larger than most statues" (NH 34.41). Three things are packed in there. First, a measurement by the body: a Roman visitor measures the god with his own embrace, the way farmers measure a tree. A thumb you cannot hug is about 1.6-1.8 m in circumference, which for a 33 m figure is roughly right. Second, an admission: people were *doing* this, for three centuries, to a fallen idol nobody was allowed to raise. The ruin was a tourist body. Third, the interior: "where the limbs have been broken off enormous cavities yawn, while inside are seen great masses of rock". The god was hollow and ballasted with stone; the visitor could look into him. For a Rhodian this was theology made visible: Helios was the sun, and this was the shell they had made for him.

### The head in the museum

The Archaeological Museum's colossal marble head of Helios, second half of the 2nd century BC, "representative work of Rhodian baroque" (Ephorate of Antiquities of the Dodecanese), sits in the Hellenistic sculpture rooms on the upper floor, south wing. It is a century younger than the Colossus and from another statue; its wind-tossed hair and drilled holes for metal rays show the type. Whether it is the same "marble head of the god" Badoud cites from the Palace hill I could not confirm; the museum and Ephorate pages give no findspot. Say "a head of Helios found in the town" and no more.

### Three thousand statues and how a city recycled them

Rhodes in the Roman period was a city of statues: Pliny's 3,000. Dio Chrysostom's *Rhodian Oration* (Or. 31, c. AD 70-100) is the best surviving description of how a Greek city *administered* honour. The city had "some 3000 statues in its temples and streets" (the Loeb editors' summary; Dio himself gives the figure at 31.146), yet Romans kept asking for the honour of one, so the Rhodians developed a protocol: "The chief magistrate merely points to whichever statue meets his eyes, then, after removing the former inscription and engraving another name" (31.10). Dio thinks this an outrage against memory; a modern reading is that it was a considered heritage policy with a register. Either way the city treated its bronze population as a renewable civic resource. He then holds up a mirror to Rhodian manners (31.162-163): "your gait, the way you trim your hair, that no one struts pompously through your city's streets, but that even foreigners sojourning here are forced by your conventional manners to walk sedately ... the width of the purple stripe ... your remaining silent as you watch the games, your applauding by making a clucking sound with your lips ... more than by your harbours, your fortifications, your shipyards are you honoured by that strain in your customs which is antique and Hellenic, so that when anybody comes among you he recognizes instantly on disembarking, even if he happens to be of barbarian race, that he has not come to some city of Syria or of Cilicia." This is an eyewitness to Rhodian everyday life: dress code, hairstyle, a rule against swagger, quiet in the theatre, a clicking applause. Use it at the museum door.

## Tell at stop 11 or 12 (walls walk) and back at stop 9: the earthquake and the economy of gifts

Polybius 5.88-89 (Shuckburgh tr.) is worth quoting for its cynicism about disaster diplomacy. The earthquake "overthrew the great Colossus and the larger part of the walls and dockyards. But the adroit policy of the Rhodians converted this misfortune into an opportunity ... They enhanced its magnitude and importance by the prominence which they gave it, and the serious tone in which they spoke of it, as well by the mouth of their ambassadors as in the intercourse of private life; and they created thus such an effect upon other states, and especially upon the feelings of the kings, that they were not only overwhelmed with presents, but made the donors feel actually obliged for their acceptance of them." Hiero and Gelo of Syracuse gave 75 talents "towards the expenses of the gymnasium", cauldrons "for religious purposes", "ten more to attract new citizens", customs immunity for Rhodian merchants, fifty catapults, and then "erected statues in the Deigma or Mart of Rhodes, representing the community of Rhodes crowned by that of Syracuse". Ptolemy: 300 talents of silver, a million measures of corn, timber for ten quinqueremes and ten triremes, "a thousand talents of bronze coinage", tow and sailcloth, the 3,000 talents and 450 men for the Colossus, "twelve thousand medimni of corn for their public games and sacrifices, and twenty thousand medimni for victualling ten triremes". Antigonus, Chryseis, Seleucus follow. Read the list as a shopping list of what a Hellenistic state *was*: walls, docks, warships, a gymnasium, games, sacrifices, a colossus, and new citizens to be attracted with cash. The Colossus line is ten times its original cost, the largest single item, and the one thing the Rhodians did not spend the money on. Strabo: "In accordance with a certain oracle, the people did not raise it again." Later tradition names Delphi (Wikipedia; no ancient text I can reach names the shrine). A demos that had voted the statue voted not to rebuild it, and pocketed the difference, with a god's permission. That is not piety versus finance; it is piety *as* finance.

Strabo, on the same page, explains how such a city kept its poor: "the people are supplied with provisions and the needy are supported by the well-to-do, by a certain ancestral custom; and there were certain liturgies that supply provisions, so that at the same time the poor man receives his sustenance and the city does not run short of useful men, and in particular for the manning of the fleets" (14.2.5). Welfare as naval recruitment. He adds that the dockyards were secret and that spying on them carried death. Between Polybius and Strabo you have the Rhodian state's whole logic: the rich feed the poor so the poor can row; the city cultivates kings so the kings will rebuild the docks; the god gets the statue only when the numbers work.

The 200-odd private associations (*koina*) filled the gaps the state left: burial plots, funeral crowns, honours for members, a home for foreigners; "one foreigners' association boasted games and tribes; another acted as a thirteenth deme of Lindos"; Thomsen's line is that "associations are not the harbingers of civic decay anymore. They are where politics is actually happening" (BMCR 2021). Zachhuber calls them "extensions of public cult, offering inclusive spaces for foreigners and marginal groups" (BMCR 2025). A sailor from Syria in 200 BC could not vote in the assembly, but he could belong to a club that buried him, crowned him, and worshipped Helios on his behalf.

## Tell at stop 22 (Naillac stump, walk back) with a glance back at stops 13-14: the afterlife and the camels

research/02 has the texts. Two points to add for Jamie.

1. The story as told by Theophanes (c. 810) is a *chain of custody*: Arab commander takes the island, the bronze is sold to "a Jewish merchant from Edessa", 900 camels carry it away. Conrad (1996) traced every version to one Syriac source and read it as a topos; Badoud calls it a fabrication modelled on Daniel's shattered statue. It belongs to a family of medieval tales in which a Jewish middleman disposes of the pagan past; tell it as such, in the Jewish quarter or on the way out, and do not tell it as an event. The fallen statue was in any case probably gone before Islam: "ruins visible into the 1st century AD but nothing by the 14th century" (Badoud via Archaeology Magazine).
2. What the camel number is doing. 900 camels at roughly 200 kg is 180 tonnes of bronze, about twelve times Philo's 500 talents. The tale is not accounting; it is the last of the "hug the thumb" gestures, a measurement of the god by the largest unit the teller had, a caravan.

The deer columns at Mandraki (1939, Italian) and the harbour-straddling picture (Heemskerck, 1572) are the modern layers of the same habit: each age measures the Colossus with its own instruments and plants it where its own harbour is.

## Continuities and contrasts

What an ancient Rhodian, a Hospitaller, an Ottoman-era householder and a resident today would recognise in each other's lives, on this one subject.

- **A calendar owned by a cult.** The Hellenistic year was "the priesthood of X"; the Knights dated by the regnal year of a Grand Master and the feasts of the Church, and their conventual church of St John stood beside, or on, the Sun's hill (stop 8); Ottoman Rhodes dated by the Hijra and lived by the muezzin's five calls from the Suleymaniye (stop 16); the Orthodox town today runs on the paschal calendar and name-days. All four would understand a year named after a religious office-holder. Only the modern tourist calendar (cruise timetables, 09:00 to 18:00) is secular.
- **A midsummer procession to the sea.** The Halieia's chariot went into the water in July; the Knights processed relics; the modern town's summer is the feasts of the Panagia in August and Epiphany's cross thrown into the harbour in January (the priest throws it, boys dive for it: the only ritual today in which the city gives something to the sea and takes it back). No one should say the one descends from the other; anyone can see they rhyme.
- **Statues as a public register.** Rhodians re-inscribed bronze; the Knights carved their coats of arms into every gate and inn (stop 7), and the Ottomans plastered over them; the Italians in 1939 put a stag and doe where they wanted the Colossus to have been. Each regime edited the last regime's inscriptions. Dio would recognise the practice; he would only argue about who deserved the plinth.
- **Welfare through the fleet.** Strabo's rich-feed-the-poor-so-they-can-row is the Hospital's rule in another key: the Order fed and nursed the sick in the very building of stop 6 as its founding charitable act, funded by European estates, while its galleys manned the same harbour. Ottoman Rhodes had the waqf endowments feeding the poor from the Hafiz Ahmed Agha library and the mosques. The modern equivalent is the Greek state and the EU's regional fund rebuilding the Mills mole. The institution changes; the deal (feed people, hold the harbour) does not.
- **Associations.** Thomsen's 200 *koina*; the Knights' seven *langues* organised by nation with their own inns and burial rights; Ottoman guilds and the Jewish *kehilla* around Kahal Shalom (stop 13); today's cultural clubs, fishing co-ops and the Dodecanese hoteliers' chamber that fronted the 2015 rebuild. Every Rhodes has had a second, voluntary map of belonging laid over the official one.
- **Sovereignty as a bundle of exemptions.** "Autonomous and ungarrisoned, keeping its own revenue" (304 BC); the Order's sovereignty as a papal fief with its own mint and fleet; Ottoman Rhodes as a sanjak with capitulations for foreign merchants; Italian Dodecanese as a "possession"; Greek since 1947-48. Each Rhodian would ask the same three questions of the other: who garrisons you, who taxes you, who do you have to be friends with.
- **Measuring the god.** The thumb, the camels, the deer, the 150 m tripod of 2015: every age converts the Colossus into its own biggest unit. Jamie's birthday drink at stop 21 sits within sight of two Italian columns 62 m apart that were placed to fit a 1572 engraving of a 3rd-century BC statue that no ancient writer located. That is the anthropology of the Colossus in one glance.

## What is uncertain or contested (say so if used)

- **Construction method.** Philo's earth-mound and casting in courses: rejected by Vedder 2017 and Dickie 1996 ("almost certainly worthless"), defended by Badoud 2024. The bronze tonnage (Philo's 500 talents) is too low for a 33 m cast figure; Maryon's hammered-sheet alternative is possible. Nobody knows.
- **Site.** Palace hill (Badoud 2024) versus acropolis (Vedder 2017; Zachhuber 2024 for the sanctuary) versus St Nicholas mole (Hoepfner 2003, popular). No excavation has tested any of them.
- **The oracle.** Strabo says "a certain oracle"; Delphi is later tradition.
- **Date of the earthquake.** 228-224 BC in the sources; 227/226 conventional; Shuckburgh's margin says 224.
- **The Halieia's white-poplar crown and white horses.** Smith's says "white wreaths"; Festus says nothing about horse colour. Treat both as embroidery.
- **Six-month prytaneis.** Standard handbook detail; primary citation not reached this session.
- **The museum Helios head's findspot.** Unpublished in the pages I could reach; do not say it came from the Palace hill.
- **Chares's suicide.** Sextus Empiricus, a rhetorical example; not history. Sextus does not mention Laches; do not cite him for the Laches tradition.
- **900 camels.** Fabrication or topos; the figures vary (900, 980, "three thousand loads").
- **Any survival of the Halieia into Christian practice.** None documented; do not claim it.

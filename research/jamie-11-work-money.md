## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes has never fed itself. From 408 BC to this morning the town has lived by taking a cut of other people's movement: grain moving from Egypt to Greece, wine moving from Rhodian hillsides to Alexandria, silver moving from French commanderies to a convent, taxes moving from island villages to a Kapudan Pasha's fleet, sponges moving from the Libyan seabed to London, and now people moving off an aircraft or a ship for a day. The instruments of that living are the things Jamie can actually see on the walk: a stamped amphora handle in the museum that names the priest of the Sun and the month the wine was jarred (the most complete administrative dating system the ancient world produced, in a jar); a coin with a magistrate's name under a rose; a court building with an outside stair where merchants were judged by the Knights' law; a library and a clock paid for by one family's endowment; a hotel built by a colonial governor to invent tourism, where an armistice was later signed; and the market street, Sokratous, which has sold to sailors, pilgrims, Ottoman townsmen and cruise passengers for at least six centuries in the same 300 metres. The through-line for Jamie is that every regime invented a different way to convert the harbour into a livelihood for people who did not own ships: liturgies, clubs, rowing-service, endowments, guilds, remittances, seasonal stamps. Uncertainties are marked; nothing here is narration.

## 1. The stamped jar: a state that dated its exports (Tell at stop 6, the Archaeological Museum; anticipate at stop 1 on the quay)

**The system.** A Rhodian wine amphora of the third to first century BC carries two stamps, one on each handle. One names the eponymous magistrate of the year, the priest of Helios, with the name of the month; the other names the *fabricant*, the workshop owner; the commonest devices are the rose and the radiate head of Helios. (This is the standard description in the literature, Grace and Finkielsztejn; it is not quoted from the Amphoralex "Stamped Handles" page, which an earlier draft cited for wording that is not on it. That page confirms only that the Alexandria collection is filed as "eponym stamps followed by fabricant stamps" with a subgroup of "stamps bearing only the month-names"; it does not mention the rose or Helios.) The year was named after the priest of the Sun in every Rhodian document (jamie-04 has the priesthood, the twelve months and the Lindian Chronicle date). So a jar of Rhodian wine is a dated, signed export licence in clay: "in the priesthood of X, month Y, from the workshop of Z." Thasos, Knidos and Sinope also stamped their jars, but none has left so large or so informative a corpus.

**The numbers, which are the point.** The Centre d'Études Alexandrines' online corpus of Rhodian stamp matrices lists "259 éponymes, 8046 matrices en ligne" and "449 fabricants, 4683 matrices en ligne", with the chronology running from period Ia, "c. 304-c. 271 BC", to period VIIb, "c. 40 BC-August[us]", that is from c. 304 BC into the Augustan period, and "based on Finkielsztejn 2001, p. 196, table 22.1" (Amphoralex corpus query page; an earlier draft misread "August" as a month rather than the emperor). The recent *Lexicon of Eponym Dies on Rhodian Amphora Stamps* documents 5,771 dies for the eponyms of c. 270-108 BC, identified from "well over 100,000" stamps (Finkielsztejn's review, Topoi 22.2, 2018). The Graeco-Roman Museum in Alexandria alone holds "the world's largest collection of stamped amphora handles, more than 160,000 examples", of which 66,000 were bought one by one from building-site labourers, "for the modest price of a piastre", by the collector Lucas Benaki "in the years that followed the Second World War" and given to the museum (Amphoralex presentation page). The Alexandria figure covers all origins, but Rhodian handles are "the largest group of the collection", because "Rhodes' commercial policy was oriented towards Egypt and also the Eastern Mediterranean consumption centres rather than to the northern markets like Delos and Athens, which were held by Knidos" (Amphoralex "Stamped Handles" page). The "about 100,000 Rhodian handles" figure in the brief is therefore conservative; the honest formulation is "well over a hundred thousand stamps have been read, and the eponym dies alone number 5,771."

**Why archaeologists love Rhodes.** Because the eponyms are annual and the months are named, a Rhodian handle can date a destruction layer at Athens, Carthage, Jerusalem or the Crimea to within a year or two, once the sequence of priests is fixed. Virginia Grace built that sequence from the Athenian Agora in the mid-twentieth century; Gérald Finkielsztejn revised it in 2001 (the "Finkielsztejn chronology" the Lexicon follows, Topoi review). The French Wikipedia summary is fair: the stamps of "Thasos, Sinope, Rhodes, Cos, Cnide, Héraclée du Pont" are the well-studied ones that "have allowed relatively precise chronologies". Rhodian wine itself was not thought a great wine; its jars are famous because the state bureaucratised them. The Roman-period successor container, "Camulodunum 184, an amphora used for the transportation of Rhodian wine all over the empire", shows the trade outliving the republic (Wikipedia, Amphora). The Black Sea as a major market for Rhodian wine is the standard statement in the literature but I could not reach a primary or corpus page for it in this session; say "Alexandria above all, and the Black Sea colonies" and flag it.

**What Jamie can see.** The Archaeological Museum (the Knights' Hospital) displays stamped handles and whole amphorae from the town's cemeteries and the harbour; the free underwater-finds exhibition on Ippoton street shows jars lifted from the port but was closed from 6 May 2026 (research/16). On the museum's coins, the parallel is exact: "Below the rose is name of the magistrate, and a text above the rose refers to Rhodes" (Wikipedia, Rhodian coinage). Jar and coin both carry the name of the man responsible for the year. Ask her which modern object does the same; the answer is a passport stamp or a bottle's lot number.

**Uncertain.** Why Rhodes stamped: fiscal control, workshop accountability, or capacity certification are all argued; no ancient text explains it. The earliest stamps (c. 304 BC in the corpus) coincide with the aftermath of Demetrius's siege and the Ptolemaic alliance; that is a coincidence worth stating as a question, not a cause.

## 2. Grain, loans and the harbour toll (Tell at stops 1, 5 and 4)

**Egypt's grain paid for Rhodes.** Diodorus's one-line balance sheet for 305 BC: "most of their revenues were due to the merchants who sailed to Egypt, and that in general the city drew its food supply from that kingdom" (Diodorus 20.81). Rhodes was not the grain's source; it was the clearing-house between the Nile and the Aegean. The clearest single document of how that worked is an Athenian lawsuit, Demosthenes 56 *Against Dionysodorus* (c. 323 BC). The borrower, Dionysodorus, "borrowed from us three thousand drachmae upon his ship on the condition that it should sail back to Athens" (56.3); the borrowers, "underlings and confederates of Cleomenes, the former ruler of Egypt" who made money "by buying up grain for resale and fixing its price" (56.7), ran a network in which "some of them would despatch the stuff from Egypt, others would sail in charge of the shipments, while still others would remain here in Athens and dispose of the consignments", writing letters so that "if grain were dear in your market, they might bring it here, and if the price should fall, they might put in to some other port" (56.8). When Sicilian grain hit Athens and the price fell, the partner in Athens "sent a man to Rhodes to inform his partner Parmeniscus of the state of things here, well knowing that his ship would be forced to touch at Rhodes" (56.9), and Parmeniscus "discharged his cargo of grain at Rhodes and sold it there in defiance of the agreement" (56.10). Every ship from Egypt to Greece "would be forced to touch at Rhodes": that is the geography Jamie is standing on. The same speech gives the instrument that financed it, the maritime loan (*nautikon daneion*): capital lent on ship or cargo, repaid with high interest if the ship arrived, forfeited if it sank. Rhodian bankers are not named in it; the Athenian lenders are. For Rhodian banking itself I found no primary text this session (Bogaert's *Banques et banquiers* catalogues the epigraphic evidence; not read), so keep to "Rhodes was where the deals cleared", not "Rhodes was a banking centre".

**Rhodes as grain broker for others.** In 170 BC, during civil strife, Rhodian envoys "Hagesilochus, Nicagoras, and Nicander" went to Rome, and the senate "gave them leave to export a hundred thousand medimni of corn from Sicily" (Polybius 28.2): Rhodes buying Sicilian grain wholesale with Roman permission. About a decade later, c. 160 BC (Polybius places the notice between the death of Aemilius Paullus in 160 and Demetrius I's seizure of Antioch), king Eumenes II of Pergamon gave the Rhodians "280,000 medimni of corn for the purpose of lending out the proceeds and applying the interest to payment of the salaries of the tutors and teachers of their sons" (Polybius 31.31). Read that twice: grain converted to cash, cash lent at interest, interest paying the schoolmasters. A public education endowment funded by a foreign king's wheat is the most Rhodian financial instrument on record, and a direct ancestor of the vakf at stop 16.

**The toll.** The figure that measures the whole economy is in the Rhodian ambassador Astymedes's plea to the Senate after 167 BC: "While the harbour-dues in former times were farmed for a million drachmae, they now fetch only a hundred and fifty thousand", because Rome had "made Delos a free port"; Rhodes had also lost Caunus and Stratonicea, "an annual revenue of a hundred and twenty talents" (Polybius 30.31; jamie-02 uses this for sovereignty; here it is the economic point). Note the verb "farmed": the harbour dues were sold to contractors for a lump sum, the same tax-farming the Knights and the Ottomans would use. One Roman decision cut the port's yield by 85 per cent. The Windmills mole where Scarlet Lady ties up is where that toll was collected.

**Gifts as capital.** After the earthquake of c. 227 BC the kings rebuilt Rhodes: Ptolemy's timber for ten quinqueremes and ten triremes, 3,000 pieces of sail-cloth, 3,000 talents for the Colossus, 20,000 artabae of grain to feed crews (Polybius 5.89, quoted in research/16). Polybius's list also records customs exemptions: Hiero and Gelon of Syracuse "relieved Rhodian ships trading to their ports from the payment of customs" (5.88.7), and Seleucus, "besides exempting Rhodians trading to his dominions from custom duties, presented them with ten quinqueremes fully equipped, two hundred thousand medimni of corn" (5.89.8); Ptolemy's corn was "a million artabae" in the main gift plus 12,000 for games and sacrifices and the 20,000 "to feed the crews of ten triremes" (5.89.1-4; Loeb text at LacusCurtius, verified). Rhodes, in other words, converted an earthquake into a trade-treaty round. Polybius's own moral is that the Rhodians "obtained" this by dignified public diplomacy; he is contrasting them with the beggarly Greek states of his own day.

**The law that survived the fleet.** Research/16 has the *Lex Rhodia de iactu* text and the emperor's line "I am lord of the world, but the law is lord of the sea". For Jamie the point is the continuity: the Rhodian rule that jettisoned cargo is "made good by the contribution of all" is general average, codified in the York-Antwerp Rules (Wikipedia's General average article says "first codification ... 1890", "updated in 1994, 2004 and 2016"; the fuller history is the York Rules of 1864 and the York and Antwerp Rules of 1877, revised at Liverpool in 1890 and again in 1924, 1950, 1974, 1994, 2004 and 2016, the current text being YAR 2016; the pre-1890 dates could not be checked online this session, so say "codified in the nineteenth century and last revised in 2016"). Every bill of lading Scarlet Lady's operator signs for stores contains a general-average clause. That is a Rhodian invention still charging premiums. (Wikipedia's "c. 800 BC" for the Lex Rhodia is worthless; the rule is Hellenistic custom quoted by a Roman jurist of c. AD 230.)

## 3. Clubs as mutual insurance: the eranos and the shared grave (Tell at stops 13 and 14, the Jewish quarter, as the deep prehistory of a self-organising minority; also stop 6)

jamie-01 has the scale (about 200 associations, one man in twenty-three of them) and the mixed membership. This section adds what the clubs did with money, from the inscriptions Philip Harland has translated.

**A grave bought by subscription, 185 BC (Ialysos).** A club of "contributors devoted to the Samothracian gods, Aristoboulos, Hermes, and the Panathenaian festival" had 36 members, "several Rhodians and a mixture of immigrants". The stone records that "the following contributors, indicating that they would enhance the fund (*eranos*), promised to give voluntarily money for the price of the place, and they did pay", about 1,000 drachmas in all; the benefactor Ktesiphon from Chersonesos "who has also been crowned with a gold crown, dedicated this on behalf of the contributors", "when Nikasagoras was priest" (Harland, "Dedication of a communal grave by an immigrant association", dated by the priest of Helios, of course). *Eranos* is the key word: originally a shared meal to which each guest brings a dish, then a friendly loan raised by subscription, then the club itself. The club was a burial society, a credit union and a dinner club in one.

**A building fund, c. 195 BC (Rhodes town).** "Those choosing to contribute towards the construction of the buildings" around the club's plots signed up by name and city with sums: Xouthos from Antioch 20 drachmas, Myron from Ephesos 30, Dionysios from Sikyon 5, Sophon from Ilion 10, Dromon from Cappadocia "5 (plus doors)" (Harland, "List of contributors ... led by Xouthos of Antioch"). Members were *eranistai*, "contributors", under an *archeranistes*. A Cappadocian immigrant paying five drachmas and a set of doors toward the club's funerary buildings is the texture Jamie wants.

**A benefactor's decree (second century BC).** "Zenon the Selgian, benefactor" of "the association of Hermogeneian Aphrodisiasts" had the club's title deeds "concerning the properties belonging to the association ... and of the burial plots" cut in marble and "set up ... at the communal burial plots in a place which seems ... clearly visible and secure"; members came from Selge, Phaselis, Galatia and Arabia (Harland, "Decrees by Zenon of Selge"). Property, legal title, a visible notice-board: the club behaved like a small municipality.

**Craft guilds.** Not attested as such at Hellenistic Rhodes in anything I could reach; the associations are cultic, ethnic or benefactor-named, not trade-named. The nearest thing to organised labour is Diodorus's siege sentence, "the craftsmen gave their skilled services for the preparation of the arms" (20.84). Say "no guilds, but clubs", and let the Ottoman esnaf at stop 16 be the contrast.

## 4. The fleet as employer, and the estates across the water (Tell at stops 19, 20 and 17)

Strabo's sentence is the welfare state in one line: "the people are supplied with provisions and the needy are supported by the well-to-do, by a certain ancestral custom ... there were certain liturgies that supply provisions, so that at the same time the poor man receives his sustenance and the city does not run short of useful men, and in particular for the manning of the fleets" (14.2.5). Rowing was the job; the liturgy was the wage; the trierarch was the employer. Vincent Gabrielsen's thesis (*The Naval Aristocracy of Hellenistic Rhodes*, 1997; summarised in jamie-01) is that the families who commanded the ships also monopolised the priesthoods whose names are on the jars in section 1. The shipsheds (up to about 100 around Mandraki, research/16) are the factory floor. The Peraia, the mainland estates whose people held Rhodian magistracies (Wiemer, in jamie-01), supplied timber, grain and the land-rents that let a rich man afford a trireme. From the Mandraki mole Jamie can see that mainland.

## 5. The Knights' economy: a mint, a court, a customs farm and a remittance pipe (Tell at stops 7, 9, 4 and 5)

jamie-05 section 4 already has the responsions (180,000 florins a year in 1329, of which 20,000 from Cyprus; the 1320 debt of 500,000 florins to the Bardi and Peruzzi), the sugar ("the lucrative sugar crop from their estates in Cyprus and Rhodes", Luttrell), the slave market (Coureas), the customs farm (the *commercium*, sold to the Genoese Tobias Lomelino and the Catalan Bartolomeu de Parets in 1471-74) and the exchange rate (a Rhodian ducat = 36.5 aspers in 1449-54). Do not repeat those. Add:

**The mint.** The Order's early Rhodian coinage was "Greek influenced" and "did not facilitate trade", so Grand Master Hélion de Villeneuve (1319-46) replaced it with coins on "the weight standard of the gigliato, and a reverse type clearly derived from it, but with an obverse based on the seal of his order", copying, it seems, the Provençal rather than the Neapolitan gigliati (Wikipedia, Gigliato; the Neapolitan original weighed about 4 g). The classic Rhodian type, which the museum and dealers' cases show, is the Grand Master "kneeling before a double cross placed on three steps" with his shield behind him, and on the reverse "the bust of Saint John the Baptist holding in his left hand the Order's banner and the Agnus Dei" (Lambros 1877, describing two silver aspers of Raymond Zacosta, 1461-67, then "the only one of the nineteen Grand Masters of Rhodes from whom no coins had previously been found"). So every Grand Master struck his own money, the way every priest of Helios had his own jar-stamp. The "asper" is a Byzantine-Turkish word (the Ottoman *akçe* was also called asper by Europeans); the ducat was Venetian; the gigliato Angevin. The Knights' purse was a map of who they traded with. The mint's location is not securely known (probably in the Collachium; unverified).

**The Castellania as market court.** jamie-05 section 9 has the building (d'Amboise, 1507; the angel with two shields). For this track the point is function: the *Castellania* was the seat of the *castellanus*, the Order's civil judge for the Greek and Latin townsmen, on the "Magna et Communis Platea", the town's common square at the foot of the market street; the minority reading makes it the *Basilica Mercatorum*, the merchants' court; under the Ottomans its ground floor was the fish market (jamie-05 and verify-deltas). Whichever reading is right, a commercial dispute over a cargo landed at the Marine Gate 150 m away was judged here, and the Rhodian Sea Law of section 2 was, via Roman and Byzantine law, part of what a Latin judge would cite. Point Jamie at the outside stair: courts with external stairs are courts where the crowd waits in the square for the verdict.

**Who worked.** The four legal conditions (free, *marinarii* owing hereditary rowing service, serfs, slaves) are in jamie-05 section 6. The economic gloss: rowing service was the Knights' version of the Rhodian liturgy turned inside out; the ancient state fed the poor so they would row, the Order made rowing a hereditary tax on Greek families, and by the fifteenth century commuted it to money (jamie-05, hedged). The Malta figures give the later trajectory: on the Order's galleys non-Christian slaves rose "from 30% in 1548 to 71% in 1669" of rowers (Wikipedia, Slavery in Malta). The windmills on the mole where the ship is berthed (Casola counted sixteen in 1494, research/16) ground the grain that fed those rowers.

## 6. The Ottoman sanjak: tax farms, endowments, guilds, sponges (Tell at stops 16, 4, 15 and 12)

**Whose province.** From 1546 Rhodes was a sanjak of the Eyalet of the Archipelago (1533-1864), the maritime province created for Hayreddin Barbarossa and held by the Kapudan Pasha, "a vizier of three horsetails and a member of the Imperial Council"; its sub-governors were titled *derya-bey*, "sea-lord", rather than sanjak-bey, and its revenues were the fleet's (Wikipedia, Eyalet of the Archipelago). The island's taxes, in other words, were a naval budget line: the Rhodian fleet-state of section 4 reappears with the Sultan's admiral as trierarch.

**How the tax reached him.** Through the *iltizam*: "a form of tax farm that appeared in the 15th century in the Ottoman Empire", sold "to wealthy notables, who would then reap up to five times the amount they had paid by taxing the peasants", "typically an annual agreement", replaced from 1695 by the life-term *malikane* and abolished "during the Tanzimat reforms in 1856" (Wikipedia, Iltizam). Polybius's "farmed for a million drachmae" and the Knights' Genoese customs-farmer are the same instrument; the Ottomans simply extended it from the harbour to the olive groves. Rhodes-specific farm figures I could not reach; keep it structural.

**The endowment instead of the budget.** jamie-06 has the vakf in detail (the 1885 register: 44 mosques, 4 tekkes, 3 madrasas, 1 library, 2 schools, 50 fountains, 20 cemeteries; the Hafiz Ahmed Agha foundation of 1792 and its clock of 1852; the Vakouf Rodou today). Two additions. First, scale: "by the early 1800s, more than half of all arable land in the Ottoman Empire was classified as a waqf", including "one-third in Greece" (Wikipedia, Waqf). Second, the mechanism, which is Eumenes's grain fund again: a founder locks property away for ever, names an administrator (*mutawalli*), and the rents pay for a library, a soup kitchen, a fountain, a teacher. The endowment complex of Haseki Sultan in Jerusalem, the textbook example, was funded by "shops, a bazaar, two soap plants, 11 flour mills and two bathhouses" (Wikipedia, Waqf). On Rhodes the shops of Sokratous street paid for the library across from the mosque at its top.

**The guilds.** An Ottoman town's trades were organised as *esnaf*, "corporations": membership "gave people a voice and was an important part of one's identity"; "guilds were an urban phenomenon"; they were religiously mixed, and "economic activity within guilds was controlled by political powers", with buyers assigned to sources (Wikipedia, Esnaf; the Sanjak of Bosnia article adds that esnafs "had precise statutes (*nizam*) that regulated all aspects" of production). For Rhodes specifically I could not reach a guild register or an Evliya Çelebi trade list in this session; what is secure is the town's function, which Wikipedia's Rhodes (city) article states plainly: under the Ottomans the city "maintained its main economic function as a market for the agricultural products of the interior of the island and the surrounding small islands". And the market street had a judge at its head: the Jews called Sokratous *Arriva del Kadi*, "up at the Kadi's", because the Islamic judge sat at the top and the Jewish shops were at the bottom (jamie-06). Kadi's court, Castellania, Rhodian prytaneis: three eras of commercial justice within 300 m of Hippocrates Square.

**The sponge and the ship.** The Dodecanese's real Ottoman-era wealth was not on Rhodes but on Symi and Kalymnos, whose boats sailed from Rhodes's harbours. Symi submitted peacefully in 1522 and "was allowed to retain many of its privileges, so its prosperity continued virtually uninterrupted"; "at a certain point, the island had the largest sponge fishing fleet in the world" and "the population peaked at approximately 22,500 during the 19th century" (Wikipedia, Symi); Greek Wikipedia has it at 25,000 in 1900-10, with the Petrides Brothers running "offices in Syra, Piraeus, Paris, London and Trieste" and the industry booming "between 1865-1930" on the new diving technique. That technique was the *skafandro*, the surface-supplied helmet suit, introduced around 1865; "some estimates put the death toll at around 10,000 Mediterranean divers in the first 50 years of surface supplied diving"; in 1867 the suit-suppliers reported "that out of 24 divers using 12 scaphandros, 10 died during the season", and in 1868 an Italian doctor found that "of the thirty divers at Kalymnos, two died and two were paralysed that year" (Wikipedia, Sponge diving). Kalymnos's folk dance *Mihanikos* "depicts the crippling effects of decompression sickness" (Wikipedia, Kalymnos). This is Jamie's anthropology of work at its starkest: a livelihood so lethal it had its own dance. The colonial-era cost was emigration: from 1905 "John Cocoris introduced the technique of sponge diving to Tarpon Springs" Florida, and the first Saronic divers "were soon outnumbered by those from the Dodecanese islands of Kalymnos, Symi, and Halki" (Wikipedia, Tarpon Springs).

## 7. The Italian firm: tobacco, public works, and the invention of the tourist (Tell at stops 18 and 21; stop 9 for the Palace as showcase)

jamie-08 has the Foro Italico, the buildings and their reuses. For the economy: the Italian Wikipedia article on Governor Mario Lago says the Dodecanese "experienced a phase of growth, supported by Italian investment", with modern agricultural enterprises and industrial activity including "la manifattura tabacchi TEMI", the tobacco manufactory; and that "Greek merchants and large landowners were particularly disadvantaged, subject to tax controls and expropriations" (it.wikipedia, Mario Lago). TEMI is usually expanded as *Tabacchi Egei Manifattura Italiana*; I found no page giving its founding date, workforce or the fate of its Rhodes factory, so name it and stop. The public works were "sometimes using forced Greek labor" (Wikipedia, Italian Islands of the Aegean), and Italian settlers (16,711 across the islands by 1936) "of Rhodes and Kos were farmers involved in setting up new agricultural settlements" (same). The 1936 census counted 140,848 people in the islands. The lira was the currency: the fourth monetary regime on the same quay after drachma, asper-ducat and kuruş.

**The hotel that started it.** "The Italians also surveyed the islands for the first time in history, and began to introduce mass-scale tourism to Rhodes and Kos" (Wikipedia, Italian Islands of the Aegean). The instrument was the Grande Albergo delle Rose, 1925-27, by Florestano Di Fausto and Michele Platania (it.wikipedia, Rodi (città) and Isole italiane dell'Egeo), on the Mandraki shore a few hundred metres from stop 21, with the Kallithea thermal baths (opened July 1929) as the spa half of the offer. It is now Casino Rodos. Its most consequential guests were not tourists: the Egypt-Israel armistice talks "began on the Greek island of Rhodes on 12 January" 1949 under Ralph Bunche, and "on 24 February the Israel-Egypt Armistice Agreement was signed in Rhodes" (Wikipedia, 1949 Armistice Agreements); the venue was this hotel, the Hotel des Roses, Bunche's headquarters, on the hotel's own account (verify-deltas). Do not cite the Moshe Sharett article for that: its sentence "The final agreement was signed at the 'Grande Albergo delle Rose' in Rhodes (now the Casino Rodos) on 3 April 1949" refers to the Jordan agreement, which was negotiated at Shuneh and only formally signed at Rhodes. Bunche got the Nobel Peace Prize for it. A neutral island renting itself out as a place for enemies to talk is exactly what Hellenistic Rhodes did for a living; tell Jamie that the "Rhodes formula" of indirect talks is still a diplomats' term.

## 8. Today: the airport, the ship, the hundred stamps, and Sokratous (Tell at stops 1, 21, 15 and 4)

jamie-09 has the 2025 cruise total (547,549 passengers, sixth Greek port), the €5 cruise levy in force to 30 September, the 100-insurance-stamps rule for winter benefit, the 9,000 imported seasonal workers, the July 2026 rents and the laiki markets. Do not repeat; add the frame numbers.

- **Air first, sea second.** Rhodes airport handled 5,542,567 passengers in 2019 (fourth in Greece), 1,551,123 in 2020, 6,142,813 in 2023, 6,921,748 in 2024 and 7,093,264 in 2025 (Wikipedia, Rhodes International Airport, statistics table). Set beside 547,549 cruise passengers: the ship Jamie arrived on is about 7 per cent of the island's visitor flow, but it is the part that lands in the Old Town between 09:00 and 18:00.
- **The town's bed count.** Within the city limits there are "some 25-30,000 hotel beds", and "during summer population reaches 100,000" against an official 56,440 in the 2021 census (49,541 in 2011) and an estimated 65-70,000 actual residents (Wikipedia, Rhodes (city)). The city's three ports divide the work: Kolona "serves intra-Dodecanese traffic", the Central port "cruise ships and international traffic", Akandia (2009) "domestic as well cargo traffic" (Wikipedia, Rhodes (city)). The claim in an earlier draft that Rhodes became a Costa Cruises home-port in 2012 is in neither Wikipedia article and is unsourced; drop it.
- **This autumn.** The cruisetimetables calendar lists 20 calls in September 2026, 20 in October, 8 in November and 9 in December, 57 in the last four months of the year (cruisetimetables.com, Rhodes page, read 8 Sep 2026); verify-deltas adds that on 30 September Scarlet Lady (09:00-18:00) shares the port with Celestyal Discovery (07:00-18:00).
- **Seasonal life.** jamie-09's structure (hotels close 1 November; the hundred stamps; the housing squeeze) is the modern liturgy: the season is the trierarch, and the stamps are the ration.
- **Sokratous.** The bazaar street from Hippocrates Square up to the Suleymaniye sells, on a ship day, what its buyers can carry: gold and silver jewellery, leather sandals and bags, natural sponges (from Kalymnos, and increasingly imported, so ask), olive-oil soap, spices and herb teas, embroidered linens, umbrellas, fridge magnets, replica Knights' armour and "Colossus" bottles of ouzo; the Turkish coffee-mill at Sokratous 76 (jamie-06) and the fixed-price restaurants of Hippocrates Square (verify-deltas warns of table-hustling and a €20 dropped-glass charge) mark the two ends. Shops run roughly 09:00 to 20:00 in season (research/07 and 09). Treat the list of goods as this writer's observation to be confirmed on the day, not as a sourced inventory. The same street sold to Casola's pilgrims in 1494 (research/16) and to Ottoman townsmen under the kadi; the customer has changed, the pitch has not.
- **The lost livelihood.** "The Greek sponge trade was centered close in the Dodecanese, featuring Kalymnos until the mid-80s, when a disease hit the eastern Mediterranean destroying a great number of sponges"; "today, Kalymnos faces a lack of sponges due to the outbreak of a disease that has decimated sponge crops", and the island keeps a "Sponge Week" a week after Easter to honour "Kalymnian gold" (Wikipedia, Kalymnos). Sponges "are still fished individually, by hand" by a handful of boats. The sponge in the Sokratous shop window is the memory of an industry, sold to the industry that replaced it.

## Continuities and contrasts

**What an ancient Rhodian would recognise.** The Hospitaller's customs farmer (his own city sold the harbour dues to contractors); the kadi's price-fixing and the esnaf's assigned markets (his agoranomoi did the same); the vakf's rent-funded teachers (Eumenes's grain fund); the cruise passenger who lands, spends and leaves (every Egyptian grain ship "forced to touch at Rhodes"); the sponge boat as a family's whole capital (his trireme). He would not recognise wage labour as the norm; his poor were fed to row, not paid to serve.

**What a Hospitaller would recognise.** The Ottoman sanjak's revenue going to a naval commander (his Order's Admiral); the vakf (his Order's own hospital was an endowment); the remittance economy of 20th-century Dodecanesian emigrants and of today's imported seasonal workers (his responsions came from 656 commanderies; jamie-05); a coin with the ruler's picture (his Grand Master kneeling before the cross). He would be puzzled that nobody owns the rowers.

**What an Ottoman-era householder would recognise.** The ancient club that buried its members by subscription (his guild and his mahalle did both); the Knights' Castellania as a merchants' court (his kadi's court, with the same commercial cases); the ancient priest-dated jar (his own contracts dated by the Hijri year and sealed by a guild kethüda); the Sokratous shopkeeper's day organised by a public clock (Fethi Pasha's 1852 London clock; jamie-06). He would not recognise a state that taxes without farming the tax to a contractor.

**What a resident today would recognise.** Astymedes's 85 per cent revenue collapse when Rome opened Delos (a season lost to a war, a pandemic, or a cruise line's re-routing); the Italian hotel built to manufacture visitors (the whole modern island); the diver's dance about decompression (the waiter's arithmetic about a hundred stamps); the clubs of immigrants pooling money for a place of their own (the Bangladeshi and Pakistani seasonal workers' associations of the New Town, which I could not source but which exist in every Greek tourist town; hedge). She would be surprised only by the stamped jar: that a state once cared enough about its exports to date every one of them to the month.

**The single line through all four:** Rhodes sells position, not product. The jar, the toll, the responsion, the tax farm, the sponge and the ship are six ways of monetising a rock that happens to sit where Egypt's traffic has to pause.

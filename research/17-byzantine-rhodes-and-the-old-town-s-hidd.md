# Byzantine Rhodes and the Old Town's hidden chapels (654-1310), the Gabalas rogue-barons, the Hospitaller "heist" of 1306-1310, and the birthday cake of the saint of lost things

## The 650 years the digest skips

Stand on the hill under the Palace of the Grand Master and you are standing on the oldest fortress in town. The palace "was originally built in the late 7th century as a Byzantine citadel" (Wikipedia, Palace of the Grand Master). Kastra.eu spells out the three-part Byzantine city that grew from it: "the citadel (where the Palace of the Grand Master was later built), the Collacium which was the fortified upper city and the burgo for the general population which in the Byzantine era was outside of the walls." The Greek page adds the timeline: "the ancient city was succeeded by a Byzantine one, considerably smaller in size and already fortified since the 7th century AD... In the 12th century the wall was expanded to encompass approximately 175 stremmata," and "portions of this original fortification survive today incorporated into later structures, primarily in the Collachium area."

Why the 7th century? Because the sea had turned hostile. In 654 Caliph Muawiya's fleet under Abu al-A'war pillaged Rhodes and carried off the scrap of the Colossus (covered elsewhere). In 672/673 Junada ibn Abi Umayya came back to stay: Rhodes "was converted into a forward supply base and centre for Muslim naval raids. Its garrison of 12,000 men was regularly rotated back to Syria... and the Arabs even sowed wheat and brought along animals to graze on the island" (Wikipedia, Siege of Constantinople 674-678). Only after the Arab armada broke on Constantinople's Greek fire were "the Arab garrisons... withdrawn from their bases on the Byzantine coastlands, including Rhodes, in 679-680." Rhodes then served the Cibyrrhaeot naval theme; in 715 the imperial fleet "rebelled, killed their commander John the Deacon" here at Rhodes and sailed off to make an emperor, two years before the great siege of 717.

**Where to see the Byzantine line today (all mapped by OSM from Ephorate signage):**

## The rogue barons: Caesar Gabalas

After the Fourth Crusade in 1204 nobody in Constantinople could hold Rhodes, so a local magnate did. Leo Gabalas ruled "ca. 1203 (?) - ca. 1240", styling himself "Caesar" and "Lord of Rhodes and the Cyclades." His copper coins are pure D&D faction heraldry: obverse "ΚΑΙϹΑΡ Ο ΓΑΒΑΛΑϹ" (The Caesar Gabalas), reverse "Ο ΔΟVΛΟC ΤΟV ΒΑCΙΛΕ[ΩC]" (Servant of the Emperor) (Wikipedia, Leo Gabalas). Servant in name only. Emperor John III Vatatzes of Nicaea "launched an expedition against Rhodes in 1232/3... led by his grand domestic Andronikos Palaiologos and closely supervised by the Emperor himself"; the island was ravaged, yet the very next August (1234) Gabalas "recognized the Doge of Venice as his lord, agreed on mutual assistance against an attack by Vatatzes... and promised assistance in subduing any rebellions on the Venetian colony of Crete." Then "in 1235/6, however, Gabalas joined in Vatatzes's attack on Constantinople, and is even recorded as having fought against the Venetian fleet." His brother John, "whom the sources do not call a Caesar but only 'Master of Rhodes'", struck coins reading "Ο ΑVΘΕΝΤΗC ΤΗC ΡΟΔΟV" and quietly dropped the "servant" formula.

The end came as a night raid. In 1248, while John was away campaigning near Nicomedia, a Genoese fleet "found [Rhodes] to be virtually unprotected... and in a surprise night attack seized the city of Rhodes." The Nicaean historian George Akropolites, blockading from Filerimos, "even complains that they slept with the most beautiful local women, expelling the old and ugly ones from the city." William of Villehardouin dropped off 100 crusader knights to help the Genoese; Nicaean reinforcements under Kontostephanos "caught the Achaeans off guard while they were scattered in the countryside... and killed them all." The Genoese surrendered in late 1249/early 1250 and Rhodes became a province again (Wikipedia, Genoese occupation of Rhodes).

## The heist: 1306-1310

Fulk de Villaret's takeover reads like a contract job. Rhodes was still Byzantine, nominally under Andronikos II, but its islands were farmed out to Genoese corsairs. On 27 May 1306 the Order signed with one of them, Vignolo de' Vignoli: "Vignolo thereby ceded his rights on Kos and Leros to the Hospitallers, with the right of retaining Lardos and one more estate of his own choice on Rhodes. In the other islands... Vignolo would enjoy extensive rights as 'vicarius seu justiciarius'... and would divide their income, with two thirds going to the Order and one third to Vignolo." The Pope "was most likely kept in the dark." The crew sailed from Limassol on 23 June: "35 Knights, 6 Levantine horsemen, and 500 foot soldiers." Vignolo's scouting nearly ended in capture when "the locals were warned... by a Greek serving the Order." Two assaults on the city failed. Then, per the Cypriot chronicles of Amadi and Bustron: "On 11 November, they took the citadel of Filerimos... through the treason of a local Greek; the garrison of 300 Turks was massacred" (Wikipedia, Hospitaller conquest of Rhodes; kastra.eu: "the garrison, consisting of Turkish mercenaries employed by the Byzantine emperor, were slaughtered"). Andronikos sent "eight galleys to aid the city"; the Knights "were forced to lift the siege after killing 80 Greeks and losing about a dozen of their own and about 40 horses." Villaret returned in 1310 with "26 galleys... 200-300 Knights and 3,000 foot soldiers," and the city fell "on 15 August 1310" - by luck: a Genoese supply ship "was blown off course to Famagusta," its captain was hauled before the Grand Master, and "to escape execution, the ship captain convinced the garrison to surrender on the condition that their lives and property would be spared." Delaville Le Roulx argued for 1308; "every year from 1306 to 1310" has been proposed, but 15 August, the Assumption, "seems secure."

What became of the Greeks: the population "had dwindled to some 10,000"; the Knights imported Latin settlers; "the Knights evicted the Orthodox metropolitan, and installed a Latin Archbishop in his stead. The Patriarchate of Constantinople continued to appoint metropolitans in exile... the Orthodox community on the island was administered by a council comprising local priests and secular potentates," and the 1447 Union of Florence "met with ardent opposition by the Orthodox populace... forcing the Knights to violently suppress their reactions" (Wikipedia, Metropolis of Rhodes). Panagia tou Kastrou, the Orthodox cathedral of the 11th century, became "Sancta Maria Castelli Rodi."

## The chapels a walker can enter on a Wednesday morning

**Agios Fanourios (Dorieos square, 36.44153, 28.22597)** - the one to build the birthday around. A working parish, open summer 07:00-19:00 (Metropolis of Rhodes). A 13th-century free-cross domed church "built on the remains of Late Roman buildings" with three fresco layers: early 13th century in the sanctuary, showing "clear influences from the art of the Empire of Nicaea"; 1335/6 over the whole church including an "extensive Last Judgement above the arcosolia flanking the doorway," where the donors are painted "in a paradise landscape"; and a mid-15th-century layer repainted by the Italians. Under the Ottomans it was "Peial el Din Medjid." It was only dedicated to Fanourios in 1946, because the saint's real church "clearly placed outside the walled city, was already destroyed when the Knights began works to extend the Byzantine wall (or, according to others, was demolished in 1481)."

**The legend, separated from fact.** The Greek synaxarion (saint.gr) says the icon was found in the 14th century when workers "were excavating old houses in the southern part of the old wall"; Metropolitan Neilos II Diasorenos (1355-1369) read the inscription "ὁ ἅγιος Φανῶ...", rebuilt the church and wrote the saint's service. The OCA version says a Muslim ruler rebuilding the walls "at the end of the fifteenth century, or the beginning of the sixteenth" found a ruined church of rotted icons and one that "looked as if it had been painted that very day"; OrthodoxWiki says c. 1500 and Arabs. Rhodes was not under Muslim rule until 1522, so the 14th-century Neilos version fits history; the c.1500 version is the popular one. All agree on the icon: a young soldier holding a cross and lit candle ringed by twelve scenes - questioned by the judge, stoned, beaten with rods, raked with iron hooks, jailed, tried again, burned with candles, racked, thrown to beasts, crushed under a boulder, holding hot coals before idols, and standing in a furnace in prayer.

**The cake.** Fanouropita "traditionally consists of seven, nine, or eleven ingredients, the number varying by region" (Wikipedia); seven "as many as the sacraments" (Greek Wikipedia): flour, oil, sugar, orange juice, baking powder, walnuts, raisins, plus cinnamon, clove. No butter, no eggs. It is blessed in church, "cut into forty pieces," and eaten with a wish that the saint's sinful mother be forgiven - a folk belief the Church "does not formally hold." You bake it so the saint "reveals" (phanerono) what you lost: an object, a job, a spouse. Birthday quest: light a candle, name the lost thing, then go find cake. Whether any Rhodes bakery has fanouropita on a Wednesday is unverified; the nearest bakeries are Old Town Corner Bakery (36.44089, 28.22460) and Stani, the town's classic confectioner on Agias Anastasias (36.43644, 28.22661).

**Agia Triada, Ippoton (36.44510, 28.22555)** - built 1365-1374, originally dedicated to Archangel Michael, single-nave Gothic, with a rare "Throne of Grace" fresco; Ottoman "Khan Zade Mescidi"; Orthodox since 1947 (Wikipedia). It stands on the north side of Ippoton between the Inn of France (36.44515, 28.22609) and the Inn of Provence, exactly where guides put the Chapel of the French Langue and the chaplain's house; the 1365-74 dates are Grand Master Raymond Berenger's. Treat "chaplain's chapel" and "Agia Triada" as the same complex. Usually locked.

**Agios Spyridon (Diokleous, 36.44277, 28.22615)** - 13th-century church rebuilt in the 16th century on top of the old one, keeping the sanctuary arch and an early-16th-century Virgin and St John; Ottoman "Kavakli Mescidi"; its minaret "survives whole to this day." Active Orthodox; hours unverified.

**Agia Aikaterini (Perikleous, 36.44094, 28.22987)** - three-aisled, four building phases from the 14th century; by tradition Suleiman prayed here first after 1522, hence its Ottoman name "Ilk Mihrab," the niche of first prayer; frescoes of Peter and Paul, four healer saints after a plague, a kneeling donor couple offering a model of the church, and a life-of-St-Catherine cycle "unique in the monumental painting of the East" (Metropolis of Rhodes). Locked monument.

**Panagia tou Bourgou (Pindarou, 36.44284, 28.23087)** - early 14th century under Helion de Villeneuve, a Gothic three-aisled basilica with six burial chapels and seven arched tombs; "only three vaulted arches have survived," bisected by a 1955 road; summer events, arches lit at night. Walk-through ruin.

**St Catherine Hospice (36.44311, 28.23134)** - the Knights' hostel of 1391-92 "for distinguished visitors," rebuilt after the 1481 earthquake. Hours unverified.

**Panagia tou Kastrou / Byzantine Museum (36.44508, 28.22749)** - the 11th-century cross-in-square cathedral, rebuilt 1319-1334 as a basilica "covered with Gothic ribbed vaults" with the arms of Pope John XXII and Villeneuve, "Enderum mosque" 1522-1947, with a 14th-century Virgin on the north-west pier and a Western St Lucy. The Metropolis page says the museum is "temporarily" closed for reinstallation; the digest's 08:30-15:30, Tuesday closed, 5 EUR could not be confirmed - check at the Palace ticket desk.

## Reading the layer

A 7th-century citadel became a Grand Master's palace and then Mussolini's holiday house. An 11th-century Orthodox cathedral became a Latin one, then the "inner" mosque, then a museum. Little cross churches became mesjids with names like "first prayer niche" and "Alemnaki," then Orthodox parishes again in 1946-47. And the one thing that outlived every regime is a painted board that came out of the rubble of a wall repair with its colours intact - the saint whose whole job is finding what was lost.

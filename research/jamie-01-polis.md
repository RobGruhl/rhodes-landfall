# Jamie 01. The Rhodian city-state as a way of organizing people

## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes town is one of the very few places where you can walk on a city that was *designed* as a social contract. In 408/7 BC three old Dorian towns, Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros, agreed to stop being three states and to pour their citizens into one new city drawn on a grid at the island's northern tip. The trick, and Jamie's angle, is that they did not dissolve the old identities: the three towns became the three "tribes" (phylai) of the new state, the old villages became demes, an even older layer of land-units (ktoinai) kept running underneath, and for the next three centuries the Rhodians ran a mixed constitution that Strabo bluntly called "not democratic" while insisting the rich fed the poor so there would always be men for the fleet. The navy was the welfare state and the class system at once. Around that citizen core lived thousands of foreigners, metics, slaves and freedmen who built their own parallel society out of private clubs (koina) that copied the state's tribes and festivals in miniature. The ancient city is under the medieval one: the Great Harbour (where the ship docks) was the ancient commercial harbour, Mandraki the closed military harbour, the Palace hill was the sanctuary of Helios, and the Old Town's street lines still tilt the way the ancient grid did. Rob's track tells the sieges and the Knights as factions; this track tells who lived where, who counted as a citizen, who fed whom, and why. Nothing below is narration; it is material, with the uncertainties marked.

## 1. The founding contract (Tell at stops 1, 2 and 9; full version at Monte Smith B-side)

**The act.** Diodorus, our only narrative source, gives it one sentence under the 93rd Olympiad year: "the inhabitants of the island of Rhodes left the cities of Ielysus, Lindus and Cameirus and settled in one city, that which is now called Rhodes" (Diodorus 13.75.1). Convention dates this 408/7 BC; Diodorus's own archon-dating in book 13 is slightly out of step with modern chronology, so treat "408" as the conventional year, not a documented month. A modern tradition credits Dorieus son of Diagoras (the Olympic boxer of Pindar's Olympian 7) with leading the union; no ancient source states it, and the municipality's history page does not mention him either (it says only that in 408 BC the three cities founded the city of Rhodes), so if you use it at all, tell it as an unattributed modern conjecture.

**The architect.** Strabo, writing under Augustus (there is no clear evidence he visited Rhodes himself): "The present city was founded at the time of the Peloponnesian War by the same architect, as they say, who founded the Peiraeus" (14.2.9), i.e. Hippodamus of Miletus. The "as they say" is Strabo's own hedge; Hippodamus would have been very old by 408. What is certain is the grid. The standard reconstruction (from Kondis's 1958 study of surviving street lines and sewers, refined by later rescue digs, and repeated on the municipality's and Greek Wikipedia's pages) gives uniform blocks of about 47.7 by 26.5 m, each holding three houses, ringed by streets 5-6 m wide, with wider avenues of 8-11 m bounding districts of 36 blocks (108 houses). The blocks are regular: the point of a Hippodamian city is that every household starts with the same-sized plot. Streets and underground drains ran north-south and east-west; the walls followed the two coasts of the triangle and, on the south, the ridge best suited to defence (Perseus/Princeton Encyclopedia site entry). Greek Wikipedia credits Hippodamus with "a rich system of underground sewers" and a water-supply network. None of the block dimensions are footnoted online; treat them as the received figures rather than verified measurements.

**Why a grid is a social statement.** A grid with identical blocks means allotment: land was measured and handed out, and three populations who had been rivals had to be mixed street by street. Strabo's praise is telling because it is about infrastructure, not beauty: the city "is so far superior to all others in harbours and roads and walls and improvements in general that I am unable to speak of any other city as equal to it" (14.2.5).

**Sovereignty timeline in one breath** (useful at stop 9, the Palace). 408/7 one state; 305-304 Demetrius's siege ends with Rhodes "autonomous and ungarrisoned" (Diodorus 20.99, already in research 05); 3rd-2nd century the great neutral naval republic; 188 BC Rome hands Rhodes Caria and Lycia south of the Maeander; 167 BC Rome, angry at Rhodian neutrality, takes the mainland back and in 166 makes Delos a free port; 42 BC Cassius sacks the city and takes "their ships, money, and public and sacred treasures, with the exception of the chariot of the Sun" (Cassius Dio 47.33; Wikipedia gives 43 BC, but Dio and Appian place it in the campaign season before Philippi, 42); under Rome a "free city" in name; Byzantine theme; 1309 the Knights; 1522 the Ottomans; 1912 Italy; 1947/48 Greece. Rhodes has been a state of its own, a province, a monastic-military colony, a sanjak, a colonial showcase and a Greek region, and the physical city has absorbed each.

## 2. Three cities inside one: tribes, demes, ktoinai (Tell at stop 6 with the museum's inscriptions; expand at Monte Smith)

**Phylai.** Thomsen (The Politics of Association in Hellenistic Rhodes, 2020, ch. 2): "The three formerly independent poleis took on the roles of phylai in the new joint Rhodian state under the names Lindia, Kamiris and Ialysia." The phylai competed against each other at state festivals in the capital, and "remained centred on the three cities, which persisted both as towns and as important centres of cult and local administration." Stephanie Paul (CHS Research Bulletin 2015) stresses how unusual this was: "Ialysos, Cameiros, and Lindos still functioned as political entities after 408/7 BCE and preserved to a certain extent their autonomy and decision-making power. In this respect, Rhodes is a quite unique case compared with other synoecized poleis."

**Demes.** Each tribe was divided into demes (villages or districts), and a Rhodian citizen was formally "so-and-so of deme X". Lindos had twelve demes; Blinkenberg's "triennial rule" showed that the priesthood of Athana Lindia rotated among three groups of four demes each (Parker, in CHS's Subjection, Synoecism and Religious Life). At Lindos one of the old sub-tribes was called Argeia, which suggests the classic Dorian tribal names (Hylleis, Dymanes, Pamphyloi) had already been replaced there (CHS, Mythical Foundations chapter). The deme system reached across the water: mainland communities in the Peraia were Rhodian demes too (see section 6).

**Ktoinai, the oldest layer.** Underneath the demes ran the ktoinai, small territorial units of "mysterious character" (Parker) that predate the synoecism and were "superseded but not replaced by the deme system" (BMCR review of Thomsen). The one document a walker can be told about is a Kameiran decree of soon after 325 BC (Syll.3 339, attalus translation): the Kamirans resolve "to inscribe the names of all the townships (ktoinai) of the Kamirans, on the island and on the mainland" on a stele to be set up in Athena's temple, with clauses on hiring the contractor and paying through the treasurer. Parker adds that another Kameiran decree summons representatives of the ktoinai to consider "the publicly-financed rites of the Kameirians." So a Rhodian household belonged, simultaneously, to a ktoina (land and local rites), a deme (civic registration), a tribe (the old city, festivals and priesthoods) and the polis (assembly, fleet, law). That nested belonging is the anthropological heart of the story.

**Religion as the glue.** Zachhuber (Religious Life in Late Classical and Hellenistic Rhodes, OUP 2024) treats the synoecism "as a pivotal religious shift as well, marked by the rise of Helios as a supra-demotic deity." Paul: "the priesthood associated with his cult was the culmination of the Rhodian priestly career, and the priest was the eponymous official of the polis", appointed "in turns from the tribes of Ialysos, Cameiros, and Lindos." Rhodian public documents are dated "in the priesthood of X" of Helios, the way Athens dated by archon. On the new acropolis "a temple dedicated to Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus was built", with a joint priesthood from at least the 3rd century (Paul); Wikipedia's Acropolis entry adds that this temple "originally housed the written treaties", i.e. the state's foreign-policy archive sat with its guardian gods. Athena Lindia stayed vital; the cults of Ialysos and Kameiros declined as the capital grew (Zachhuber via BMCR). The Halieia, the festival of Helios, had "a procession, a sacrifice, as well as athletic and musical contests" (Paul); Festus, a Roman lexicographer (s.v. October equus), says that every year the Rhodians dedicated a four-horse chariot (quadriga) to the Sun and cast it into the sea; the detail that the horses were "white", and the victors' wreath of white poplar, come from Smith's Dictionary and Wikipedia's Halieia entry, not from Festus, so say "a four-horse chariot" and keep "white" out of the Festus attribution. The sea-chariot is a Roman report of a Rhodian rite; treat it as reported, not archaeologically confirmed.

## 3. The constitution: an "aristocratic democracy" (Tell at stop 4, Hippocrates Square and the Castellania, the medieval court; echo at stop 9)

**What Strabo saw.** "The Rhodians are concerned for the people in general, although their rule is not democratic; still, they wish to take care of their multitude of poor people. Accordingly, the people are supplied with provisions and the needy are supported by the well-to-do, by a certain ancestral custom; and there were certain liturgies that supply provisions, so that at the same time the poor man receives his sustenance and the city does not run short of useful men, and in particular for the manning of the fleets" (Strabo 14.2.5, Loeb). This is the single most important paragraph for Jamie: a welfare system justified by naval manpower, run by the rich through "liturgies" (obligatory public services), and described by a contemporary as explicitly not democratic.

**What Cicero heard.** In De Republica 3.48, a speaker describes Rhodes: "all the people were senators and common citizens alternately, and they had a regular system of rotation in accordance with which they acted as senators for certain months of the year, and as private citizens during certain other months. They received payment for attending meetings in both capacities, and, both in the theatre and the senate-house, the same men decided capital cases and those of every other sort. The senate possessed as much power and influence as the multitude" (attalus translation). Note "the theatre": the Rhodian assembly and courts met in a theatre, and Diodorus confirms that public honours were conferred "in the theatre at the Dionysia" (20.84). The ancient theatre has not been found (Wikipedia, Acropolis of Rhodes), so do not point at the little odeion on Monte Smith as the assembly place.

**The machinery.** Thomsen: "Collective decision-making in the federal Rhodian state was in the hands of the council (boula) and the people (ho damos or ho sympas damos) which met in assembly (ekklesia)." The executive were the prytaneis, a small board (five is the figure usually given, but I could not pin it to a primary source; treat "five" as unverified) serving six-month terms: Polybius 27.7.2 mentions that "Stratocles was prytanis for the second half-year", and Polybius shows the prytaneis receiving envoys and spies (13.5, the Heracleides affair already in research 16). Posidonius the philosopher held the prytany in the 1st century BC (Wikipedia, Posidonius). Alongside them stood strategoi and nauarchs.

**What Aristotle saw go wrong.** Rhodes appears in the Politics as a case study in how a naval democracy breaks: "At Rhodes the demagogues not only provided pay for the multitude, but prevented them from making good to the trierarchs the sums which had been expended by them; and they, in consequence of the suits which were brought against them, were compelled to combine and put down the democracy" (Politics 5, part 5, Jowett; also part 3: "at Rhodes the notables conspired against the people through fear of the suits that were brought against them"). The trierarchs were the rich men who fitted out warships; when the state stopped reimbursing them and let creditors sue, they overthrew the democracy (4th century BC). The Hellenistic settlement Strabo describes, rich men feeding the poor, poor men rowing, is the peace that followed that war. Gabrielsen's 1997 book calls the result a "naval aristocracy": Rhodes' "economic prosperity, internal stability, military might and high political esteem among foreign powers, can be directly linked to the naval aristocracy, as a constantly publicised pride in naval experience was paramount to the self-perception of the upper class" (publisher's summary). "Aristocratic democracy" is a modern label, not an ancient one; say so.

**Law.** The Castellania at Hippocrates Square is the Knights' commercial court; the ancient analogue is the Rhodian maritime custom Rome later called "the Rhodian law" (Digest 14.2, already in research 16). Strabo's death penalty for anyone who "spied on" the secret dockyards or "passed inside them" (14.2.5) is a reminder that this was also a security state.

## 4. Who counted: citizens, metics, foreigners' clubs, slaves, numbers (Tell at stop 5, the Marine Gate; stop 6, the museum; stop 13-14, the Jewish quarter, as the deep prehistory of a foreigners' quarter)

**The census of 305 BC.** Diodorus 20.84: when Demetrius came, the Rhodians counted "about six thousand citizens and as many as a thousand metics and aliens" fit to fight. Citizens of military age at roughly six to one against resident foreigners; multiply by families and the walled city was a place of a few tens of thousands. Popular pages give "50,000 to 60,000" for the Hellenistic city; I found no scholarly derivation for that figure, so quote Diodorus's numbers and call the rest estimate. Rhodes was, by Greek standards, a large city with an unusually large foreign population.

**The welfare decree.** The same chapter is the best window into Rhodian household policy: slaves who fought bravely would be freed and made citizens; the fallen would be buried at public expense; "their parents and children should be maintained, receiving their support from the public treasury"; unmarried daughters would receive "dowries at the public cost"; sons on reaching manhood would be crowned "in the theatre at the Dionysia" and given "a full suit of armour" (Diodorus 20.84). Dowry, arms, burial and pensions: the state stepping into the four duties of a father. The wealthy contributed money, craftsmen labour, and everyone competed "in a spirit of rivalry to surpass the others."

**Foreigners with ethnics.** The "Migrants and Membership Regimes" project (Copenhagen/Aarhus) was cited for a count of about 350 people buried outside the walls whose grave markers carry an "ethnic", the city they came from, in inscriptions of c. 200 BC to AD 100, with "Antioch, Alexandria and Ephesos ... well represented among the foreign population buried in Rhodes." Caution: the project's domain (migrantsandmembership.org) now serves spam and the post could not be re-read, so the 350 figure and the date range are unverified; say "a few hundred" and name the three cities as the commonly cited leaders. Three Phoenician-Greek bilingual stones, found 1914-15 south of Monte Smith, 1939 by the "Apollo" temple and 1968 in the eastern necropolis, are in the Archaeological Museum; one commemorates 'Abd-Melqart, who in Greek called himself "Heraclides of Citium" (Wikipedia, Rhodes Phoenician-Greek bilingual inscriptions). That double name is the immigrant experience in one object.

**Clubs as a shadow state.** Rhodes has produced about 200 private associations, "ca. 165 listed in the Copenhagen Inventory of Ancient Associations" (BMCR on Thomsen), an extraordinary number. They were called koina, eranoi or thiasoi, often named for a god (Sabaziastai, Aphrodisiastai, Haliastai for Helios; these names are standard in the epigraphic literature but are not in the BMCR or Harland pages cited here) or for a benefactor: "many Rhodians gave their names to private associations, such as the Timapoleion koinon"; one unnamed man belonged to "no fewer than twenty-three." Harland's survey of the inscriptions finds mixed membership rather than ethnic ghettos: one association lists "Ephesians, Antiochians, a Phrygian, a Selgian, an Amphipolitan, Alexandrians, Solians, a Knidian, and a Chian alongside several Rhodians"; a communal-grave subscription names contributors from Athens, Chersonesos, Kyzikos, Thera, Chios, Ephesos, Laodikeia, Lucania, Halikarnassos, Herakleia, Miletos, Amisos, Pisidia, Phaselis, Alexandria, Samos, Cilicia, Selge and Armenia. Some associations "divided itself up into sub-groups or 'tribes' in imitation of civic life in Rhodes more generally" for their own festival competitions (Harland). Thomsen's thesis is that associations were not an alternative to the state but "civil society" that played "a mediating or integrative role between state and society", giving foreigners access to property, burial and honours; and that the magisterial elite "hoarded political power by mobilizing certain non-state groups" (BMCR). A foreigner in Rhodes could not vote, but could be crowned, buried and remembered by a club that mimicked the city. The Jewish quarter of the Knights' town, and the Ottoman-era mahalles, are later answers to the same question of how a trading city houses people who are in it but not of it.

**Kinship in stone.** In the museum's sculpture rooms is the grave stele of Krito and Timarista from Kameiros, c. 420-410 BC (research 06): the dead mother Timarista and her daughter Krito standing and embracing, carved in the decade of the synoecism. It is the last generation of Kameiros as its own city, and a mother-daughter monument at that.

## 5. The navy as a social institution (Tell at stops 19 and 20, Mandraki; preface at stop 18, the Pervola shipsheds)

Research 16 already has the shipsheds, the trihemiolia, Chios and the Sea Law; do not repeat those. The Jamie angle is manpower and class. Strabo's liturgies fed the poor "for the manning of the fleets" (14.2.5). Aristotle's trierarchs show who paid for the ships and what happened when they were not repaid. Diodorus 20.84 shows the state guaranteeing the families of the men it sent to die. The Lindos relief's crew of 144 for a trihemiolia, 120 of them rowers (research 16, via Wikipedia), gives the scale: a fleet of even forty such ships needed several thousand rowers, which is most of the six thousand citizens Diodorus counted. Rhodes could not keep a citizen-rowed fleet unless the citizens ate; the "ancestral custom" of rich feeding poor was a naval budget wearing the clothes of charity. Gabrielsen's phrase "naval aristocracy" is the reverse face: the officer families that publicised their sea-service in inscriptions were the same families holding the priesthood of Helios and the prytany. Mandraki was the closed military harbour, guarded, with the secret sheds on its south and east shores; the Great Harbour where the cruise ship lies was the emporion, open to the world (research 16; Perseus site entry: "Two natural harbors, enhanced by the construction of moles, were located on the E side of the city"). One harbour for citizens with oars, one for foreigners with cargo.

Harbour money is documented. In 164 BC, on his second embassy (three years after the humiliating one of 167), the Rhodian envoy Astymedes told the Senate that "while the harbour-dues in former times were farmed for a million drachmae, they now fetch only a hundred and fifty thousand" after Rome made Delos a free port in 166 (Polybius 30.31). A million drachmae a year is the tax base of the welfare-navy.

## 6. The Peraia: a city-state with a mainland (Tell at stop 22, the Naillac stump and St Paul's Gate, looking across to Turkey; also from the walls at stop 11)

From the mole you can see the Anatolian coast. Before 408 the three cities each held land there; after the synoecism it was all Rhodian. Wiemer ("Structure and development of the Rhodian Peraia", Ausonius) distinguishes an **incorporated Peraia**, the Chersonesos (the Bozburun peninsula) and adjacent districts, whose communities were "distributed among six Rhodian demes" and whose people "participated in Rhodes' political life, holding magistracies and priesthoods on the island itself", some even serving as "eponymous magistrate of one of the three old poleis"; from a **subject Peraia** of Carian communities "regarded and treated as subject territory", which lost the title polis and were called koina, sometimes stacked ("the Pisyetai koinon comprised nine koina"), administered by a Rhodian hegemon ("hegemon eis Apeiron kai Physkon kai Chersonason" in an inscription of c. 200 BC) and later a strategos, with subject peoples supplying auxiliaries (Livy's "Mniesuatae et Pisuetae et Tarmiani et Theraei" in 197 BC) and paying taxes. Six Carian communities from 197 BC "dated documents according to the Rhodian eponym", i.e. by the priest of Helios: sovereignty expressed as a calendar. Wikipedia's Rhodian Peraia entry (following Fraser and Bean 1954) gives the arc: separate holdings before 408; after Apamea in 188 "the entirety of Caria and Lycia south of the Maeander River came under Rhodian rule"; lost in 167. Polybius 30.31 has Astymedes put a number on the loss: "From these two towns [Caunus and Stratonicea] our state derived an annual revenue of a hundred and twenty talents." Whether the "Rhodioi" named in Peraia inscriptions were island Rhodians or naturalised Carians given citizenship without a deme is disputed; Wiemer says it "cannot on present evidence be either proved or disproved."

## 7. The grid under the town: what a walker can actually see (Tell at stops 3, 7, 17, 18; the whole thing at Monte Smith)

- **Scale.** The medieval walled city is 65.85 ha (UNESCO). The ancient city was much larger: a triangle from the harbours west to the Monte Smith ridge and south to the necropoleis beyond Rodini and Korakonero (Perseus site entry; research 20). The Knights' town is the north-east corner of Hippodamus's city. UNESCO's own text: the ancient city "was built in 408 BC following a perfect Hippodamian system of town planning", the Byzantine city "was built around the ancient Acropolis", and "like a spiral in space and time, the castle city evolved around its ancient core" (search summary of whc.unesco.org/en/list/493; the page itself blocked fetching, so quote as "UNESCO's description").
- **Alignment.** The municipality's history page states that the medieval street layout largely follows the ancient grid. The Street of the Knights runs almost exactly east-west and Greeka says it "replaced an ancient road that connected the town to the Acropolis of Rhodes"; the fuller guidebook version (port up to the acropolis and the sanctuary of Helios) is not in that source and is not an excavated fact, so phrase as "is said to". The Roman cardo and 2nd-century tetrapylon in the new Pervola open-air museum by Liberty Gate (research 20) are the one place inside the walls where an ancient street surface and its orientation are visible.
- **Symi Square, stop 17.** The 3rd-century BC Temple of Aphrodite is the only Hellenistic temple standing inside the walls, at the foot of the grid, next to the harbour, where a trading city puts the goddess of the sea-lanes and of the Aphrodisiastai clubs.
- **The museum, stop 6.** Grave stelai, round altars and inscriptions line the Hospital's porticoes and courtyard (research 06 and 15; the English Wikipedia museum article does not describe them); the Krito and Timarista stele and the Helios head are inside, and of the three Phoenician bilinguals the 1914-15 stone (KAI 44) stands in the courtyard. A walker should look for demotics and ethnics on the stones: a name plus "Lindopolitas" or "Antiocheus" is the whole class system in one line.
- **The Palace, stop 9.** The "Ancient Rhodes, 2400 years" exhibition, organised for the 1993 anniversary of the foundation, is open Wednesdays 09:00-17:00 (Ministry page via research 06; verify-deltas-round2 notes the upper level only, the lower level being closed for maintenance; check on the day). Badoud's 2024 study puts the sanctuary of Helios, and probably the Colossus, on this hill (research 02).
- **Monte Smith (B-side).** The acropolis was "a monumental zone with sacred places, large temples, public buildings and underground worship spaces", never a fortress (research 15): Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus with the treaties at the north end, the 210 m stadium where the Halieia were run, the gymnasium and its inscribed library catalogue, the "Apollo" temple that Vedder reads as Helios's. This is where the polis performed itself.

## Continuities and contrasts

**An ancient Rhodian** would recognise in the Hospitaller's Rhodes the two-harbour logic (one guarded, one open), the walls hugging the same coasts, the rule that a newcomer lives in the lower town and joins a corporate body to be buried with honour, and the sense that the city's officers are also its priests. He would not recognise a city one-tenth the size, or a ruling class that took vows of celibacy and recruited from abroad rather than by deme.

**A Hospitaller** would recognise the Rhodian trierarch instantly: a rich man obliged to equip a warship, whose status came from sea-service, who sat on the council and quarrelled over who paid. The Order's langues, national divisions competing at the same table, are a fair echo of the three phylai. He would not recognise a state that fed its poor so they could row; his poor were paying tenants and the rowers were increasingly convicts and slaves.

**An Ottoman-era householder** in the Chora would recognise the Rhodian ktoina and deme in the mahalle: a neighbourhood defined by a place of worship, collecting for its own rites, answering to the city through a headman. He would recognise the foreigners' koina in the millet system, where Jews and Greeks ran their own courts and charities inside a state they did not vote in. He would not recognise citizenship at all; there were subjects, not citizens.

**A resident today** lives in a Greek municipality that publishes 408 BC as the city's birthday, dates its public life by a calendar of saints' days that occupy the slots of Helios and Athena, still divides social life by parish and village of origin (a Rhodian from Lindos or Kremasti will tell you), and still watches a harbour economy, now cruise ships rather than grain, decide what the town can afford. The Peraia is Marmaris across the water, a ferry ride and a customs post away; the "subject" side of that relationship has reversed and reversed again.

**What runs through all four:** the harbour as tax base; the sea-facing elite; corporate belonging (ktoina, deme, koinon, langue, mahalle, parish) as the way individuals reach the state; and welfare framed as service, whether to the fleet, to the Hospital, to the mosque's foundation or to the Church. The synoecism's real invention was not the grid but the nested membership, and it never entirely went away.

## Open questions and cautions

- The block dimensions (47.7 x 26.5 m; three houses per block; 5-6 m and 8-11 m streets) are repeated on Greek Wikipedia and the municipal page without citation; the underlying scholarship is Kondis 1958 and Hoepfner and Schwandner's reconstruction, neither read here. Castagnoli's appendix summary was reported as giving Kondis's blocks as "100 by 150 feet" with a short side later confirmed at about 49 m, which is consistent but not identical; the MIT Press page is behind a Cloudflare block and this could not be re-checked.
- "Five prytaneis" is a standard textbook figure I could not tie to a primary text; six-month terms are secure (Polybius 27.7.2).
- The Dorieus-led-the-synoecism tradition and the "50,000-60,000" population are popular claims without visible ancient or scholarly support; the municipality page does not carry the Dorieus story.
- The Street of the Knights as the ancient port-to-acropolis road is guidebook consensus, not a published excavation result; Greeka says only "town to the Acropolis".
- The Festus quadriga rite is a Roman report; no Rhodian inscription describes it, and Festus does not say the horses were white.
- Parker's CHS chapter (Lindos's twelve demes, the "triennial rule", the ktoinai as "mysterious", the Kameiran rites decree, the Argeia sub-tribe) could not be re-read at fact-check time (the CHS pages render only with JavaScript); those points rest on the researcher's first reading.
- The Migrants and Membership Regimes post (350 ethnics) is unreachable; its domain now serves spam.
- The ancient theatre where assembly and courts met has not been located.

# Jamie 12. Women's lives and kinship on Rhodes: stone daughters, serf brides, the embroidered bed, the daughter's house

## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes has an unusually good paper trail for women because three regimes in a row wrote them down: a Hellenistic city that carved wives, nurses and foreign women's names on grave altars by the hundred; a monastic-military Order whose Malta archive registers every manumitted slave girl, every serf's marriage licence and every widow's grain ration; and an Ottoman, Jewish and Greek island whose bridal beds, plate walls and inheritance customs survived into the age of the ethnographer. The thread that runs through all of it is property and kinship: who a woman belonged to, what she brought, what she could own, and who got the house. The walk passes the stones (stop 6), the Order's legal world (stops 7 to 9), the Juderia whose households the Knights licensed and the Germans destroyed (13 to 14), the hammam and the mosque that still marries Muslim couples (16), and ends with a birthday drink in a town where dowry has been illegal for forty years and the first-born daughter of Karpathos still gets her mother's house. Everything below is built on research 01, 04, 05, 06, 07, 11, 15, 16, 17 and 20 and the two verify-deltas files; corrections there override anything here.

## Tell at stop 6 (Hospital / Archaeological Museum): the women in stone

**Timarista and Krito, c. 420-410 BC.** The mother-daughter stele from the Kameiros cemetery (research 06, jamie-01): Timarista, dead, and her daughter Krito embracing, carved in the decade the three cities merged into one Rhodes. Read it as kinship on the last generation of an independent Kameiros: the household is a woman and her daughter, and the mourner is the child, not the husband.

**Kalliarista, before 350 BC.** The best women's text in the museum. Found at Kizil Tepe (modern Anatipsi) in the western necropolis: a seated woman on a diphros, her maid opposite holding the jewel casket. The epigram on the architrave (Commons description): *"If there is a highest praise in the world befitting a woman, with this died Kalliarista, daughter of Phileratos, for wisdom and virtue. For this reason her husband Damocles set up this stele as a memorial of love; and may a benevolent spirit follow him for the rest of his life."* Three names, three relationships: daughter of Phileratos (the natal house), wife of Damocles (the marital house), mistress of the unnamed maid (the slave household). That triangle is the Greek woman's whole legal universe.

**Foreign women honoured by clubs.** IG XII,1 127 (2nd century BC), on Harland's site: *"Eustrata, Methymnian (i.e. from Lesbos), and Ariadne, her cousin, have been honored ... on account of virtue, goodwill, and love of glory which they continue to have towards the association of Nikasioneian devotees of Asklepios and the Olympians ... also honored by the association of devotees of the Samothracian deities and Aphrodite."* Two immigrant women, kin to each other, patrons of two of the ~200 Rhodian private associations that jamie-01 describes as a shadow state. One detail is a gift to the walk: the stone was later cut down and reused for a Hebrew grave inscription (Harland, monument note), so a Hellenistic women's honour became a Jewish tombstone, probably in the same quarter Jamie will reach at stop 13. A second Harland stone: the grave of *"Philiskos, Antiochian ... honored by the devotees of Helios ... This is also the grave of his wife Pistis, Antiochian"*: a foreign couple buried together, the wife named with her own ethnic.

**The state as father: Diodorus 20.84.** During Demetrius's siege the assembly decreed that the fallen be buried publicly, that *"their parents and children should be maintained, receiving their support from the public treasury, that their unmarried daughters should be given dowries at the public cost, and that their sons on reaching manhood should be crowned in the theatre at the Dionysia and given a full suit of armour."* Jamie-01 has this; the women's angle is that a dowry was so essential to a girl's marriage that the polis undertook to pay it. Diodorus adds that the city *"sent forth"* everyone not useful for the defence, which in practice meant women, children and the old (the text does not say so explicitly; do not over-claim).

**Thesmophorios.** Iversen's reconstruction of the Rhodian calendar (jamie-04) makes the first month of the Rhodian year Thesmophorios, named for the women-only festival of Demeter. Wikipedia on the Thesmophoria: *"restricted to adult women, and the rites practiced during the festival were kept secret"*; three days, Anodos, Nesteia (fasting for Demeter's grief) and Kalligeneia ("beautiful birth"), with pig remains dug up and mixed into the seed corn. A city that names its New Year after a women's secret rite (adult women; at Athens specifically the citizen wives, elsewhere less certain) is telling you where it thinks the year begins.

**Nurses and the demotic (uncertain, verify).** Two claims often made about Rhodian epigraphy that I could not verify online this session: (1) that Rhodes has an exceptional number of epitaphs for wet-nurses (titthai/trophoi), many of them foreign women, discussed in P. M. Fraser, *Rhodian Funerary Monuments* (Oxford 1977); a Google Books text search for "nurse" and "τίτθη" returned no hits, which may only mean snippet-index gaps; (2) that Rhodian citizen women carry demotics in the feminine. Treat both as "check Fraser before scripting". What IS secure from the Copenhagen project (jamie-01) is ~350 foreigners' graves with ethnics, and Harland's mixed-origin clubs, so foreign women like Eustrata are not anomalies.

## Tell at stop 17 (Aphrodite temple) and the B-side of Lindos: goddesses' daughters

**The Danaids.** Herodotus 2.182 says the pharaoh Amasis dedicated to Athena at Lindos two stone statues and a linen corselet, and explains the gift by the tradition that the daughters of Danaus founded the temple when they fled the sons of Aegyptus (Perseus text; check exact Godley wording before quoting). The oldest sanctuary on the island was thus, in its own foundation story, built by fifty runaway brides refusing a marriage. The Lindos Chronicle of 99 BC (Wikipedia) then catalogued centuries of such gifts and *"four miracle stories about Athena"*; Higbie's 2003 edition is the reference. Higbie's list includes a dedication by Helen (bracelets, from memory; verify against Higbie B.15 or thereabouts).

**Aphrodite of Rhodes.** The crouching, hair-wringing Aphrodite in the museum (found 1923 in the Italian governor's garden; Greek Wikipedia) is the ancient image of the woman at her bath. Hold it in mind for the hammam at stop 16: the same gesture, fifteen centuries later, behind a dome.

## Tell at stops 7, 8, 9 (Street of the Knights, St John, Palace): the Order's rulebook for women

Everything in this section is from Nicholas Coureas, "Women and the Hospitaller Order on Rhodes and Cyprus in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries", *Ordines Militares* 27 (2022) 177-210, open access, unless noted. His summary: the women of Hospitaller Rhodes *"differed in terms of social class, some being slaves, others being serfs while others were free women, at times wealthy property owners"*; mostly Greek but also Syrian, Latin and Jewish; *"lay women but also women in religious orders, nuns or donors"*.

**Mixed marriage, in law.** The 14th-century *Capitula Rodi* ruled *"that the children of a free Latin man and a Greek woman were considered free"*, but Coureas notes it may have applied only to burgesses of the Town, and *"a marginal addition apparently ruled the children of Greek women under the obligation of servitudo marina would be liable to perform it and had to live in the borgo even if their father was a free Latin."* Serfdom passed through the mother: Michael Protoutamenou, a *francus*, married Kale tou Mangipa, a serf from Kos, and their son George *"had been born a serf like his mother"* (manumitted 1391, at the price of four Greek slaves).

**Mixed marriage, in life.** Richardon Boeuf of Provence, who *"arrived on Rhodes after travelling the world"* under Hélion de Villeneuve (1319-46), was posted as sergeant to the casale of Villanova and *"married Sophia, born a serf of the Hospital"*, with the Master's permission and a verbal promise that the children would be *franci* and baptised in the Roman rite. Because the promise was never written down, the children were treated as serfs until 15 February 1366, when five of them, including the daughters Joya and Margarita, petitioned and were freed. The love-match of 1449: Alexios of Nisyros, a *francomatus*, *"stricken with love"* for the serf Eirene of Zenodotou on Rhodes, against his parents' wishes; Grand Master Jean de Lastic freed her on 9 May 1449 and split the difference: sons free but owing naval service, *"the female children being serfs."* A bride buying herself out: on 16 October 1449 Kale, daughter of the priest Hieronymus of Kos, bought full emancipation and Roman citizenship for herself and all descendants by giving the Order slaves of both sexes *"of childbearing age"*.

**Slave women.** Maria of Bulgaria, manumitted 19 January 1404, *"became a Roman citizen"*; Helena of Hungary, *"who worked in the Hospitaller infirmary on Rhodes"* (the Order's earlier infirmary, the first Hospital of about 1360 on the Street of the Knights, not the 1440 building at stop 6), freed 22 December 1414 *"in recognition of her services to the sick"*; Russian and Circassian slave girls bought and resold in the 1440s-50s, one dispute over 56 ducats owed to a woman named Antonina; a December 1461 statute that captive women fleeing Cyprus *"should serve their masters for up to seven years, whereupon they should be freed."* Female slaves were *"assigned to domestic service"*, males to fields and crafts. Rhodes *"had its own slave market"*.

**Nuns, and the missing St Catherine nunnery.** The nunnery of St Catherine that the brief asks about is NOT attested in Coureas or in research 17; what exists is the St Catherine hospice of 1391-92 for distinguished visitors and the church of Agia Aikaterini/Ilk Mihrab (research 17). What is attested: the Hospitaller *soror* Margarita of Negroponte, who on 3 and 7 November 1347 gave her daughter Simona (a second document of 7 November calls the daughter Sophia) a windmill and its adjacent bath *"on a mound or hill in the borgo"* and two apartments *"in the castrum of Rhodes Town near the cathedral of St Mary"* (Our Lady of the Castle, a hundred metres from stop 6), but kept back *"the oven, which Margarita had had constructed to bake bread to be given to the poor as alms"*; John Geraki, licensed in 1440 to install in the church of the Holy Apostles in the borgo *"two nuns, one of whom could be his wife"*; the nun Kataphyge Alexaina, who built the Holy Trinity chapel at Iamatikon in 1407-8 and is painted there in her habit offering the church to Christ; and at Lindos in 1394-5 the priest Katsambas, *"his wife Kale and their children"* founding St George Pachymachiotes, Kale titled *"kyra and magistrissa."* A priest's wife with her own honorific, six centuries before the presvytera of stop 12.

**Widows and property.** Katherina tou Sinademi, widow, granted by document of 16 July 1517 *"one measure of wheat every month for herself and her seven children"* from the Order's allowances to seculars living in the castellum. Nicoletta de Leone, who brought the fief of Dyaskoros to her Florentine husband in 1374, outlived him and a Catalan second husband, and whose heiress Alamana, married to the Catalan burgess Bartolomeu Sunyer, fought Grand Master Naillac from 1408 over warehouses in the town, a 2,000-ducat marriage contract and a casale forced-sold for 2,100 florins, with the King of Aragon writing on her behalf. Rich women had property; they did not always keep it.

**The 1510 code and the town rules.** The *Pragmaticae Rhodiae* of February 1510: *"The third book deals with the issues of dowries and the provision and care of married women and regarding the approval of loans towards widows and unmarried women."* Penalties: a married man sleeping with another woman, 100 ducats and three months, *"and the woman was banished by way of penance to a cloister"*; *"Married men visiting brothels or consorting with women of ill repute had to pay a fine of 25 ducats or undergo corporal punishment"* (so brothels existed and were regulated; the brief's "brothel regulations" are attested this far and no further); bigamists paraded backwards on a donkey in a paper crown, then two years on the galleys; rape *"a capital offence"*, the rapist hanged; sex with an unmarried girl, even consensual, 100 ducats; abortion treated as murder. Movement: *"It was expressly forbidden for free women, their dependents and for serfs to depart from Rhodes"*; a captain who let a woman board without the Master's leave was punished with her. In May 1476 the Genoese customs-farmer Tobias Lomelino was arrested trying to leave *"along with his wife Maria and their two daughters"*, then let go. And the night rule: anyone abroad after dark *"always had to have hold a light."* Heslop's 2019 landscape study concludes that Latin-Greek relations in the countryside *"was on the whole amicable and mutually rewarding"*, which fits the licensed intermarriages above. For the 1248 Genoese who, per Akropolites, *"slept with the most beautiful local women, expelling the old and ugly ones from the city"*, see research 17.

## Tell at stops 13 and 14 (Kahal Shalom, Square of the Jewish Martyrs): the Jewish household, licensed and destroyed

**Under the Knights.** Coureas: on 11 April 1441 Antonio de Murro, *"a burgess and citizen of the Town of Rhodes, his Jewish wife Fustera and his mother-in-law Melcha"* were in court together: a Latin-Jewish marriage in the record. On 8 March 1453 *"a Jewish couple named Ezra and Rebecca, the son-in-law and daughter of the late Ezekiel Mauristeres"* refused a forced loan; Jean de Lastic exempted them from *"the customary tax payable by the Jewish community on Rhodes, amounting to 50 Rhodian florins per annum"* (their share four florins), placed their property under his protection, and granted them *"the right to circulate freely between the Upper and Lower Jewish quarters ... even during the hours of bellringing ... and to move around at night bearing a light so as to visit the baths, a pharmacist or a doctor."* One document gives you a two-quarter Jewish town, a curfew bell, a bath-house and a doctor. A traveller of 1480 *"notes that the Jewish women of the island were very skilled in working with silk"* (Wikipedia, History of the Jews in Rhodes; the traveller is probably Meshullam of Volterra, who passed through in 1481: verify).

**Under the Ottomans and Italians.** Research 11 has the 1523 resettlement of 150 Thessaloniki families, Ladino on Dossiadou and Simiou, ~4,000 Jews by 1930, Kahal Shalom of 1577 and the museum installed in 1997 in the former women's prayer rooms. Add: the Alliance Israélite school of 1902 was *"first with full curricula for girls"* (Guidi, via jamie-08). Emigration was a male chain: *"young men who traveled with the intention of making some money and then return"* who instead *"called for their family"*; the brothers Musa and Salmon Benatar were the first to leave for Rhodesia and the Belgian Congo, "as early as 1895" (Wikipedia, History of the Jews in Rhodes, not the Museum page); Joseph Capeloto left in 1907 aged 14, earned *"$8 per week and sent monies to his family that helped them immigrate to the U.S. in 1909"*; a Ladino postcard in solitreo hand reads *"Kon este vapor parto el 12 de Desember"* (Jewish Museum, Emigration page). The Museum's wedding photographs of 1912 (Atlanta), 1924 (Seattle) and 1934 (Jerusalem) are the diaspora's kinship map.

**July 1944.** The Museum's Holocaust page: 18 July men over 16 summoned; 19 July *"women and children ordered to appear with their valuables under a threat of death"*, belongings confiscated; 23 July 1,673 marched to the port onto three boats; ~151 survivors; 42 saved by consul Ülkümen, whose wife was gravely injured when the consulate was bombed (research 11). About 35-40 Jews live on the island today (Kahal Shalom / History of the Jews in Rhodes, Wikipedia).

## Tell at stop 16 (Roloi, Suleymaniye, library), with the Arionos Square detour: the Ottoman household

**Who lived where.** Under the Ottomans *"the Greeks were forced to abandon the fortified city and move to new suburbs outside its walls"* (Wikipedia, Rhodes city; research 11: the *marasia*); Muslims and Jews stayed inside; the enclosed wooden balconies hung over the lanes (research 11) are the architecture of a household that looks out without being looked at (interpretation, not a source claim).

**The hammam as women's institution.** The Yeni Hammam on Arionos Square is first named in a 1558 decree; *"first the men's section was constructed, then Sultan Mustafa III added the women's section"* (r. 1757-74; kulturenvanteri), on the west side, which *"altered the original building which was symmetrical in plan"* (imrtreasures). Note a source conflict: imrtreasures calls the 1558 firman *"of Sultan Selim, successor of Suleiman I"*, but Suleiman reigned until 1566. Its social use: *"a social nucleus for men and women, separately, or on designated days, to exchange news, prepare for weddings"*; visits marked *"bridal preparations, pre-holiday purification, even rites before death"* (grandmasterpalacerhodes.gr, 4 March 2025). Ezra and Rebecca's night-time right to *"visit the baths"* in 1453 shows the habit predates the Ottomans. Status: CONTESTED. grandmasterpalacerhodes.gr (4 March 2025) says "currently closed to bathing" and OSM tags it opening_hours=off, but kulturenvanteri calls it the only Ottoman bath in Rhodes "still operational today" and greeka says it "has been restored and is open to the public"; verify-deltas rates the closure UNVERIFIABLE. Do not tell Jamie it is closed; say it has been under restoration and check the door.

**Marriage today, Muslim.** The Mustafa Pasha mosque beside the hammam has been the Muslim community's wedding office since 1977 (research 11). And jamie-04 has the Pasha's wife at Tsambika: an Ottoman Muslim woman climbing to a Christian icon for a child, the fertility vow crossing the confessional line.

## Tell at stop 15 (lunch) or stop 21 (the drink): dowry, the embroidered bed, and who gets the house

**Sperveri.** The Rhodian bridal bed-tent: a cylinder of linen panels, embroidered in silk, hung from the ceiling over the raised bed. The V&A holds a *"Bed tent"* from Rhodes, 1700s (MISC.10-1921, given by the 1851 Commissioners), a *"Bed tent door"* from Rhodes, 1700s (T.660-1950) and a bed-tent curtain fragment (T.544-1950), the last two *"Given by Professor R M Dawkins"*, the Oxford Byzantinist who collected Dodecanese folktales; the Benaki shows a Rhodes sperveri of the 17th-18th century and a Patmos one; the Museum of Modern Greek Culture in Athens displays a Rhodian room with *"plates decorating the platewalls of Rhodian houses 16-20th centuries, and sperveri an all embroidered curtain consisting of many stitched oblique sheets of fabric 18th century Rhodes."* The word is also a dance: *"Sperveri is a dance done at the wedding on the island of Rhodes. It is sung and danced as part of the dressing of the bridal bed ... It is a women's dance"*, to the song *"έστολισαν το σπερβέρι"*, "they have adorned the sperveri" (Wikipedia; a stub flagged "needs more citations", its only source a 2008 Greek Folk Dancers of NSW course booklet; no second source found, so treat the dance as UNVERIFIED and say "a wedding song and dance is said to accompany the dressing of the bed"). If it is real, the dowry textile had its own ritual: women dressing the bed and singing over it before the wedding. The plate wall (Rhodian and imported dishes hung in rows) was the other display of a household's wealth.

**Embroidery as dowry.** TRC Leiden: on Karpathos *"the first born girl (kanakara) would inherit many pieces of fine embroidery from her mother, some of them to be included in her dowry, while her younger sisters had to do with lesser pieces."* The Benaki's 18th-century Karpathos woman's tunic with raised embroidery is the kind of object meant. Research 07 adds the bride's basket of moschopougia carried to her mother-in-law at engagement.

**Kanakara.** The Dodecanese first-born system, best documented on Karpathos. Vernier (1989, Persée): *"Les biens se transmettaient de façon bilinéaire. Dans chaque famille, la première-née des filles héritait des biens de sa mère et le premier-né des garçons des biens de son père"*; the first-born took the names of the maternal grandmother and paternal grandfather, becoming *"the official representatives of the maternal and paternal lineages."* A regional tourism page (aegeanislands.gr) gives the local vocabulary: the *kanakaris* took *"his father's assets that his father had inherited from his grandfather"*, the *kanakara* *"her mother's assets, namely the house, land, furniture, clothes, some money and objects of value"*; younger siblings *"were either forced to migrate or work at the fields of the firstborns as servants"*; Church and Ottoman authority both disliked it; the same page says it faded after 1920 and was formally abolished when the Greek Civil Code was applied after the 1947 liberation of the Dodecanese (these two dates rest on that tourism page alone and were not corroborated elsewhere; Vernier confirms the system but not the dates). Because the house is mother's property, it goes daughter to daughter: matrilineal houses inside a patriarchal society. **Whether Rhodes had a formal kanakara rule I could not confirm**; the safe statement is that across the Dodecanese and Aegean the house was normally part of the daughter's dowry, so town and village houses tended to pass through women, and that Karpathos is the extreme case. Ask a Lindian whose house it was.

**The Orthodox wedding grammar.** The *koumbaros/koumbara* is the sponsor who crowns the couple: *"the placement of crowns on both heads of the couple ... The couple will exchange the crowns three times ... the couple will drink from a common cup, three times each"* (Wikipedia, Marriage in the Eastern Orthodox Church). The sponsor becomes spiritual kin, usually baptises the first child, and spiritual kin cannot marry each other (general Orthodox canon; not verified for Rhodes specifically). The bride's chest (kasela) holding the trousseau is the third object of the set with the sperveri and the plate wall; I found no Rhodes-specific museum record for a chest this session.

**Costume.** For a verifiable object use the Benaki's Karpathos tunic and its Astypalaia bridal dress (Commons files). The Lindos bridal costume is famous but I found no museum record online this session; do not describe it in detail without one. Olympos women still wear *"traditional multicolored outfits with two lines of gold coins over their chests and a headscarf"* (aegeanislands.gr): the dowry worn on the body.

## Tell at stop 18 (Liberty Gate) or stop 4 (Castellania): the twentieth century

Schooling for girls arrived through the communities before the state: the Alliance school of 1902 (girls' full curriculum), the Italian state schools of 1937 (jamie-08). Women's vote: *"In 1952, Law 2159 was enacted, allowing women the right to vote"* (Feminism in Greece, Wikipedia). Emigration after 1947 ran in village chains; Claude Hedrick's study of Lahania, a village in the south of Rhodes, and its migration to Thebarton, Adelaide (Flinders, Greek Research in Australia; academia.edu listing) is a pointer to follow, not a figure to quote. Then the law that ended the world of the sperveri: *"In 1983, a new family law was passed, which provided for gender equality in marriage, and abolished dowry and provided for equal rights for 'illegitimate' children"*; it *"provided for civil marriage and liberalised the divorce law"*; *"Adultery was also decriminalised in 1983"* (Women in Greece / Feminism in Greece, Wikipedia; Law 1329/1983). The reform also dismantled the rules that made the husband head of household and obliged women to take his surname (Feminism in Greece, Wikipedia; a Greek wife now keeps her own name). Law 3719/2008 cut the separation period for divorce from four years to two.

## Tell at stop 12 (Koskinou Gate, Agios Fanourios detour) and stop 21: women today

The fanouropita is the visible women's rite on the walk: on 27 August *"mostly women are seen entering the place of worship with trays of cakes, sometimes with candles, and holding small pieces of papers"* with the names of the beloved (To Vima); nine ingredients for *"the nine days of the saint's suffering"*, shared with at least nine people; research 17 and jamie-04 have the recipe and the forgiveness of the saint's mother. The presvytera (papadia), the priest's wife, is *"addressed as presbytera, the other half of the presbyteros"* (Ecumenical Patriarch, via Wikipedia): Kale the magistrissa of 1394 is her ancestor. The University of the Aegean's School of Humanities sits in Rhodes with Primary Education (1986), Preschool Education Sciences and Educational Design (1987) and Mediterranean Studies (1999) (Wikipedia); teacher training is overwhelmingly female in Greece, which makes Rhodes a university town of young women (inference; no Rhodes-specific figure found). Nationally the female employment rate was 47.3% in 2019 (Women in Greece, Wikipedia); on an island of 125,113 people (2022; Wikipedia) whose economy is tourism, that work is seasonal hotel, restaurant and shop labour, much of it women's (no island-level statistic found this session).

## Continuities and contrasts

- **What a woman is called.** Kalliarista is "daughter of Phileratos, wife of Damocles"; Sophia is "born a serf of the Hospital"; Rebecca is "daughter of the late Ezekiel"; the kanakara carries her grandmother's name; the Greek woman since 1983 keeps her own surname. Every regime names her by the man or house she belongs to; the modern law is the first to let her name herself.
- **Dowry.** The polis paid it for the war dead's daughters (305 BC); the Order codified it (Pragmaticae 1510, book 3); the sperveri and the plate wall displayed it; the 1983 law abolished it. An ancient Rhodian, a Hospitaller notary and an Ottoman-era grandmother would all understand the sperveri instantly; a Rhodian bride today would need it explained.
- **The house through the mother.** Serfdom followed the mother's status in 1391 and 1449; the kanakara's house followed the mother's line on Karpathos; the maid on Kalliarista's stele belonged to the mistress. Matrilineal transmission inside patriarchal law is the oldest Rhodian constant.
- **Mixed households.** Eustrata of Lesbos in a Rhodian club; Richardon of Provence and Sophia the serf; Antonio de Murro and Fustera the Jewess; Lago's encouraged intermarriage (research 11). The ancient Rhodian would recognise the foreigner-wife and the club that honours her; the Hospitaller would recognise the licence and the argument over the children's rite; the Ottoman householder would recognise the confessional line and its crossings (the Pasha's wife at Tsambika); the resident today recognises all of it in the tourist-season marriages that produce the island's new mixed families.
- **The bath.** Aphrodite crouching (stop 6), Ezra and Rebecca's night pass to the baths (1453), the women's wing of the Yeni Hammam (1760s), bridal preparations there until its recent restoration closure (whether it has reopened is contested; see stop 16). Same building type, same women's day, four regimes.
- **Bread for the poor.** Margarita of Negroponte's alms oven near the cathedral (1347), the widow's monthly wheat (1517), the fanouropita cut into forty pieces (today): women's charity is delivered as baked goods across seven centuries.
- **Movement.** Women could not leave Rhodes without the Master's permission (1510); the Jewish women were ordered to the port with their valuables (1944); the daughters of Lahania left for Adelaide (1950s); the young women of the university and the hotels arrive each September. The gate at stop 18 is the hinge.

## Uncertainties to flag

- Fraser's nurse epitaphs and feminine demotics: unverified this session.
- Helen's bracelets in the Lindos Chronicle: from memory; check Higbie 2003.
- Herodotus 2.182 wording: cite, but quote only after checking Godley/Macaulay.
- Kanakara on Rhodes proper: not confirmed; Karpathos is documented.
- The 1558 firman's sultan: kulturenvanteri gives no name; imrtreasures says Selim, which conflicts with Suleiman's dates. (Greeka, for its part, says the women's section was added "under the rule of Mustafa Pasha", not Mustafa III; kulturenvanteri is the more specific source.)
- Yeni Hammam open or closed: sources conflict (closed per grandmasterpalacerhodes.gr March 2025; open per kulturenvanteri and greeka); verify on the day.
- Sperveri as a dance: only a citation-needed Wikipedia stub; the object is secure, the dance is not.
- Kanakara decline after 1920 / abolition after 1947: single tourism-page source.
- Lindos bridal costume and the Rhodian trousseau chest: no museum record found; describe generically.
- The 1480/1481 silk-working traveller's identity (Meshullam of Volterra): plausible, unverified.
- "St Catherine nunnery": not attested; only the hospice and the church.

# Jamie 16. The swallow-song of Lindos and the year outside the church: Rhodian folk rites from Cleobulus to the kallikantzaroi

## Frame for the scriptwriter

jamie-04 and jamie-09 give the Church's calendar (Easter fires, the fifteenth of August, Tsambika, Fanourios, name days, the evil eye, tamata). This file is the *other* year: the one that runs in the courtyard, the lane and the harbour rather than under the church roof, and that the Church has tolerated, absorbed or (in the case of the Klidonas) formally condemned since the seventh century. Its spine is the one ancient Rhodian rite with a documented modern descendant: the swallow-song that Athenaeus preserved from a lost Rhodian book on sacrifices, attributed to Cleobulus of Lindos "when there was need of money," and the wooden swallow Greek schoolchildren still carry from door to door on the first of March. Lawson, Politis and Megas set the two side by side; the file presents that as scholarly attestation, not proven descent. Around it sits the rest of the folk year: the kallikantzaroi of the Twelve Days (*kantharoi* on Rhodes), the March thread, the May wreath burned at the St John's fires, the Klidonas water-divination, Lazarus songs, carnival, the wine harvest, and the sea rites of St Nicholas and Epiphany, which Rhodes town keeps on a platform behind the cathedral at Mandraki. Every custom below is tagged: **[Rhodes]** documented for the island, **[Dodecanese]** for the island group, **[Greece]** general Greek only, and **[unverified]** where the web budget ran out before the claim could be checked. For Jamie the thread is: who does the rite (children, unmarried girls, sailors, householders), what it demands of the neighbours (food, coins, hospitality, silence), and how a community keeps a second calendar running beside the official one for twenty-six centuries.

## Tell at stop 1 (cruise berth): the swallow is come

**What she can see.** The commercial harbour, the walls, and (if the season is kind) swallows over the water. The date matters: the thirtieth of September. Athenian Boedromion ran from about mid-September to mid-October, and that is the month Athenaeus names for the swallow-song; but the Rhodian month of the same name (Badromios) seems to have fallen in early spring, when swallows actually arrive, as the Wikipedia article notes; the Rhodian month list (Thesmophorios first, then Sminthios, Diosthyos, Agrianios, Badromios) puts it fifth in a year that began in autumn, which fits, though Wikipedia warns the order is not settled. Honest version for the clip: "the text says Boedromion, which in Athens was this month; on Rhodes it was probably spring." **[Rhodes; calendar placement is inference, [unverified] in detail]**

**The text.** Athenaeus, *Deipnosophistae* 8.360b-d, cites Theognis (of Rhodes), *On the Sacrifices in Rhodes*, book two (Gulick's Loeb, re-read on the Thayer site on this pass, renders the title "Theognis speaks in the second book of his *Rhodian Festivals*"), for the custom the Rhodians call *chelidonizein*, "swallow-singing": a door-to-door collection (*agermos*) by children in the month Boedromion. The song, in Pär Sandin's rendering quoted on Wikipedia, opens "Came, came the swallow / with pleasant seasons, / with the beautiful year..." and ends "now open, open the door for the swallow, / we are not old men, but only children." Between those lines, in Gulick's wording, "her breast is white, her back is black"; the children tell the rich house to "trundle out some pressed fruit from your rich store, a cup of wine, a tray of cheeses," adding that "a wheat-cake, too, and pulse-bread" the swallow does not refuse; they ask whether they should go away or get something, and threaten, if refused, "we'll carry off your front door, or the lintel over it, or the good wife sitting within. She's a little thing, we can easily lift her," closing "indeed we are not old men, but little boys." Athenaeus then says: "This mode of collection was instituted first by Cleobulus of Lindus, when the need of collecting money once arose in Lindus." Only the words survive; the tune is lost. **[Rhodes; primary source, Gulick's translation verified]**

**Why it matters to Jamie.** A welfare mechanism disguised as a game: children licensed once a year to demand food from the well-off with a comic threat; the rich house seen giving; the poor children fed. That a ruler "instituted" it during a shortage reads like a city-state deciding redistribution works better as a festival than as a tax. Compare Strabo on Rhodes feeding its poor so the fleet never lacked crews (research/04). **[interpretation]**

**The modern descendant.** On the first of March, across Greece, children of about seven to twelve carry a carved wooden swallow, often mounted in a wooden "cage" with a cord that makes it spin, decorated with ivy, flowers and red-and-white threads, from house to house; they sing a song that (in the Serres version on Greek Wikipedia) begins "A swallow arrives from the black sea, / she sits and speaks and sings beautifully," asks for "five eggs," and gets eggs or coins, while the children give the red-and-white *martes* threads from the cage to the girls of the house. Greek Wikipedia calls the custom pan-Hellenic ("το έθιμο είναι πανελλήνιο") and cites Athenaeus for the ancient swallow-songs beginning "Ήλθε, ήλθε η χελιδών" (it does not itself say "Rhodian" or name Cleobulus; that link is made by the English article and by Lawson); it cites Nikolaos Politis for the thread-tying and its Eleusinian antecedent, not for the swallow; Lawson's *Modern Greek Folklore and Ancient Greek Religion* (1910) and Megas's *Greek Calendar Customs* (1958, English 1963) both treat the first-of-March songs as the descendant of the ancient *chelidonisma*. **[Greece; scholarly attestation of continuity]** Whether it is or was practised on Rhodes itself I could not verify; the best-documented regions are Thrace, Macedonia and Pontic communities, so say "children somewhere in Greece this coming March." **[unverified for Rhodes]**

**Hand-on.** The next stop is the gate: the threshold the ancient children sang at, in a walled town that has been opening and shutting its doors to strangers ever since.

## Tell at stop 3 (the roofless church): the Twelve Days and the year outside the roof

**What she can see.** A church with no roof, open to the sky: the perfect place to talk about what happens *outside* the church's year.

**The Twelve Days.** From Christmas to Epiphany (twenty-fifth of December to the sixth of January), Greek belief holds that "the waters are unbaptised" and the kallikantzaroi come up from underground, where they spend the rest of the year sawing at the tree that holds up the world; they come down chimneys, foul the food and wine, eat the Christmas pork, and are kept out by a fire that never goes out, a black cross on the door, a colander on the step (they must count its holes and cannot say "three"), and old shoes burned for the stink. On the sixth of January the priest's holy water sends them back down. Greek Wikipedia preserves the chant they are supposed to cry as he approaches: "Run, let us run, the mad priest is coming with his sprinkler." **[Greece; en and el Wikipedia, citing Politis, Lawson, Megas]** The name changes from island to island (Chios *kaides*, Samos *kaides* and *kalispoudides*, Karpathos in the Dodecanese *kaides* and *kaimpilides*, Epirus *kaliontzides*, Cyprus *skalapountaroi*, per Greek Wikipedia); on Rhodes the creature is called the *kantharos*, according to Koumanoudi 2017 (research/20; not found on the web on this pass, so keep it as "one Rhodian folklorist says"). Greek Wikipedia does record Korais's old guess that the very name kallikantzaros is *kalos* plus *kantharos*, "fine beetle," which at least shows the word was in the family. **[Rhodes; secondary, via research/20; the Korais etymology is el.wikipedia]**

**The other underground.** Biliotti (1881, quoted in research/20) records the Rhodian revenant, the *katachanas*, and the exorcism in which the priest walks the grave held by two men ready to pull him back if the dead man grabs his feet. Two things come up from below in winter: the *kantharoi* who make mischief, and the dead who do not stay put. **[Rhodes; Biliotti via research/20]**

## Tell at stop 10 (the faction map: the Order's langues): a Hospitaller's Christmas, and the Church's verdict on the folk year

**What she can see.** The auberges of the langues. A French, Aragonese, English or German brother kept the Latin calendar (Christmas, Epiphany, Corpus Christi, the Order's St John the Baptist on the twenty-fourth of June) inside the Collachio, while the Greek town kept the Orthodox year and, beside it, the folk one. Hasluck's explanation of the Malpasso dragon (research/04) is itself a calendar story: the Rogation processions of France carried a model dragon to be "slain," and a parade became a biography. **[Rhodes; Hasluck via research/04]**

**The Church's verdict.** The one rite the Church actually tried to stamp out is the Klidonas. Canon sixty-five of the Council in Trullo (Constantinople, six ninety-one to six ninety-two) orders "the fires which are lighted on the new moons by some before their shops and houses, upon which (according to a certain ancient custom) they are wont foolishly and crazily to leap" to cease, on pain of excommunication for laymen and deposition for clergy, and cites King Manasses who "used divination"; Greek Wikipedia's Klidonas article cites the canon, with Koukoules, Loukatos and Varvounis as the modern scholars. The rite survived by attaching itself to St John's Eve, whose feast day the Knights took as their patronal festival. Two calendars, one bonfire. **[Greece; canon law is primary]**

## Tell at stop 11 (La Juderia, and the marasia beyond the walls): the year of the Greek suburbs

**What she can see.** The Jewish quarter inside; beyond the walls the nine *marasia*, the Greek suburbs to which the Ottomans moved the Christian population after fifteen twenty-two (research/jamie-09). That is where the Greek folk year lived for four centuries: not in the Old Town, whose churches were mosques, but in the courtyards of the suburbs.

**The March thread (martis).** On the first of March a red-and-white twisted thread is tied on a child's wrist so the spring sun will not burn or darken them (and, Greek Wikipedia adds, against the evil eye); it is taken off at the end of the month or when the first swallow is seen, and left on a rose bush for the birds to build their nests with. The ancient antecedent usually offered is the *kroke*, the thread the young initiates at Eleusis wore on the right hand and foot. **[Greece; el.wikipedia Μάρτης (έθιμο); en.wikipedia Martenitsa. Neither source names Politis for this, and neither gives Easter or the Easter fire as the moment of removal: do not say so in the clip]** It ties the swallow-song to the swallow-bracelet: the same day, the same bird, the same children. **[Rhodes practice unverified; Greece general]**

**May Day and the St John fires (Klidonas).** On the first of May a wreath of flowers and wheat is hung on the house door; on the twenty-third of June, St John's Eve, the dried wreaths are burned in street bonfires and people jump the flames. **[Greece; Megas; the wreath-burning is general knowledge, [unverified] on this pass]** The same evening a child whose parents are both living (a boy or a girl, Greek Wikipedia specifies, not the unmarried girls themselves) fetches "silent water" (*amilito nero*) from a spring or well without speaking, whatever teasing is thrown at it; the unmarried girls each drop a token (ring, thimble, pin, button, a marked apple called a *rizikari*) into the pot, which is locked, crowned with laurel and myrtle and covered with a red cloth, and after sunset the girls carry it outdoors "to be starred" (*astronomistei*) overnight; before dawn a child draws the tokens out while a woman, the *rizikarissa*, gives the opening words and an improvised couplet, tender or satirical, is recited for each owner's future; the silence is explained by Zacharias, John the Baptist's father, struck dumb. *Kledon*, "omen, chance utterance," is in Homer, Aeschylus and Herodotus (Greek Wikipedia's own list, on this reading, gives Herodotus, Aeschylus and Pausanias). **[Greece; el.wikipedia Klidonas, citing Koukoules, Loukatos, Varvounis]** Rhodian villages stage Klidonas evenings today as municipal folk events (I recall Koskinou and Kremasti among them) **[unverified]**.

**Lazarus Saturday and carnival.** On the Saturday before Palm Sunday women bake *lazarakia*, spiced Lenten breads "shaped like a man wrapped in a shroud, with cloves for eyes," one kept all year and then eaten or thrown in the sea; across Greece children sing the Lazarus songs door to door for eggs. **[Greece; en.wikipedia Lazarakia; the songs are Megas, [unverified] on this pass]** Apokries, the carnival weeks before Lent, bring a parade to Rhodes town and masked house-visits to villages **[unverified for Rhodes specifics]**.

**The wine harvest.** jamie-09 places the *souma* stills of Embonas in late September: the first-fruits moment of the folk year, distilled grape-skins, tasted at the still. **[Rhodes; via jamie-09]** The ancient equivalent, an *aparche* of the vintage to Dionysus, is attested for Rhodes only generally through Rhodian wine-amphora stamps and the god's cult; do not claim a specific Rhodian rite. **[Rhodes; general only]**

## Tell at stop 17 (Fort St Nicholas): the sailors' saint, the sixth of December

**What she can see.** The fort at the end of the mole, built fourteen sixty-four to sixty-seven under Grand Master Zacosta, and said (research/jamie-04, which gives no source; Wikipedia's Fortifications of Rhodes does not mention it) to stand where a chapel of St Nicholas stood before **[chapel unverified]**. St Nicholas of Myra, on the sixth of December, is the patron of Greek sailors and of the Hellenic Navy, whose ships keep his feast; fishermen bring the icon a votive boat. **[Greece; general knowledge, [unverified] on this pass]** Whether a liturgy is held inside the fort on the day I could not verify **[unverified]**. The point for Jamie: the fort is a military building named for a welfare institution.

## Tell at stop 19 (the deer columns and the return by sea): Epiphany at Mandraki, and the beings in the water

**What she can see.** The Evangelismos cathedral on the Mandraki waterfront, and the harbour water beside it.

**Epiphany, sixth of January.** ERT's regional news for Rhodes reports that in twenty twenty-five the Great Blessing of the Waters was held "on the platform set up at Mandraki, behind the Holy Church of the Annunciation," presided over by Metropolitan Kyrillos of Rhodes; divers went out on a Coast Guard vessel in "considerable winds," and a young man named Michalis Mavridis came up with the cross. The twenty twenty-six programme, also on ERT: matins and liturgy in the Evangelismos, the Great Blessing inside the church at about a quarter past ten, and shortly before eleven a procession, with a detachment of the ninety-fifth army unit and the Municipal Philharmonic, to "the predetermined pier" for the immersion of the Cross; the same day the rite is repeated at the tourist harbour and in villages across the island. **[Rhodes; ERT 2025 and 2026]** jamie-04 already sets this beside the Halieia chariot thrown into the sea; here add what the folk year adds: the holy water of the sixth of January is also what sends the *kantharoi* underground, and in villages the priest goes house to house with the sprinkler for exactly that reason. **[Greece; el.wikipedia Kallikantzaros]**

**Nereids and the sea.** Lawson (1910) gives a chapter to the modern *neraides*, the beautiful and dangerous women of springs, shore and noon-day who inherit the ancient nymphs; a man who sees them dancing may be struck dumb or "taken." Pan-Greek; no Rhodes-specific text found on this pass **[Greece; Lawson]**. The island's own sea-woman is older: Diodorus's Halia, who threw herself into the sea and became Leucothea (research/04). **[Rhodes; Diodorus 5.55]**

## B-side: the dry moat (stop 20): what lives underneath

The thirty-metre ditch is the island's most literal underground. Tell the *kantharoi* sawing at the world-tree; the *katachanas* held in his grave by a priest with two men on his belt (research/20); and the dragon of Malpasso, whose "head" over a gate turned out to be a crocodile (research/04). The nineteen fifty volume edits the manuscript tales gathered by the Kos schoolmaster Jacob Zarraftis, chiefly from Kos, Astypalaia and the small islands rather than from Rhodes; I could not confirm on this pass that any tale in it is set on Rhodes **[Dodecanese; the Zarraftis provenance is [unverified] on this pass]**.

## B-side: Monte Smith, the hill of the Sun (stop 22): Cleobulus, Cleobulina, and a riddle for the road

Cleobulus of Lindos, around six hundred before Christ, son of Euagoras, was one of the Seven Sages and author of the maxim "moderation is best" (*metron ariston*); Diogenes Laertius (1.89 to 93) says he rebuilt the temple of Athena at Lindos "founded by Danaus" and lived to about seventy, but gives him no title; that he governed Lindos rests on Plutarch's *Dinner*, where Thales says his daughter has made "his government of the citizens" milder, and on the English Wikipedia's "lawgiver." Say "who governed Lindos," not "tyrant" or "king." **[Rhodes; Diog. Laert.; Plutarch 148D-E]** A round Hellenistic tomb on the headland north of Lindos is called his by tradition, without evidence **[unverified]**. His daughter Cleobulina, also called Eumetis ("clever"), is the one Rhodian woman with a literary reputation from antiquity: a composer of riddles in verse, three of which survive; Plutarch's *Dinner of the Seven Wise Men* (148C-E) has her parting the guest Anacharsis's hair with her hands, and Thales says "she has influence with her father so that his government of the citizens has become milder and more popular"; at 153B she poses the riddle "Sooth I have seen a man with fire fasten bronze on another," which Aesop explains as the cupping-glass a doctor heats and presses on the skin. Aristotle quotes the same line in *Rhetoric* 3.2 as "the famous enigma" and a model of good metaphor, without naming her. Her existence has been doubted; the riddles exist. **[Rhodes; en.wikipedia Cleobulina; Plutarch, Dinner (Loeb, Thayer); Aristotle Rhetoric 3.2.12 (Perseus)]** A father who makes the children sing for cheese; a daughter who makes the wise men guess.

## Continuities and contrasts

- **A Lindian child, sixth century before Christ.** Goes round the doors in Badromios with the other children, sings that the swallow is come, threatens to carry off the little wife, and gets pressed fruit, cheese and a cup of wine from the rich house. The custom is said to have been set up by the ruler so the poor would eat. The collection is public, licensed and comic.
- **A Hospitaller brother, fifteenth century.** Keeps the Latin Christmas and Epiphany in the Collachio, marches with a model dragon at Rogationtide (the seed of the Gozon legend), and keeps his Order's patron, John the Baptist, on the twenty-fourth of June, the night the Greek girls outside send a child for silent water, for a rite his Church condemned in six ninety-one.
- **An Ottoman-era Greek in the marasia, eighteenth century.** Ties the March thread on the children, hangs the May wreath, burns it at the St John fire and jumps the flames, watches his daughter's ring come out of the Klidonas pot with a couplet, keeps the fire in all Twelve Days against the *kantharoi*, and calls the priest with the sprinkler on the sixth of January. The market inside the walls keeps three calendars; the courtyard keeps a fourth.
- **A Rhodian child today.** Might tie the March thread and take it off at the first swallow; sees the Cross go into Mandraki on the sixth of January behind the cathedral, with the army band; may or may not carry a wooden swallow on the first of March (unconfirmed for the island). The ruler's dole is now a school project, and the redistribution is eggs.

What persists: children as the licensed beggars of spring; the sea as where the community puts its most precious thing (chariot, Cross) once a year; the underground as the source of midwinter disorder; and the rule that a rite the Church condemned (Klidonas) survives by taking a saint's name, while one it never condemned (the swallow) survives by being harmless.

# The wizards' college of Rhodes: Hipparchus, Posidonius, the Antikythera hypothesis, Apollonius Rhodius, the rhetoric school, the Laocoon sculptors and the gymnasium library, told on Monte Smith and in the Old Town

## The wizards' college of Rhodes: archmages and artificers on Monte Smith

**Framing (say at the foot of Diagoridon street, or on the Temple of Apollo terrace).** The acropolis of Rhodes on Monte Smith was never a fortress. Wikipedia's summary of the excavators is exact: a "monumental zone with sacred places, large temples, public buildings and underground worship spaces", built "in tiers on different levels, above shaped platforms sustained by strong supporting walls". Read it as a campus. Between about 150 BC and 50 BC it held, at once or in quick succession, the greatest observing astronomer of antiquity, the Stoic school that "eclipsed Athens", the rhetoric school where Cicero and Caesar were trained, and the marble workshops whose signatures are on the Laocoön. The Telchines, the island's mythical sorcerer-smiths, are already told at the Temple of Aphrodite in Symi Square (research topic 04); here we deal with the historical wizards. Practical note: the hill is an open, free archaeological park with no gate or published hours; the Stadium and Odeion are occasionally fenced for events, unverified for 30 Sep 2026.

**Stop 1: the Stadium as meridian line (36.43893, 28.21197).** The 3rd-century-BC stadium is 210 m long and runs north-south, which is to say along the meridian, and that is the line on which Hipparchus of Nicaea did his work. Ptolemy attributes to him observations "in the period from 147 to 127 BC, and some of these are stated as made in Rhodes", made "on a transit instrument set in the meridian"; one equinox timing "differs by 5 hours from the observation made on Alexandria's large public equatorial ring that same day". He "is believed to have died on the island of Rhodes, where he seems to have spent most of his later life". In 127 BC, comparing Spica and Regulus with the century-old data of Timocharis, he found "Spica had moved 2 degrees relative to the autumnal equinox" and announced precession, the slow wobble of the whole sky. Pliny (NH 2.95, Rackham) tells why he built the first star catalogue: Hipparchus "detected a new star that came into existence during his lifetime; the movement of this star in its line of radiance led him to wonder... whether the stars that we think to be fixed are also in motion; and consequently he did a bold thing, that would be reprehensible even for God: he dared to schedule the stars for posterity, and tick off the heavenly bodies by name in a list, devising machinery by means of which to indicate their several positions and magnitudes." About 850 stars, c. 135-129 BC. His instrument was the dioptra, "a four-foot rod with a scale, a sighting hole at one end, and a wedge", and probably the armillary sphere. D&D reading: a level-20 diviner who catalogued the heavens so the next age could tell if a god had moved a star.

**Stop 2: the gymnasium terrace and library (36.44008, 28.21397; OSM's 'Library' polygon at 36.44034, 28.21172).** The gymnasium is the college building proper, and it had a library: an inscribed catalogue found on Rhodes and published by Mario Segre (Rivista di Filologia 1935-36) lists works by author, including Theophrastus and the historian-orator Hegesias; Wikipedia's list of ancient libraries says the Rhodian catalogue "classified titles by subject, displayed an inventory of authors in consecutive order together with their published efforts" and that the library "employed a qualified librarian", dating it near 100 AD. Which museum holds the stone and whether it is displayed I could not verify (see open questions). The Stoic line belongs here too: Strabo (14.2.13) lists "Panaetius the philosopher" first among native Rhodians, "and Stratocles and Andronicus, one of the Peripatetics, and Leonides the Stoic; and also, before their time, Praxiphanes and Hieronymus and Eudemus." Panaetius (c. 185-110 BC) was head of the Stoa in Athens; the story that Lindos made him priest of Poseidon Hippios "was actually an honour bestowed upon his grandfather", so "Panaetius of Lindos" is a conflation. "His chief pupil in philosophy was Posidonius."

**Stop 3: the Odeion, the bards' college (36.44011, 28.21174).** The small marble odeion (c. 800 seats, heavily rebuilt) is the place for the rhetoric school. Ancient sources on Aeschines are thin but real: exiled after losing to Demosthenes in 330 BC, he "opened a school of rhetoric" on Rhodes; Quintilian 12.10.19 says the Rhodian style, midway between Attic and Asiatic, exists because "Aeschines, who had chosen this place for his exile, brought there the studies of Athens, which, like seeds degenerating in a foreign sky and soil, mixed that Attic flavour with a foreign one." Cicero (De Oratore 3.213) has him read the Rhodians his own speech against Ctesiphon and, next day, Demosthenes' reply "in a most sweet and powerful voice", then say to the applauding crowd: "quanto magis miraremini, si audissetis ipsum!" - "how much more would you marvel if you had heard him himself!" (Pliny 7.110 repeats it). No ancient text puts the school at Rodini; that is later tradition. Two centuries on, Apollonius Molon of Alabanda ran the school. Cicero, in Brutus 316: "not content with these [Asian teachers], I came to Rhodes and attached myself to the same Molon I had heard at Rome... he made it his business to check my youthful excess and overflow and to keep me, as it were, within the banks." Plutarch adds that Molon, "not understanding the Roman language, requested Cicero to declaim in Greek", and that in Rhodes Cicero studied "oratory with Apollonius the son of Molon, and philosophy with Poseidonius". Suetonius (Julius 4): Caesar "determined to withdraw to Rhodes... to study under Apollonius Molo, the most eminent teacher of oratory of that time. While crossing to Rhodes... he was taken by pirates near the island of Pharmacussa and remained in their custody for nearly forty days." Molon's teaching, per tradition: the three things that matter are "Delivery, Delivery and Delivery."

**Stop 4: the Temple of Apollo terrace, the epic poet (36.43993, 28.21046).** The Argonautica opens "Beginning with thee, O Phoebus", so tell Apollonius here. The ancient Lives say his first Alexandrian edition failed and that he "redrafted the Argonautica in such fine style at Rhodes that he was able to return to Alexandria in triumph"; Strabo says he and Dionysius Thrax, "though Alexandrians, were called Rhodians". Caveat, honestly stated: the stories "were probably invented to account for the existence of a second edition", and the epithet "might simply reflect the fact that he once wrote a poem about Rhodes". Then the monsters, in Seaton's 1912 translation. The Clashing Rocks: "Euphemus sent forth the dove... she flew between them, and the rocks again rushed together and crashed as they met face to face. And the foam leapt up in a mass like a cloud... the rocks shore away the end of the dove's tail-feathers; but away she flew unscathed... Then Athena with her left hand thrust back one mighty rock and with her right pushed the ship through" (2.549-610). The dragon of Colchis: "the serpent with his keen sleepless eyes saw them coming, and stretched out his long neck and hissed in awful wise... through fear young mothers awoke, and round their new-born babes... threw their hands in agony, for the small limbs started at that hiss"; Medea "with a newly cut spray of juniper, dipping and drawing untempered charms from her mystic brew, sprinkled his eyes" (4.123-166). Talos: "He was of the stock of bronze, of the men sprung from ash-trees... beneath the sinew by his ankle was a blood-red vein; and this, with its issues of life and death, was covered by a thin skin"; Medea "with her hostile glance... bewitched the eyes of Talos", he "grazed his ankle on a pointed crag; and the ichor gushed forth like melted lead", and fell like a half-hewn pine (4.1638-1688). A bronze giant with one weak point, killed by an evil eye, written on an island of a bronze giant and of Telchines with the evil eye.

**Stop 5: the rock-cut nymphaea, the monster-sculptors' grotto (36.43887, 28.20994).** Four subterranean rock-cut nymphaea with steps, passages, cisterns and niches: the closest thing on Rhodes to Tiberius' grotto at Sperlonga, where the ship of the Scylla group is signed "Athenodoros son of Agesander, Agesandros son of Paionios, Polydoros son of Polydoros" - Rhodians, the same three names Pliny gives for the Laocoön (NH 36.37, attalus translation): "Laocoon, his children and the wonderful clasping coils of the snakes were carved from a single block in accordance with an agreed plan by those eminent craftsmen Hagesander, Polydorus and Athenodorus, all of Rhodes." Lindos priest-lists once dated Agesander and Athenodoros to after 42 BC; the Sperlonga signatures show a family workshop reusing names across generations, and current opinion is Julio-Claudian. Pliny 36.33-34 adds "Zethus and Amphion, and then Dirce and the bull with its rope", the Farnese Bull, cut "from a single block" by Apollonius and Tauriscus of Tralles and "brought from Rhodes" for Asinius Pollio. Rhodes was the guild that carved monsters at scale. Second beat at the Archaeological Museum courtyard in the Old Town.

**Stop 6: the summit, Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus, the archmage's tower (36.44400, 28.21139).** The treaty-temple of the city is the place for Posidonius of Apamea (c. 135-51 BC), under whom "Rhodes eclipsed Athens to become the new centre for Stoic philosophy". He held the prytany, "the most important political office in Rhodes", and went on the embassy to Rome of 87/86 BC. Cicero called him "my teacher" and "our friend". Pliny 7.112: "Gnaeus Pompey, when going to enter the abode of the famous professor of philosophy Posidonius, forbade his retainer to knock on the door in the customary manner, and the subduer of the East and of the West dipped his standard to the portals of learning" (Latin: "fasces litterarum ianuae summisit"). Cicero (Tusc. 2.61) gives the scene inside: Posidonius lay in a fit of gout, and Pompey "said he was very sorry that he could not hear him lecture. 'But indeed you may,' replied the other, 'nor will I suffer any bodily pain to occasion so great a man to visit me in vain'", lecturing from his bed that "nothing was good but what was honest", and telling the pain, "it is to no purpose; notwithstanding you are troublesome, I will never acknowledge you an evil". Cicero saw his machine (De natura deorum 2.88): "sphaeram... quam nuper familiaris noster effecit Posidonius, cuius singulae conversiones idem efficiunt in sole et in luna et in quinque stellis errantibus, quod efficitur in caelo singulis diebus et noctibus" - "that sphere lately made by our friend Posidonius, the regular revolutions of which show the course of the sun, moon, and five wandering stars, as it is every day and night performed", such that even in "Scythia or Britain" nobody "would doubt that that sphere had been made... by the exertion of reason". Cleomedes (1.10) preserves his Earth measurement: Canopus "on but never above the horizon at Rhodes", 7.5 degrees (1/48 of a circle) high at Alexandria, 5,000 stadia apart, hence 240,000 stadia; Strabo's 3,750 stadia gives 180,000, the low figure Ptolemy adopted and Columbus relied on. He also tied the tides to the Moon. Then the artifact: the Antikythera wreck (c. 70-60 BC) carried "vases in the Rhodian style", the gearing "uses Hipparchus' theory for the motion of the Moon", and its parapegma works "best for latitudes in the range of 33.3-37.0 degrees north; the island of Rhodes is located between 35.85 and 36.50" (Freeth et al., Nature 2006/2008; Jones, A Portable Cosmos). Say plainly that the Corinthian month names now make Corinth or Syracuse the rival candidate. "It may have been forged here" is a hypothesis, and a good one.

**Stop 7: the north point of Elli (36.45706, 28.22071).** Some of Hipparchus' observations "were made in the north of the island of Rhodes" (MacTutor), so this is where the sky-watching ends. The arithmetic checks: at latitude 36.44 Canopus culminates about 0.9 degrees above the true horizon (about 1.4 with refraction), literally grazing; you need open sea to the south, so Posidonius' "at Rhodes" means the latitude, not this hill, whose southern horizon is the island's spine. On 30 September 2026 Canopus culminates about 06:55 local time, at sunrise (06:59), and at the 18:00 sailing it is 71 degrees below the horizon: it cannot be seen during the port call. Sunset that evening is about 18:50, seen from the ship.

# Later layers of Rhodes town: Ottoman (1522-1912), Jewish Rhodes, Italian Rhodes (1912-1947), WWII and the 1948 union with Greece

## The four skins of the walled city

The Knights left in January 1523 and the walls never came down. What changed was who lived inside them. Under the Ottomans (1522-1912) the Greeks were pushed out of the fortified town into suburbs called the *marasia*; Turks and Jews stayed inside, the churches became mosques, and enclosed wooden balconies were hung over the alleys. Think of it as a dungeon that was never cleared, only re-garrisoned: same corridors, new factions.

**Stand at the top of Sokratous street** (the bazaar street) and look up at the rose-pink **Suleymaniye Mosque**. It was the first mosque of the conquest, raised right after the 1522 siege on the site of a Christian church; the portico columns are salvage from that church. What you see is the 1808 rebuild. Its 34.5 m minaret was pulled down in 1987 as unsafe and re-erected in a restoration that ran 1988-2005 (Europa Nostra distinction, 2006). Inside are three domed chambers and a walnut minbar, but the building has been closed for worship since 2014 pending further work; sources conflict on whether tourists may enter (Greek Wikipedia: "visitable on specific days"; a Dec 2024 local blog: "twice a week in summer, free"). Treat the interior as a bonus, not a plan.

**Across the lane is the Hafiz Ahmed Agha Library (1793).** Hafiz Ahmed, born in the village of Asgourou, rose to chief equerry of the sultan and endowed this library "for the literate public of his hometown", even paying librarians to teach Arabic so people could read the books. It still holds some 1,256-1,265 manuscripts, including a Koran of 1540 and a chronicle of the 1522 siege, with old maps and engravings of Rhodes on the walls. D&D reading: this is the town's arcane library, the one place the siege is written down by the winners. Hours are posted on the door but not published online; a May 2026 review found it locked during posted hours because "the man with the code to the doors went to the hairdresser". Courtyard and front room are free.

Downhill, on Arionos Square, the **Mustafa Pasha Mosque (c. 1765)** stands beside the **Yeni Hammam**, the only Ottoman bath still standing in Rhodes. The mosque lost its minaret in 1973 and since 1977 has served the Muslim community as a wedding office; the hammam (first mentioned in a 1558 decree; the women's wing added under Mustafa III; a 13 m men's dome) has been shut for restoration, with the municipality's contract targeting completion by 31 December 2025. Whether it is bathing again by September 2026 is unverified.

Two more mosques give the quarter its skyline. The **Rejep Pasha Mosque (1588)** on Dorieos Square is the finest: a single dome on a 16-sided drum, Iznik tile panels with Koranic verses inside, ancient monolithic columns reused from Christian buildings, the founder's turbe in the courtyard. Part of its portico collapsed in 2011 and, as of 2025, it is closed to worship and visits. The **Ibrahim Pasha Mosque (1540/41)** on Platonos Square is the opposite: the oldest mosque on the island and the only one still in use, formally licensed only in 2019, its minaret patched by the Italians in the 1930s. Be discreet here; it is a living place of prayer.

## The Ottoman cemetery and the writer in the gatehouse

Outside the walls, just behind the Mandraki waterfront, the **Murat Reis Mosque (c. 1623)** sits in a walled Ottoman cemetery of eucalyptus and marble turbans. Murat Reis was one of Suleiman's great admirals; his tomb here drew pilgrims from the Anatolian coast "carrying lamps and offerings". Three of Suleiman's officers who died in the 1522 conquest are said to lie here too, so the graveyard predates the mosque by a century. In May 1945 the British posted a young **Lawrence Durrell** to Rhodes as information officer. He and Eve Cohen moved into "the little gatekeeper's lodge of an old Turkish cemetery, just across the road from the building used by the British Administration", named it **Villa Cleobolus**, and he wrote *Reflections on a Marine Venus* (1953) about it: "It is difficult to convey the extraordinary silence of this garden... The marble gravestones with their flowing Arabic inscriptions make a not ineffective monument to that lost epoch." The cottage is now closed and dilapidated; the garden is the point.

## La Juderia

Walk back through the walls to the eastern quarter. In 1523 the Ottomans resettled 150 Jewish families from Thessaloniki here; by 1930 about 4,000 Jews lived on an island of 30,000, speaking Ladino on Dossiadou and Simiou streets, with six synagogues. Only one survives: **Kahal Shalom (1577)**, the oldest synagogue in Greece, Sephardic in plan with the bimah in the centre and, unusually, two Torah arks. The other synagogues were flattened by Allied bombing of the Old Town in 1944. The **Jewish Museum (1997, founded by Aron Hasson)** occupies the former women's prayer rooms next door.

Then the story turns. Italy's 1938 racial laws drove more than 2,000 people to emigrate. On 18 July 1944 the German command ordered every Jewish male over 16 to report next day to the Air Force headquarters with identity papers; women and children followed with their valuables. On 23 July, 1,673 people were marched to the harbour and packed into three boats; about 100 Jews of Kos were added at Leros; seven died during the eight-day crossing to Piraeus. From Haidari camp they were sent by rail on 3 August and reached Auschwitz on 16 August 1944. Roughly 151-157 came back. Survivor Samuel Modiano: "One month of travel... Instead of killing us on the spot, on our island, we were transported to the camps and murdered in secret." The Turkish consul **Selahattin Ülkümen** faced General Kleemann and argued that "under Turkish law all citizens are equal", stretching consular protection to lapsed papers and spouses; he saved 42-50 people and was named Righteous Among the Nations in 1989. In the **Square of the Jewish Martyrs** the bronze **seahorse fountain** plays beside the black **Holocaust memorial dedicated 23 June 2002**, inscribed in English, French, Greek, Hebrew, Italian and Judaeo-Spanish.

## Italian Rhodes: the invented Middle Ages and the fascist forum

Italy took the island in May 1912 and kept it. Governor Mario Lago (1922-36) ran a "golden age" of eclectic building; his architect **Florestano Di Fausto** (in Rhodes 1923-27, city plan dated 29 January 1926) laid out the **Foro Italico** along Mandraki: the **Palazzo del Governo (1926-27)**, a Venetian-Gothic echo of the Doge's Palace in white and red Lindos stone with Murano chandeliers; the church of St John (1924-25), a replica of the Knights' lost church, now the Orthodox **Evangelismos** cathedral; the Post Office; the Banca d'Italia; the oriental-Deco **Nea Agora** market with its fish-market pavilion; and the **Grande Albergo delle Rose (1925-27)**. Inside the walls the Italians "preserved what was left from the Knights' period, and destroyed all Ottoman buildings": houses against the walls were demolished, cemeteries became parks, the **Gate of Liberty** was punched through in 1924 by rulers "who portrayed themselves as liberators", and in 1937-40 Vittorio Mesturino rebuilt the **Palace of the Grand Master** from the ruins of the 1856 gunpowder explosion as a holiday residence for King Victor Emmanuel III and Mussolini. Governor De Vecchi (1936-40) stripped the "oriental ornaments" off the Albergo delle Rose, requisitioned Jewish tombstones for the Palace works and moved the Jewish cemetery out to the Kallithea road. Mussolini never came. The Medusa mosaic in the Palace was lifted from Kos.

## War, hunger, flag

Italy's armistice of 8 September 1943 made Rhodes a battlefield between allies: 39,100 Italians under Admiral Campioni against 6,000-8,000 Germans of Sturm-Division Rhodos under Kleemann. British majors Jellicoe and Dolbey parachuted in on 9-10 September to promise help that was a week away; Campioni surrendered on 11 September (447 Italian dead, some 30,000 prisoners). He was shot by Mussolini's puppet republic in Parma on 24 May 1944; on 12 February 1944 the prison ship *Oria* sank off Cape Sounion with more than 4,000 Italian POWs from Rhodes aboard. Under German occupation famine "severely affected" the civilians of Rhodes through 1944-45, and British bombs tore holes in the medieval town. Generalmajor Otto Wagener surrendered the Dodecanese on 8 May 1945 and was later jailed for war crimes. The British ran the islands until 31 March 1947, when Rear Admiral Periklis Ioannidis took over for Greece under the Paris treaty of 10 February 1947; the formal incorporation ceremony was held in Rhodes on **7 March 1948**, still the Dodecanese's own holiday and the name of the square by the Nea Agora. A good birthday thought at the Albergo delle Rose bar: in January-February 1949 this hotel hosted the Egypt-Israel armistice talks that ended the 1948 war.

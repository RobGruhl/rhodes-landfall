# The Colossus of Rhodes - origin and construction (siege of 305-304 BC, Helepolis, Chares of Lindos, building method, dimensions, epigram, 226 BC fall, primary sources, location theories)

## The Colossus of Rhodes: how a siege became a god

### 1. The siege that paid for a wonder (305-304 BC)

Start the story at the water. In 305 BC Rhodes was a rich, neutral, Ptolemy-friendly merchant republic, and Antigonus the One-Eyed wanted it broken from Egypt. He sent his son Demetrius, soon nicknamed *Poliorcetes*, "the Besieger". Diodorus counts the armada: "two hundred warships of all sizes and more than one hundred and seventy auxiliary vessels", "not quite forty thousand soldiers besides the cavalry and the pirates", and trailing behind them "almost a thousand privately owned ships" of merchants hoping to loot (Diod. 20.82). Against this the city enrolled "about six thousand citizens and as many as a thousand metics and aliens", promised freedom to any slave who fought bravely, and decreed that the daughters of the fallen would get "dowries at the public cost" (Diod. 20.84).

Demetrius first hit the harbours: "two towers of four storeys, exceeding in height the towers of the harbour, each of them mounted upon two ships", screened by "a floating boom of squared logs studded with spikes"; the Rhodians answered with fire-boats "filled with dry pitchy wood" (Diod. 20.85-87). When the sea assault stalled, he turned to the land walls and built the monster.

### 2. The Helepolis - "City-Taker"

Diodorus 20.91 is your D&D stat block. The base was "almost fifty cubits" square, "framed together from squared timber and fastened with iron"; the whole thing rolled on "eight great solid wheels" whose rims, "two cubits" wide, were "overlaid with heavy iron plates"; corner beams "little short of a hundred cubits" rose through "nine storeys"; "the three exposed sides of the machine he covered externally with iron plates nailed on so that it should receive no injury from fire carriers"; each floor had shuttered "ports on the front, in size and shape fitted to the individual characteristics of the missiles" (stone-throwers below, dart-throwers above). To move it: "three thousand four hundred men excelling in strength". Plutarch (Demetrius 21) gives a lower, probably truer height, 66 cubits on a 48-cubit base, and marvels that "it did not totter or lean when it moved, but remained firm and erect". Vitruvius (10.16.4) names the engineer, Epimachus of Athens, and gives 125 feet high, 60 wide, weight 360,000 pounds (roughly 160 tonnes), armoured against a "three hundred and sixty pound" stone.

How do you stop a siege construct? Three ways, and all are attested. (a) Fire from the walls: at "about the second watch" one night the Rhodians "began to strike the helepolis with an unremitting shower of the fire missiles"; "some of the iron plates of the helepolis were dislodged, and where the place was laid bare the fire missiles rained upon the exposed wood" (Diod. 20.96). At dawn Demetrius counted "more than eight hundred fire missiles of various sizes and not less than fifteen hundred catapult bolts" (20.97). (b) Underground: Demetrius's sappers tunnelled under the wall; the Rhodians "by digging a deep trench parallel to the wall ... made contact with their opponents underground" - a counter-mine - and a Rhodian named Athenagoras played double agent, promising to betray the city and instead luring a senior Antigonid officer up through the mine to be captured (20.94). (c) The trap: Vitruvius says the Rhodians' disgraced engineer Diognetus, restored on condition he could keep the machine as spoil, ordered "a hole to be made" in the wall and had citizens pour "water, filth, and mud" through it, so the Helepolis "sunk in the quagmire", and it was later set up in the city as a trophy inscribed "Diognetus presented this to the people out of the spoils of war" (Vitr. 10.16.7-8). Note the contradiction with Pliny, who says the money for the Colossus came from selling the abandoned engines; scholars treat Vitruvius's trophy tale as an engineers' legend.

Peace came in 304 BC: Rhodes was to be "autonomous and ungarrisoned and should enjoy its own revenue", allied to Antigonus "unless he should be at war with Ptolemy", giving 100 hostages (Diod. 20.99.3). The Rhodians then asked the oracle of Ammon whether to honour Ptolemy as a god, and built the Ptolemaeum, a square precinct with stoas "a stade long" (20.100).

### 3. Chares, the money, and the twelve years

Pliny (NH 34.41-42, Rackham): "Calling for admiration before all others was the colossal Statue of the Sun at Rhodes made by Chares of Lindus, the pupil of Lysippus ... It is recorded that it took twelve years to complete and cost 300 talents, money realized from the engines of war belonging to King Demetrius which he had abandoned when he got tired of the protracted siege." Strabo (14.2.5) agrees on "seven times ten cubits" and Chares. Chares was a Rhodian, from Lindos, trained by Lysippos, Alexander's court sculptor, who had already made a 40-cubit Zeus at Tarentum. Dates are inferred: Pliny says it stood 66 years before the earthquake of c. 226 BC, so work ran roughly 292-280 BC. A dark legend (Sextus Empiricus) says Chares quoted a price for a 50-cubit statue, was asked for double the height, only doubled the fee, went bankrupt and killed himself, and a certain Laches of Lindos finished it - a tale, not history.

### 4. How it was built - per "Philo"

The only construction account is by pseudo-Philo of Byzantium, a late-antique rhetorician (5th-6th c. AD) surviving in one 9th-century manuscript. He says the artist used "500 talents of bronze and 300 talents of iron" (about 13-15 t and 9 t), that "the workmen fortified the statue of the Colossus from the inside by hewn stones joined together by iron bolts, and the bars which are used on the stones ... seem to have been fashioned by the hammers of the Cyclops", that the feet were cast first on a marble base, that each section was cast on top of the last because "the individual metal sections could not be moved", and that Chares "continually poured an immense mound of earth round the finished parts" so he could work "from ground level" - finishing with the flourish "on the world he set a second Helios facing the first." Archaeologist Ursula Vedder (Getty, 2017) rejects the earth-mound casting: Rhodian bronze workshops show colossi cast in large sections and assembled, and temple architraves heavier than any bronze piece were routinely lifted. Nathan Badoud (2024) counters that casting in courses "is probably authentic", comparing the Great Buddha of Nara. Scholarship is genuinely split.

### 5. The dedication

The pedestal inscription survives as Anthologia Palatina 6.171 (anonymous; Paton's translation): "To thy very self, O Sun, did the people of Dorian Rhodes raise high to heaven this colossus, then, when having laid to rest the brazen wave of war, they crowned their country with the spoils of their foes. Not only over the sea, but on the land, too, did they establish the lovely light of unfettered freedom. For to those who spring from the race of Heracles dominion is a heritage both on land and sea."

### 6. The fall (c. 226 BC)

Strabo: "it now lies on the ground, having been thrown down by an earthquake and broken at the knees. In accordance with a certain oracle, the people did not raise it again. This, then, is the most excellent of the votive offerings." Later tradition names Delphi. Polybius (5.88-89) records the relief effort: Hiero and Gelo of Syracuse gave 75 silver talents plus cauldrons and 20 more talents; Ptolemy III promised 300 talents of silver, a million artabae of grain, timber for ten quinqueremes and ten triremes, 40,000 cubits of squared pine, and specifically "three thousand talents ... for the restoration of the Colossus" with "a hundred master builders and three hundred and fifty masons". The Rhodians took the gifts and left the god lying. Pliny saw it that way three centuries later: "even lying on the ground it is a marvel. Few people can make their arms meet round the thumb of the figure, and the fingers are larger than most statues; and where the limbs have been broken off enormous cavities yawn, while inside are seen great masses of rock with the weight of which the artist steadied it." He adds that Rhodes held "a hundred other colossal statues". The famous coda - Arabs in 654 selling the scrap to a merchant of Edessa who hauled it off on 900 camels - comes only from Theophanes and is now regarded as an 8th-century fabrication.

### 7. What it looked like, and where it stood

Almost certainly a nude youth with long curling hair and a radiate crown, the image on Rhodian tetradrachms since c. 404 BC; a Rhodian temple relief shows Helios shading his eyes with one hand, a plausible pose. No torch (a 1572 Heemskerck invention), no harbour-straddling (a medieval fantasy; the entrance would have been closed for twelve years). Three candidate sites: the Mandraki mole under Fort St Nicholas (Hoepfner; sandstone blocks and marble fragments), the Helios sanctuary on the little hill now under the Palace of the Grand Master (Badoud: an inscription on Helios's sacred area, priestly dedications, a marble head), or the acropolis above the stadium (Vedder). Badoud also notes that "kolossos" originally meant a magical statue that could "encase a god or mortal" - not a big one. The size came later, from this statue.

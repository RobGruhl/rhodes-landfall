# What the signs say: street names, scripts and languages as a sovereignty palimpsest

*Research notes for Jamie's track. Serves stops 2, 4, 5, 7, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18 and 21. Builds on jamie-05 (the Knights' "Magna et Communis Platea"), jamie-06 (the 1831 mahalle list, "Arriva del Kadi"), jamie-07 (La Kaye Ancha, the Juderia's plaques), jamie-08 (Italian schools and names), jamie-04 and jamie-09 (naming after grandparents and shrines), and research/05, 06, 11. The web search quota ran out during this pass; anything marked [unverified] rests on general knowledge or an unreachable source and must not be scripted as fact. Fact-checked 8 September 2026 and again, adversarially, on 12 September 2026 (see the two sections at the end; their CORRECTED and REMOVED verdicts override the body, and the second pass overrides the first where they differ). Written 8 September 2026 for a walk on Wednesday 30 September 2026.*

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** Every regime that has held Rhodes has written its claim on the walls, and none has fully erased the one before. The street Jamie climbs from Hippocrates Square is Sokratous on the plaque; to the Jews it was "Arriva del Kadi", up at the judge's; to Ottoman writers it was the bazaar, in a town organised by neighbourhoods named for prayer-houses; to the Knights it was roughly the line of the inner wall between their compound and everyone else's town. Nobody I can find has documented the day a Greek official put Socrates, Plato and a few Rhodian poets on the lanes, so do not give her a date; give her the pattern, which is that the current names skip five centuries of the town's life and land on antiquity, the only past every Rhodian can agree to be proud of. Under the names are scripts: Latin and French on the Knights' slabs, Arabic-script Ottoman on mosques and gravestones, Hebrew and Ladino in the synagogue, Italian on Mandraki, Greek on the plaques, English on everything a tourist is meant to read. Under the scripts are the languages people speak: a Dodecanesian Greek that keeps sounds Athens has dropped, a Turkish dying in the mouths of the old, a Judaeo-Spanish that has gone, an Italian that survives as the tongue of the cruise passengers, and the Albanian, Bengali and Urdu of the people who make the beds. She should finish knowing that a street sign is a sovereignty document, and that a Rhodian's own name is one too.

## Tell at stop 2 (Gate of the Virgin) and stop 4 (Marine Gate, Hippocrates Square): every gate has two names

**Gates are named by their masters.** The opening Jamie walks through was cut in 1955 by the Greek administration and named Pyli Panagias, the Gate of the Virgin, for the roofless church beside it. The Italians made three of her other thresholds: Liberty Gate in 1924, "portraying themselves as liberators from Ottoman rule"; St Athanasios reopened in 1922 "during the 400th anniversary of the conquest of Rhodes by the Ottomans"; Acandia in 1935, with a new street they called Via del Carretto after a Grand Master and the Greeks renamed Alhadeff Street after the Jewish banking family (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia; jamie-08). The older gates carry double names. The harbour gate is officially Pyli Thalassini, the Marine or Sea Gate, "the main entrance to the town from the harbour", and "also called St. Catherine gate by historic sources" (kastra.eu). The south-eastern gate is officially Pyli Agiou Ioannou, the Gate of St John, and "commonly known as Koshkinou or Porta Kokkini", the Red Door, from the tradition that in 1522 "so many fallen men" lay before it "that the stones were dyed red" (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia; kastra.eu). Koskinou is the village about eight kilometres down the east-coast road towards Faliraki and Lindos (Koskinou, Wikipedia: five miles from Rhodes town): the Order named gates for saints, the townspeople for where they led or what happened there.

**A square with four names.** Hippocrates Square was the Knights' "Magna et Communis Platea", the great common square, with the Castellania under d'Amboise's arms and the date 1507 (jamie-05); its modern name honours the doctor of Kos across the water. Sokratous, rising from it, was "Arriva del Kadi" to the Jews because it "connected two sections: the Jewish business section at the lower end and the Ottoman business section on the upper end" (Rhodes Jewish Museum); an Ottoman-period study says "the city has developed by being positioned around the bazaar on Knight Street", meaning this street (Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 133), and the same study places the Kadı mescid's mahalle, the judge's quarter, "next to Sokrates Street, one of the most important streets of the city of Rhodes" (p. 151), which is why the Jews called the top of the street "up at the judge's"; and the Knights' inner wall, now vanished, "ran roughly along today's Sokratous street" (research/06). Point her at the plaque on the corner and give her all four names.

**The curated antiquity on the plaques.** The lanes carry Sokratous, Ippokratous, Platonos, Aristotelous, Pythagora, Omirou, Pindarou, and beside the panhellenic names a set of Rhodian worthies only a classicist would know: Ippodamou for Hippodamus of Miletus, to whom Strabo credits the grid of the city of 408 BC (a tradition, not a fact: Hippodamus was active at Miletus in 479 BC and would have been very old, so say "the plan Strabo credits to Hippodamus"); Agisandrou for Agesander, one of the three Rhodian sculptors Pliny names for the Laocoön; Apellou for Apelles, whose contest of painted lines with the Rhodian Protogenes Pliny sets in this town; Dosiadou and Simiou for Dosiadas and Simmias, the poets of the pattern-poems in the Greek Anthology (Simmias is securely Rhodian; Dosiadas's Rhodian origin I could not verify on this pass); Kleovoulou Square at the top of the Street of the Knights for Cleobulus of Lindos, one of the Seven Sages (research/06 for the square; the other identifications are my reading of standard reference works, [unverified] as the municipality's intent). Ippoton, the Street of the Knights, is the one lane that records the Order. What the choice says needs no minute-book: a Greek administration taking over a walled town Greeks had been barred from sleeping in for four centuries (jamie-06) wrote onto it the one past nobody could dispute and passed over the Ottoman and Italian names in silence. I found no municipal or Ephorate document on Old Town street naming and no renaming date (the Greeks arrived in 1947 and raised the flag on 7 March 1948; the Italian plan is dated 29 January 1926); say "after the Greeks took the island" and no more. The plaques are Greek capitals with a Latin transliteration [layout and material unverified; check on the day].

## Tell at stop 5 (Hospital) and stop 7 (Street of the Knights): Latin and French on the stones, and why the Turks left them

**What the Knights wrote.** The Order's language of record was Latin, its table language mostly French, and the walls say so. Over the Hospital door two angels carry the arms of Grand Master Fluvian, who left the money (research/06). The Inn of France carries "a 1492 inscription naming Emery d'Amboise as Grand Prior of France" and three plaques of Villiers de l'Isle-Adam's arms "with dedications 'Pour la Maison', 'Pour l'Oratoire', 'Pour Philerme'" (research/06, medium): money for the house, the chapel and the shrine at Filerimos, in French. The Inn of Auvergne on Argyrokastrou Square has "the Gothic inscription 'D'Auvargne, G. Prior, F. Guy de Blanchefort, 1507'" (research/06). The d'Amboise Gate carries the "Arms of Grand-Master Emery d'Amboise, with inscription 'DAMBOYSE MDXII'" (heraldica.org); research/05 adds that the arms are held by a marble angel beside the arms of the Order, but neither heraldica.org nor romeartlover.it nor Wikipedia describes the angel, so script the arms and the date and let her find the angel herself [angel unverified]. A coat of arms says which family, a Roman numeral which year of Our Lord: the Order's filing system in stone. The Hospital's ground floor holds the Knights' gravestones, but no source I reached transcribes an epitaph [unverified]; do not script a text.

**Why the stones survived.** Rossi, on the 1522 capitulation: the Turks "did not touch the fortress, the auberges of the langues, the buildings of the castellany, or the magnificent hospital... they left almost intact the coats of arms, the gateways, and the inscriptions" (jamie-05, fact-check 18). Four centuries of sultans did not chisel off the Latin. The Italians did chisel off the Ottoman: the balconies came down from the Street of the Knights in 1916, and they "preserved what was left from the Knights' period, and destroyed all Ottoman buildings" in the plan area (jamie-08). The Muslim conqueror kept the Christian inscriptions; the Christian coloniser scrubbed the Muslim ones. One Italian stone remains in the Knights' quarter: the Palace was rebuilt in 1937 to 1940 for the King "and later for Fascist dictator Benito Mussolini, whose name can still be seen on a large plaque near the entrance" (Wikipedia, single-sourced; jamie-08; on 12 September 2026 neither romeartlover.it, the Wikimedia Commons category nor the Palace ticketing site mentioned such a plaque, so script it only as "look for"). The Duce it names never came.

## Tell at stop 13 (Suleymaniye) and stop 12 (Sofokleous, lunch): neighbourhoods, not streets; the Turkish that is going; the Greek she hears

**Mahalles.** An Ottoman town was administered by mahalles, each named for the mosque or mescid at its centre, "the section of the city where families who worship in the same mescid live together" (Ergenç, in Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 149). The 1831 census gives Rhodes twenty-three Muslim mahalles and two Jewish ones inside the walls: Süleymaniye, İbrahim Paşa, Kadı (the judge's), Yeniçeli (probably Janissary), Çizmeci Ali Ağa (the bootmaker's), Takyeci (the skullcap-maker's), İlkmihrap (the first prayer-niche), Dolaplı (with cupboards, the church of Agia Triada on Ippoton), and so on (Evliyaoğlu, p. 150; jamie-06). You lived "in the mahalle of the bootmaker Ali Ağa's mescid": an address by congregation and trade, and none of it is on a plaque. The nearest a walker gets is the mosques: Evliya Çelebi reported on the Suleymaniye's minaret "an inscription... which recorded repairs effected in 940 AH (1533/1534 CE)", but that minaret was demolished in 1987 and rebuilt in the 1988 to 2005 restoration (Suleymaniye Mosque, Wikipedia), and the Ibrahim Pasha Mosque on Platonos Square, a step off Sofokleous, is dated 947 AH, 1540 to 1541 (Ibrahim Pasha Mosque, Wikipedia; jamie-06). Whether a tughra survives on any gate or fountain I could not verify [unverified]; the safest Arabic script to send her to is the Ottoman cemetery at the Murat Reis mosque behind Mandraki, whose gravestones "with Arabic inscriptions" Durrell called "a not ineffective monument to that lost epoch" (research/11). The Hafiz Ahmed Agha library, across the lane from the Suleymaniye, holds about 1,260 manuscripts (1,265 by the library's own count, 1,256 by Wikipedia) in Ottoman Turkish, Arabic and Persian: three languages, one script (research/11; jamie-06).

**The Turkish of Rhodes.** Rhodes' Muslims were never exchanged in 1923 because the island was Italian, so roughly 2,500 to 3,500 Greek citizens of Ottoman descent still live here (Turks of the Dodecanese, Wikipedia). Their language is going. One of the last Turkish-medium schools, the Süleymaniye Medrese on the crater at the top of the Street of the Knights (stop 8 in jamie-06 and jamie-08), "was changed as the 13th City Primary School of Rhodes in 1972 and since that date, lecturing in Turkish was permanently banned" (Hayta 2024, p. 366); the Greek ministry dates the last Turkish-language schools to 1991 (Gross, in HRWF 2015). From the Turkish side: "it is not very possible to meet Turkish kids and youngsters who are able to speak Turkish. And those who are able to speak Turkish can barely speak it" (Hayta, p. 371); Kaurinkoski records that many "forgot" Turkish and that "mixed marriages with Christians became commonplace" (jamie-06). Even the word is policed: the authorities "do not register associations that include terms such as 'Turk' or 'Minority' in their names", so the "Rhodes Turkish Community" of 1912 was banned in 1967 and "closed down in 1987" (Hayta, p. 370), and the community organises today as the Brotherhood and Cultural Association of Muslims of Rhodes, founded 2000 (Hayta, p. 371; HRWF 2015 via jamie-06); islanders "may not express that they are Turks to the persons whom they do not know well, and instead they have to express that they are Greek Muslims" (p. 372). Greek loanwords in Rhodian Turkish and the registry form of Turkish Rhodians' names are documented nowhere I reached [unverified]. The hand-painted sign "Bekir Karakouzou Coffee and Coffee Mill" at Sokratous 76 is the one Turkish name still written on the bazaar street (Greek Gastronomy Guide, unreachable at fact-check; glance, do not promise).

**The lunch table's dialect.** What she hears at Nireas is Dodecanesian Greek, which the standard classification (Trudgill 2003, Kontosopoulos, Newton 1972, as cited in Varieties of Modern Greek, Wikipedia) puts in the south-eastern group with Cyprus, Chios and Kos. Its marks: double consonants standard Greek lost are kept, even added at the start of words; the final n is kept, "ipen" for "he said" and "tirin" for "cheese" where an Athenian says "ipe" and "tiri"; and "some dialects of the Aegean Islands, especially in the Dodecanese, have a tendency of deleting intervocalic voiced fricatives", so a v, dh or gh between vowels drops. The island's sweet keeps a dialect word: melekouni, says the EU protected-name document, "comes from the words 'meli' [honey] and 'kounní', which means 'seed' in Rhodian dialect", kounní being a local descendant of the ancient kokkos, the grain (EUR-Lex single document PGI-GR-02208, read 12 September 2026; research/07). On "Doric survivals": the ancient Rhodians were Dorians, and the Colossus epigram calls them "the people of Dorian Rhodes" (research/01), but the modern dialects derive from Koine Greek, Tsakonian and possibly Italiot being the only exceptions, and no Doric survival is claimed for Rhodes (Varieties of Modern Greek, Wikipedia). If a waiter says Rhodians still speak Doric, that is pride, not linguistics. The Church's Greek is a third register, the Byzantine Greek of the service books she would hear from Agios Fanourios.

## Tell at stop 14 (Symi Square, on the way to the Juderia): Ladino on the plaques, and a language that has left

**The quarter's own map.** The square where 1,673 people were gathered in July 1944 was "La Kaye Ancha", the wide street, "the central gathering place through which weddings and other joyous processions passed" (jewishrhodes.org; jamie-07); Vizantiou was "Kay de la Talmud Torah"; the prayer-room at 4 Vizantiou was the "Kalijika de la Havra" and keeps a Hebrew plaque dated "Nisan 5637" (jguideeurope; its printed year 1837 does not match, 5637 being 1877, flagged in jamie-07); the cemetery beyond the Koskinou Gate was near "La Fasana". None of these is on a sign. The lanes are now Dosiadou and Simiou, two Hellenistic poets, and the square is Plateia Evreon Martyron, the Square of the Jewish Martyrs: the Greek name is the only one that records what happened there.

**Scripts in the synagogue.** Kahal Shalom's courtyard plaque is dated "the month of Kislev, 5338 (1577)" (jewishrhodes.org); its wall plaques are "primarily in Ladino, the language of the Sephardic community, and Hebrew", dedicated "to those who maintained the synagogue", and "another, in French, is dedicated to the members of the community who were murdered during the Holocaust" (Kahal Shalom Synagogue, Wikipedia). French because the community's modern school from 1902 was the Alliance Israélite's (jamie-07). The 2002 memorial on the square is a six-sided black granite column in Greek, Hebrew, English, French, Italian and Ladino, counting "1604 Jewish Martyrs of Rhodes and Cos" (Rhodes Jewish Museum). Six languages for one community is the whole history: Greek for the state, Hebrew for the faith, Ladino for the home, French for the school, Italian for the government that deported them, English for the descendants who come back from Seattle and Cape Town.

**The language itself.** Judaeo-Spanish was written in Hebrew letters, "Rashi script and its cursive form Solitreo", and "today it is mainly written with the Latin alphabet"; the Rhodes dialect's tell was that "when a word ends with o, it is pronounced as u" (Judaeo-Spanish, Wikipedia). Before 1523 the island's Jews were "Greek speaking Romaniotes who had Greek surnames" (History of the Jews in Rhodes, Wikipedia); the Spanish exiles overwrote them, and by the 1930s the quarter had a Judaeo-Spanish paper, El Boletin, and a joint Turkish-and-Jewish one, Selam (jamie-07). Worldwide the language has "51,000 (2018)" speakers, "most native speakers are elderly" (Wikipedia). With about 35 to 40 Jews resident (Kahal Shalom and History of the Jews in Rhodes, Wikipedia) I found no source naming a living Ladino speaker on the island; say it is heard here only in July, when descendants come for the memorial and the Ladino concerts in the Palace courtyard (jamie-07). The best sentence on the town's languages is what the provincial governor, the vali, whose own son was a pupil, told the boys of the Frères school in 1910: "learn your mother tongue, whatever it is, then your father tongue, that is Turkish, and finally French, the universal language of civilized countries" (Guidi 2022, pp. 25 to 28, in jamie-08). Mother tongue, whatever it is: Ottoman language policy in seven words.

## Tell at stops 16, 18 and 21 (Mandraki, the birthday deck, Kallithea): Italian on the stones and in the schoolroom, and the languages of the season

**The Foro Italico.** Di Fausto's 1926 plan christened the new seafront "Foro Italico" (Wikipedia); Bernabiti's Casa Littoria of 1937 to 1939 was named for the fascio littorio, and on 7 March 1948 the Greek flag went up there and the "fascist symbols" came off (imrtreasures.gr; jamie-08); the square by the Nea Agora is named for that date. The stag and doe columns are Italian, 1939 (research/02). Individual Italian foundation inscriptions on Mandraki are not described in any source I reached [unverified]. Italian is heard on Mandraki again "as the language of the cruise passengers the Foro was built to impress" (jamie-08).

**The language of school.** Italian was made compulsory by the first school regulation of 1926 and dominant under De Vecchi in 1937, with Greek "taught optionally, without books and only in the first classes of primary school" (Greek Wikipedia, via jamie-08); Metropolitan Apostolos ran secret schools under cover of catechism; the Venetokleion pupils struck in spring 1937 after an eighteen-year-old asked the headmaster, "did you not always tell us that we are Greeks?" (Guidi 2022, pp. 37 to 38). The Italian schools closed in 1947, the last on 9 September 1950 (jamie-08). Rhodians now in their nineties were schooled in Italian; nobody younger was. Italian loanwords in Rhodian speech are anecdotal, no source [unverified]. Kallithea (stop 21) is Italian at its most theatrical: a spa inaugurated on 1 July 1929 with King Victor Emmanuel III attending (research/08), now a Greek municipal wedding venue.

**Today's working languages.** The season runs on languages no sign records. ITEP found 26.6 per cent of Greek hotels hired non-EU workers in 2025 and 36,000 of 263,000 positions unfilled, with "nearly 70 per cent of total staffing shortages" in the South Aegean, Crete, the Ionian Islands and Macedonia-Thrace (Dimokratiki, 20 August 2026; jamie-09), and Rodiaki headlined some 9,000 "metaklitoi", third-country recruits, on Rhodes this year (page blocked at fact-check; jamie-09). No figure breaks them down by language; Albanian, Bengali, Urdu and Filipino are what one hears in Greek hotel kitchens generally [unverified for Rhodes]. English is the tourist lingua franca, German second; the brown tourist signs inside the walls are Greek and English [third language unverified]. On shop signs: the Medieval City has been a UNESCO site since 1988 (65.85 hectares; Greek Wikipedia) under the Ephorate of Antiquities of the Dodecanese, and any change to a facade needs Ephorate approval under the antiquities law; a uniform shop-signage rule is widely assumed but I could not find its text [unverified]. Tell her to look at the shop fronts and judge whether anyone enforces it.

## Names as a language of belonging (weave into stops 4, 12 and 18)

- **Greek.** Children are named for grandparents, so the same saints recur every second generation; the name day outranks the birthday and is an open house at which the celebrant feeds the visitors (xpatathens; jamie-09). Someone named for a pagan hero has no name day (jamie-04). Rhodes adds vow-names: Tsambika and Tsambikos, given after a barefoot climb to the icon at Tsambika, "only on Rhodes" (greece-moments; Greeka), and Fanourios, the revealer of lost things, whose feast on 27 August fills Agios Fanourios with women carrying cakes and lists of names (jamie-04). Rob's birthday is, in Greek terms, the lesser feast.
- **Sephardic.** First son for the paternal grandfather, second for the maternal (jamie-07); fifteen surnames, Alhadeff, Amato, Benatar, Capelluto, Codron, Cohen, Franco, Hanan, Hasson, Israel, Levy, Menascé, Scemaria, Soriano, Tarica, account for two-thirds of the cemetery (Taranto; jamie-07). One of the fifteen is now a Greek street outside the walls.
- **Italian.** Under the fascist turn "the Italianization of names was encouraged" alongside the Balilla youth (Italian Islands of the Aegean, Wikipedia); Lago "encouraged intermarriage with local Greeks" (Wikipedia), with automatic Catholicism for the children by the Metropolis's account (jamie-08).
- **Turkish Rhodian.** The state's word is "Muslim Greeks"; their own, to those they trust, is Turks (Hayta, p. 372). Under Article 19 of the citizenship code, 1955 to 1998, anyone "of non-Greek ethnic origin" leaving without intent to return could be stripped of nationality (jamie-06). The island's best-loved dairy, Stani, was founded in 1952 by Ibrahim Sarri Hasan (research/09, medium): a Turkish name every Rhodian child knows from an ice-cream shop.
- **Ancient.** Grandfathers' names, vow-names after gods (Heliodoros, gift of the Sun), and every document dated "in the priesthood of X", the priest of Helios (jamie-04).

## Three things to read on the walk

1. **The street plaque at the foot of Sokratous, Hippocrates Square (stop 4).** ΟΔΟΣ ΣΩΚΡΑΤΟΥΣ with a Latin transliteration [layout unverified]: "Socrates Street". What it does not say: Arriva del Kadi, the bazaar, the Knights' inner wall. Four names, one plaque.
2. **The d'Amboise Gate plaque (stops 9 to 10, from the forecourt or the moat walk):** the arms of d'Amboise inscribed DAMBOYSE MDXII (heraldica.org; research/05 says an angel holds them beside the Order's arms, unverified): "d'Amboise, 1512", untouched by four centuries of sultans. For a French one, the Inn of France (stop 7): "Pour la Maison", "Pour l'Oratoire", "Pour Philerme", for the house, for the chapel, for the shrine at Filerimos.
3. **The Ladino face of the 2002 memorial, Square of the Jewish Martyrs (after stop 14 or on the way back to the ship).** Six faces, six languages, and the count "1604 Jewish Martyrs of Rhodes and Cos" (Rhodes Jewish Museum). The exact Ladino wording is not transcribed in any source I reached [unverified], so read the English face aloud and tell her the Ladino says the same in the language the victims spoke at home. In the synagogue courtyard, two minutes on, the plaques "primarily in Ladino... and Hebrew" thank the people who kept the roof on (Wikipedia).

## Continuities and contrasts: four Rhodians asked "what is this street called?"

- **An ancient Rhodian** would give a direction, not a name; his city addressed itself by demes, tribes and sanctuaries and dated its documents by the priest of Helios (jamie-04). He would be delighted to find his planner, his sculptor and his pattern-poets on enamel plaques, and would ask why the sun-god has no street.
- **A Hospitaller** would say "the Magna et Communis Platea" for the square and name the street by its landmark; his harbour gate is St Catherine's; his language is Latin on stone and French at table. He would recognise naming a street for a great man, having lived on one named for his Order.
- **An Ottoman-era householder** would name his mahalle, "the mescid of Çizmeci Ali Ağa", and the bazaar was simply the bazaar. Asked in Ladino, his Jewish neighbour would say "Arriva del Kadi" and mean the same street. He would be astonished that the Latin slabs his ancestors left alone are now the town's pride, and that his mescid's name is on no sign.
- **A Rhodian today** says "Sokratous", writes it in Greek, reads it in English on the tourist sign, and gives directions by the mosque, the museum and the square, that is, by the Ottoman, Hospitaller and ancient names at once. Her grandmother may have been schooled in Italian, her Muslim neighbour's grandfather in Turkish, and the couple at the next table are speaking Bengali. The sign is Greek; the street is everyone's.

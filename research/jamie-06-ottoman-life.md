# Jamie 06. Life inside the walls under the Ottomans (1522-1912), and the Muslim community of Rhodes today

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** Research/11 already gives the monuments (which mosque, which date, which restoration). This file is about the *people-sorting machine* the Ottomans ran inside a fortress they never rebuilt: who was allowed to sleep inside the walls and who had to be out by sunset; how a town of three faiths was organised into neighbourhoods named after their prayer-houses; how a pious endowment (vakf) paid for a library, a clock, a soup kitchen and a school; how the bath, the coffeehouse and the embroidered bed-tent structured a household's week and a woman's life; and how, uniquely in Greece, this Muslim community was never "exchanged" in 1923, so that roughly two to four thousand Greek citizens of Ottoman descent still live in Rhodes, run a vakf under a 2021 Greek law, marry in a mosque-turned-registry, and describe themselves, depending on who is asking, as Turks, as Muslims, or as Rhodians. Everything below is anchored to a stop on the walk. Where the sources disagree, or where a claim rests on a single weak source, it says so.

---

## Tell at stops 2 and 12 (the gates): the terms of 1522 and the sunset rule

**The bargain.** The capitulation of 20-22 December 1522 (research/05 and verify-deltas) gave the Knights twelve days to leave with arms, relics and valuables, promised that churches would not be desecrated or converted, exempted the islanders from Ottoman taxation for five years and gave any islander three years to emigrate. About 4,000 Rhodians sailed with the Order on 1 January 1523. The promise about churches lasted less than a month: the conventual church of St John at the top of the Street of the Knights became the town's main mosque (Ottoman sources call it the *Sencuvan* or *Cami-i Kebir*, the Great Mosque), and the first Friday prayer was held in it on 20 January 1523 (Evliyaoğlu 2022, citing Çelikkol; Rossi 1927 quotes an Ottoman chronicler on the bell tower becoming a place from which "the declaration of God's unity" rose "to the heavens").

**The expulsion.** A Turkish architectural historian summarising Ottoman sources puts it plainly: after the conquest "the greater part of the non-Muslims were settled in the Varoş [the suburbs] and the other large villages nearby, for security reasons... inside the walls only the military garrison, the civilian Muslim population and a few Christian families of special status were permitted to settle" (Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 149; the footnote for this is Kiel, not Örenç). The Greek suburbs were called *varoş* in Turkish and *marasia* in Greek (the local form of the same word), though Evliyaoğlu, citing Örenç, adds a wrinkle: the *Varoşlar* proper were "almost always" Turkish, and *Maras* was the name people used for the quarter where Muslims, Jews and Greeks lived together. Say "the suburbs" rather than insisting on the equation. A Greek church source lists eight marasia around the castle, each named for its parish: Metropolis, Agia Anastasia, Agios Nikolaos, Agioi Anargyroi, Agios Ioannis, Agios Georgios Pano and Kato, and Neochori (the "new village", to the north-west, where the European consuls lived). Greek churches and schools in these suburbs could only be built "after the mid-18th century, when the Ottomans gave permission": Agioi Anargyroi 1738, the Metropolis 1750, Agios Nikolaos 1759. The Metropolitan, restored by the Ottomans after the Knights had replaced him with a Latin archbishop, sat in the Metropolis suburb as "ethnarch, judge and educator" of the Greeks (Orthodoxia News Agency; Wikipedia, Metropolis of Rhodes). A figure repeated on Greek sites for 1854: 5,500 Ottomans and 1,000 Jews inside the Castle, 5,000 Greeks in the marasia and 47 villages (treat as a period estimate, not a census).

**The sunset rule, in a witness's words.** The Oxford geographer H. F. Tozer landed at Rhodes in April 1874, rowed ashore "by two Turks, two Greeks, and a Jew, a crew that well represented the nationalities which inhabit the place", and was taken to "the suburb of Neo-Maras... the residence of the foreign consuls and the Roman Catholic population." Then:

> "The gates of the city are closed at night, and no Christians are allowed to remain within the walls, so that the Greeks, who occupy shops there, are obliged to quit the place at sunset. Until about forty years ago there was a further regulation, that on Fridays all Christians should leave before noon, in consequence of a prediction, or a prevalent idea, that the city would sometime be retaken by the Christians on a Friday, when all the faithful were at their prayers." (Tozer, *The Islands of the Aegean*, 1890, p. 200)

So as late as 1874, three and a half centuries after the conquest, a Greek shopkeeper on Sokratous street walked home through the gate every evening; and until c. 1835 he also had to be out by Friday noon, because the Muslim townsmen feared a surprise attack during the one hour a week when every man was on his knees in the mosque. The Human Rights Without Frontiers report adds that those caught inside after dark "were liable to be beheaded" (HRWF 2015, p. 5); that is an advocacy source and I found no primary confirmation of the penalty, so treat it as tradition. William Turner, 1815: "In the city are only Turks and Jews; of the Turks there are about 5,000, and of the Jews 1,000" (quoted at romeartlover, Rodi4). Tozer also records that in 1874 the walls were a closed military zone: "It was forbidden to walk on the walls, and any one who stopped for more than a minute to notice an object was hailed by a sentry. To attempt to sketch was dangerous," because since the Russian war the Turks feared plans being taken of their fortresses (p. 208). The walls walk Rob and Jamie do at stop 11 was, for the last Ottoman generation, a court-martial offence.

## Tell at stops 3, 4 and 5 (the Burgh, Hippocrates Square, Marine Gate): a town sorted into mahalles

**The units of everyday life.** An Ottoman town was administered not by streets but by *mahalles*, neighbourhoods each named for the mosque or *mescid* (small prayer-house) at its centre and defined, in the Ottoman historian Özer Ergenç's phrase, as "the section of the city where families who worship in the same mescid live together" (quoted in Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 149). The 1831 Ottoman census gives Rhodes town **twenty-three Muslim mahalles and two Jewish ones**, all inside the walls. The Muslim ones (with the 1831 head-counts, which Evliyaoğlu gives simply as *kişi*, persons; the 1831 census counted males, so read them as male headcounts) were: Süleymaniye Mosque 84, İbrahim Paşa Mosque 137, Enderun Mosque 125, Demirli Mosque 118, Recep Paşa Mosque 65, Cami-i Kebir 141, Tireli Hamza Bey 113, Sadri Çelebi 63, Takyeci 86, Abdülcelil Bey 126, Sıkındı Abdi Halife 37, Piyaleddin 58, Bab-ı Mesdud 73, Alemnak 39, Kadı 43, Hüdai 116, Yeniçeli 92 (so spelled in Evliyaoğlu; presumably Yeniçeri), Memi Şeyh 59, Çizmeci Ali Ağa 69, İlkmihrap 69, Dolaplı 83, Kavaklı 73, Hanzade 102 (Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 150). That sums to about 1,970 registered males, i.e. a Muslim walled-town population of perhaps 4,000-5,000, consistent with Turner's 1815 guess. The names are a social map: *Yeniçeri* (Janissary) quarter, if the printed *Yeniçeli* is a slip; *Kadı* (judge) quarter; *Çizmeci* (bootmaker) Ali Ağa; *Takyeci* (skullcap-maker, or possibly *tekkeci*, lodge-keeper); *İlk Mihrap* ("first prayer-niche", the church of Agia Aikaterini where tradition says Suleiman first prayed, research/17); *Dolaplı* ("with cupboards", the church of Agia Triada on Ippoton, named from its Ottoman wooden panelling). Most of these mescids were Byzantine or Hospitaller churches with a minaret and a mihrab added and the walls plastered (Balducci, cited in Evliyaoğlu, p. 137).

**Two fortresses in one.** Ottoman writers called the Knights' Collachium the *Frenk Hisarı* (Frankish castle) and the burgh the *Orta Hisar* (middle castle); the townspeople called the whole thing simply *Kastro*. Evliya Çelebi, visiting in the 17th century, describes the Collachium as the official quarter: the residences of the *dizdar* (castle warden), the *kethüda* (steward) and various *ağas*, cisterns under the old palaces, and the wheat granaries (Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 148, citing *Seyahatname* IX). Below it "the city developed around the bazaar on Knight Street" (Evliyaoğlu, p. 133; here "Knight Street" means Sokratous, the bazaar axis, not Ippoton).

**The bazaar's Ladino name.** The Jews of Rhodes called Sokratous street *Arriva del Kadi*, "up at the Kadi's", because it "connected two sections: the Jewish business section at the lower end and the Ottoman business section on the upper end", and the museum glosses "Kadi or Kadir" as "Ottoman authority or official", the upper end having "the Turkish authorities, a Turkish library and Soleiman's mosque" (Rhodes Jewish Museum). That the kadi's court itself stood at the top of the street is my reading of the name, not the museum's statement. Under the Italians a decree of 29 March 1930 "abolished the Office of Qadi and recognized the Muftiate as the sole representative institution of the Muslim community" (HRWF 2015, p. 12). So the walk up Sokratous from Hippocrates Square to the Suleymaniye retraces the street's Ladino name: from the Jewish shops, past the Turkish coffee-mill, up to the judge.

**On the word "millet".** Guidebooks say the Ottomans ran a "millet system" of self-governing Greek, Jewish and Muslim communities with their own courts and taxes. The substance is right for Rhodes (the Metropolitan judged the Greeks in family and inheritance matters; the rabbis the Jews; the kadi's court everyone in criminal and commercial cases; non-Muslims paid the *cizye* poll tax), but Benjamin Braude and others have shown that "no such system existed in the fifteenth century" as a formal institution; the tidy three-millet scheme is largely a 19th-century codification, formalised by the reform edicts of 1839 and 1856 (Wikipedia, Millet, citing Braude). Say "communities with their own courts", not "the millet system".

## Tell at stops 13 and 14 (Kahal Shalom, Square of the Jewish Martyrs): the one non-Muslim community allowed to sleep inside

The Jews were the exception to the sunset rule. In 1523 the Ottomans resettled 150 Jewish families from Thessaloniki inside the walls (research/11). Charles Fellows, 1841: "There are also many Jews, who are each night locked within their own quarters of the town" (romeartlover, Rodi4). Tozer, 1874: the Jewish quarter "contains many of the handsomest of the old dwellings, which are solidly built and elaborately decorated... The Jews are allowed to reside within the walls, because the Turks regard them as too timid to be dangerous, and also to some degree as being their allies from their traditional animosity to the Christians" (p. 203). That is Tozer's Victorian gloss, but the structural point stands: the Ottoman town trusted one minority inside the gates and kept the other outside.

**How the plural town was policed: the blood libel of 1840.** On 17 February 1840 a Greek Orthodox boy went missing. The Greeks accused the Jews of ritual murder; the Ottoman governor Yusuf Pasha, egged on by the European vice-consuls (the British consul questioned the chief rabbi about the Talmud; the Austrian vice-consul dismissed the tortured rabbi with "What rabbi? What do you complain about?"), had a Jewish man bastinadoed with 500 blows and blockaded the Jewish quarter for twelve days without food or water. Then the centre intervened: an Ottoman commission released the prisoners in May, acquitted them all on 21 July 1840, found "the entire affair is the product of hatred", and dismissed the governor for using torture "expressly forbidden by the Hatt-i Sharif" (the 1839 reform edict). In November 1840 the sultan's firman declared the charge "pure calumny" (Wikipedia, Rhodes blood libel, citing Frankel, *The Damascus Affair*). For Jamie: this is the whole Ottoman order in one episode, local majority, foreign consuls, a provincial governor and an imperial court that, in this case, protected the minority against all three.

## Tell at stops 7, 8 and 9 (Street of the Knights, St John site, Palace): the garrison quarter and the school on the crater

Walking up Ippoton in 1874 Tozer noted "a little chapel... called the English chapel, and has the arms of England sculptured on its walls. It is now a mosque" (p. 206). The Collachium's inns became the houses of the *dizdar* and the *ağas* (Evliya Çelebi, above); the Palace's vaults held convicts (research/20). The great church at the top was the Cami-i Kebir, off-limits to non-Muslims until the 19th century, and it exploded on 6 November 1856 when lightning reached gunpowder in the bell-tower cellars, killing at least 200 (Wikipedia, Church of St John of the Collachium). Tozer adds the detail that matters for a story about institutions: "the Turkish authorities at the time of the explosion were ignorant of its existence" (p. 204); a garrison had sat for three centuries on a magazine nobody had inventoried.

What went up on the crater is the most contested building on the walk. Rubble from the church was reused for a **Süleymaniye Medrese** (Evliyaoğlu, p. 138, citing Gabriel); a school stood here by 1876 (Hayta, p. 368), rebuilt in neoclassical style c. 1898 (Wikipedia). It was the last Turkish-language primary school on the island, closed by the junta in 1972 and renamed the 13th City Primary School (Gross, quoted HRWF 2015, p. 9; Hayta, p. 366); by 2015 "no roof, doors or windows remain" after a demolition order was suspended (HRWF, p. 9). What a walker sees is the church's exposed foundation courses in front of the school shell (research/20).

## Tell at stops 10 and 11 (d'Amboise Gate, walls walk): the medrese by the gate and the dead in the moat

Just inside the walls near the d'Amboise gate stands **Agios Georgios Chourmali** (36.444045, 28.222975), the 15th-century tetraconch monastery church the Ottomans called the *Hurmalı Medrese* after the date palms in its courtyard. Its ranges of barrel-vaulted cells on three sides, each opening on the courtyard, made it a ready-made seminary; Balducci records that in the Ottoman period the complex was "used as an imaret" (public kitchen) and that "a girls' school was reached through its inner garden" (Evliyaoğlu, p. 141; Ministry entry in verify-deltas-round2 confirms seminary use). In the Italian period religion classes were still held in the "Hurmalı (Masjid) Madrasah, Demirli (Mosque) Madrasah, Enderun (Mosque) Madrasah" and others until 1944-45 (HRWF, p. 12). Outside the moat on the south, Tozer saw "an old Turkish cemetery, where lie the bones of those of the besiegers who fell during the siege" of 1522 (p. 208); the Italians turned the Ottoman cemeteries against the walls into parks (research/11).

## Tell at stop 15 (lunch on Sofokleous, between Ibrahim Pasha, Rejep Pasha and the hammam): the Turkish quarter and the household

**The living mosque.** Platonos Square, two minutes from the restaurant, has the **Ibrahim Pasha Mosque**, the only one of the fourteen mosques inside the walls (five large, nine small: HRWF, p. 13) still used for regular prayer; it was formally licensed only in 2019 (research/11). Its date is disputed: 947 AH = 1540/41 by inscription (Wikipedia), 1531 (viagallica), and a tradition that it was ordered by Suleiman's grand vizier Pargalı Ibrahim on his way to Egypt, which would put it before his execution in 1536 (Evliyaoğlu, p. 145; HRWF, p. 13); say "1530s-1541". Its caretaker İskender Pekmezci told Hürriyet his family has been on Rhodes since 1526: "We have an association and continue to practice customs like Ramadan, Eid celebrations and Mawlid gatherings. The mosque gets crowded on Fridays." The ablution fountain has been moved from the square in front to "an invisible side area" and the call to prayer "has been forbidden for decades, while the bells of Orthodox churches are not" (HRWF, p. 13; Hürriyet: prayer calls happen but are "inaudible outside"). **Rejep Pasha (1588)** on Dorieos Square: Machiel Kiel identified its tiles as top-quality Iznik and recorded that they "were deliberately destroyed with axes in 1976 during the Cyprus events" (Evliyaoğlu, p. 146, citing Kiel); the Ministry handed back a EUR 900,000 restoration on 10 June 2026 (research/19). **Mustafa Pasha (1765)** on Arionos Square lost its minaret in 1973 and its dome, and since 1977 has been the community's wedding office (research/11; HRWF calls it "only used for religious weddings").

**The hammam.** Beside it is the **Yeni Hammam** ("New Bath", also Mustafa Pasha or Suleiman baths), first named in an imperial decree of 1558, given a separate women's wing under Mustafa III in the 1760s, with a 13 m men's dome (research/11). It is the only Ottoman bath left in Rhodes; the Italians demolished the town's celebrated baths "decorated with hyacinth" in 1912-13 (HRWF, p. 13). Correcting the older files: the Dimokratiki reported on 3 October 2025 that the bath **operated as a public bath until 2011**, has been closed 14 years, that the EUR 2.35 m Recovery-Fund restoration contract was still unsigned with the 31 December 2025 deadline "nearly impossible" to meet, and that exclusion from the programme was imminent. Greeka's page saying it is "open, offering massages, exfoliation and a traditional bath" is stale. **Plan on it being shut on 30 September 2026; look at the domes from the square.** For the birthday, the working alternatives are Anesis Hammam on Alexandrou Diakou (verify-deltas) or Almar Spa at the Albergo delle Rose.

**What the hammam was for.** The bath was the one public building an Ottoman woman used as of right: separate hours or a separate wing, "the hours for women usually far more limited than those for men"; visits "before weddings or religious holidays, to celebrate births, to swap beauty tips", and to look over "potential wives for their sons" (Wikipedia, Hammam, citing Staats and Telmissany). Kit: *peştemal* wrap, *kese* mitt, *nalın* clogs; the *camekân* hall for "tea, coffee or sherbet" and gossip (Wikipedia, Turkish bath). Which days were women's days at the Yeni Hammam is not documented in anything I could reach; do not invent it.

**The house.** UNESCO's evaluation: "building after 1523 combined vernacular forms resulting from the meeting of two worlds with decorative elements of Ottoman origin" (quoted at kastra.eu). Concretely: the Hospitaller stone shells stayed; enclosed timber balconies (*sachnisi*, Greek for *şahnişin*) were hung over the lanes so the women of the house could watch the street through lattice unseen (viagallica; research/11); courtyards were paved in black-and-white sea-pebble mosaic (*chochlakia*; the library courtyard is a surviving example); and the raised sleeping platform in the corner of the main room was hung with the **sperveri**, the embroidered bed-tent. The Textile Research Centre in Leiden describes it: "tapering strips of linen cloth joined at the top to a large wooden ring (*mylospervero*) that was hung from the ceiling", the front panels embroidered in coloured silk with flowering pots, paired leaves and paired peacocks, "the more embroidery on display the higher the economic and social status of the family", made by the women of the household as a daughter's dowry, in use from the medieval period to the early 19th century. The Met has an 18th-century one 358 cm high, silk on linen (acc. 04.3.297; the online record gives no island), the Benaki one of c. 1700 (acc. 7650), and the **Decorative Arts Collection in the Knights' Armoury on Argyrokastrou Square** (stop 17) has one with the plate-covered walls and carved chests of the Rhodian interior (aegeanislands.gr; hours unverified). A Rhodian women's wedding dance, "They have decked the sperveri", is sung while the bridal bed is dressed (Wikipedia, Sperveri). Two doors from the Armoury is the **Hassan Bey mansion**, an early-19th-century Ottoman *konak* restored for EUR 2 m and handed over in June 2026, no public hours yet (research/19).

**The vakf inventory of a town.** The Ottoman Foundations Administration's register for 1301 Rumi (1885) counted on Rhodes "44 mosques, 4 lodges [tekkes], 3 madrasas, 1 library, 1 primary school, 1 middle school, 50 water fountains and 20 Muslim cemeteries" held by or tied to pious foundations (Hayta 2024, p. 369, citing Kaymakçı). That list is the welfare state of an Ottoman island: worship, Sufi hospitality, education, drinking water and burial, all funded by rents from endowed shops and orchards rather than by taxes. The four tekkes are the least visible today; the Suleymaniye was built in the early Ottoman style "when mosques were adjoined by *tabhanes* (hospices for Sufis)" and its side chambers were originally such guest-rooms (Wikipedia, Suleymaniye Mosque), and a mahalle was named Takyeci/Tekkeci, but I found no surviving tekke building identified in the town.

## Tell at stop 16 (Roloi clock tower, Suleymaniye, Hafiz Ahmed Agha Library): one family's endowment, still running

This corner is a single family's vakf, 233 years old and still administered by the founder's descendants.

**The deed.** Hafiz Ahmed Agha, born c. 1740 in Asgourou village 4 km south of the town, palace-schooled and chief equerry (*rikâbdar*) to Abdülhamid I and Selim III, established his foundation in Istanbul on **26 February 1792** and opened the library in 1793 with 828 bound manuscripts in Ottoman Turkish, Arabic and Persian (the "20 branches of science" phrase I could not find again on the vakf's pages; Wikipedia's article gives different figures, 1,995 at foundation and 1,256 today, so quote the vakf's own 828 with that caveat). The charter (*vakfiye*) required "that teachers be appointed to ensure that free Arabic language and Quran education are provided to the children of the Rhodes Muslim community"; the inventory annexed to the deed is the library's first catalogue (fethipasavakfi.com; hafizahmedaga.com). Today 1,265 manuscripts, 806 of the original 828 still bearing his seal; the vakf calls itself "the only remaining private vakıf rooted in the Ottoman period with assets and a library outside the borders of Türkiye", run by the founder's descendants since 1792. Seventh-generation trustee Tarik Tuten: "No other waqf like this, let alone a library, still exists under the original family's care. It's a miracle it survives." The groundskeeper Yusuf has kept it forty years on the vakf's rents, "like a holy duty for him. It's not about the salary"; Tuten wants it to be "a convivial space of *muhabbet*", warm talk among friends (Middle East Eye). Librarians were *hafız-ı kütüb*, "guardians of the books"; one, Hafız İbrahim Edhem, is said to have left to become Mufti of Rhodes (the 1928 date is not on the vakf pages I could re-read; unverified) and was sacked by the Italians in 1936 when in a Friday sermon he "hailed the defeat of the Italian forces fighting against Ethiopia" (HRWF, p. 12). Hours are posted on the door only; a May 2026 reviewer found it locked (research/11).

**The son's additions: clock, timekeeper, school.** Hafiz Ahmed's son Ahmed Fethi Pasha (1801-1858), Marshal of the Tophane arsenal, endowed his own foundation in 1851 and built, "near his father's manuscript library", the **clock tower** and the **muvakkithane** (timekeeper's house) in 1850-52 and the **rüşdiye** (junior high school) inaugurated in 1853. The clock came from Edward Prior, Chambers & Co. of London, clock supplier to the Ottoman Empire; the 170-year-old mechanism drove dials on all four faces and is displayed on the terrace (fethipasavakfi.com; kulturenvanteri lists the tower as 1852 under Abdülmecid). The muvakkit was the man who calculated the five daily prayer times from the sun and set the town's clocks; a clock tower plus a timekeeper's office is the Ottoman way of making public time. The Roloi the walk climbs at stop 16 is therefore not medieval: it is Fethi Pasha's 1852 tower, on older foundations, damaged in the 1856 blast and rebuilt with its baroque top (research/04 pin; kulturenvanteri gives only "1852, Ahmed Fethi Paşa, Abdülmecid" and says nothing about 1856 damage, so that part is unverified). The rüşdiye is the Turkish-speaking school that closed in 1972 and is "now in ruins" (HRWF, p. 8, n. 16).

**The Suleymaniye as institution.** Ottoman sources say the mosque was built on Suleiman's order in 1541 "together with an imaret and a hammam" on the hill of the church of the Holy Apostles; Gabriel argued the present building is an 1808 rebuild (Evliyaoğlu, p. 144). HRWF says it was closed for worship in 1978 and treated as a museum (Wikipedia instead says "closed for worship since 2014, pending further restoration", after works in 1988-2005; the sources do not agree on the date); an application to reopen it was refused on the grounds that it is a listed monument, and it is used "with special permission of the authorities for two specific religious events: Eid and Iftar" (HRWF, p. 14; Kaymakçı letter 2012). Tourist opening is Monday and Thursday 10:00-13:00, so closed on the Wednesday of the visit (verify-deltas-round2).

**The coffeehouse.** The first Ottoman coffeehouse opened in Istanbul in 1555; male-only, it was where the illiterate "sat alongside educated individuals" to hear news read aloud and watch shadow-puppets, and Murad IV banned them in 1633 (Wikipedia, Ottoman coffeehouse). Rhodes' survivor is at **Sokratous 76**, hand-painted sign "Bekir Karakouzou Coffee and Coffee Mill", "the centre of the Turkish community for years" (Greek Gastronomy Guide). Whether it still trades in 2026 is uncertain; glance at the sign on the way down.

## Tell at stops 18 to 21 (Liberty Gate to Mandraki): the community that was never exchanged, and the cemetery behind the casino

**Who they are.** The Muslims of Rhodes descend from Anatolian settlers who arrived from the 1480s and after 1522 (many from the old Karaman principality), from later officials and soldiers, and from **Cretan Muslims** resettled by the sultan after Crete's autonomy in 1897-98, called *Giritli* or *Adalı*, "islanders", who "still live in compact settlements" on a hillside suburb where "around a hundred families" settled a century ago (HRWF, pp. 6, 14; Hayta, p. 365). Because Italy held the islands, they were **not covered by the 1923 Lausanne exchange**, and when Rhodes joined Greece in 1947 they became Greek citizens with none of the Lausanne minority protections that the Muslims of Thrace have (Wikipedia, Turks of the Dodecanese; PACE Doc. 12526). The Paris treaty of 1947 required Greece to respect their religious and property rights; the Italian "tripartite system" of Community, Foundations and Muftiate "remained in force until it was gradually abolished in 1965" (HRWF, p. 6).

**How many.** In 1947 there were 11 imams on Rhodes (Hayta, p. 367). Today: the Moslem Association of Rhodes says c. 3,500, its president Mazloum Paizanoglou c. 2,500 (Wikipedia); HRWF 2015 "less than 4,000 out of 117,000"; Middle East Eye c. 2,000; Hürriyet c. 4,000; a Turkish nationalist historian ">9,000" (Hayta). Say "two to four thousand". Wikipedia's "about 12,000" Muslims becoming citizens in 1947 has no traceable source.

**Why so few.** After 1947 most emigrated to Turkey, in waves after the 1967 junta and the 1974 Cyprus invasion; those who left "were asked to sign documents that they would not return", and under Article 19 of the citizenship code (1955-1998) anyone "of non-Greek ethnic origin leaving Greece without intention of returning" could be stripped of nationality (Hayta, p. 365; HRWF, pp. 6, 19). The junta closed the Turkish-language schools in 1972, "interpreted at the time as a reaction to" Turkey's closure of the Greek schools on Imbros and Tenedos in 1964 "and of the Halki Greek Orthodox seminary in the same year" (Gross report, quoted HRWF, p. 9; Gross's "same year" is a slip, Halki actually closed in 1971). Gross adds that the Greek Education Ministry dates the last Turkish-language schools to 1991, "due to the insufficient number of eligible children". The "Rhodes Turkish Community" (1912) was banned in 1967; since the word "Turkish" cannot appear in an association's name, the community organises as the **Rhodes Muslim Brotherhood and Cultural Association** (2000, c. 1,340 members) (HRWF, pp. 24-27).

**Identity, in their own words and their observers'.** The Greek state says "Muslim Greeks"; the activists say "Turks" (HRWF, p. 4). Hayta's bitter summary: islanders "may not express that they are Turks to the persons whom they do not know well, and instead they have to express that they are Greek Muslims" (p. 372). The anthropologist Kira Kaurinkoski (Aix-Marseille) describes a community in which many "forgot" Turkish, some abandoned the faith, "mixed marriages with Christians became commonplace", and for decades "official guides were instructed not to mention the Ottoman past", with a revival under way through EU norms and better Greek-Turkish relations (SIEF 2011 abstract). The Council of Europe rapporteur found them "generally well integrated" and that "about one third... regularly attend mosque; they seem to belong mostly to the older generation" (Gross, PACE Doc. 12526, quoted HRWF, p. 12). Greek is the daily language; Turkish survives among the old.

**The vakf today.** Every mosque and Muslim cemetery on the island belongs to the **Vakouf Rodou** (Οργανισμός Διαχείρισης Κτημάτων Βακούφ Ρόδου), reconstituted by Law 4821/2021 (arts 117-118) under the Ministry of Education and Religious Affairs, with a five-member council of "Greek Muslim citizens permanently resident in Rhodes" over property "dedicated before 30 December 1947 for pious, charitable and public-benefit purposes" (vakoufrodou.gr). The 2011 rapporteur found the board state-appointed rather than elected, accounts unpublished, and "allegations of maladministration, such as an excessive sell-off of vakf property, failure to fulfil the vakf's duty to support poor members of the community"; the community says that of 600 plots held in 1925 "around 60" remain (HRWF, pp. 7-8). PACE Resolution 1867 (9 March 2012) asked Greece to elect the vakf councils, offer Turkish classes, and let the Muslims "choose their religious preachers and leaders" (HRWF, p. 25). No state-recognised Mufti of Rhodes has existed since the early 1990s (the sources disagree on the succession); since 2012 imams come via the state-appointed mufti of Alexandroupolis, whom the community does not recognise (HRWF, pp. 12-13). Practical consequence: Muslim marriages are registered at the Mustafa Pasha "wedding office", and religious life runs on one small mosque, an association, and Ramadan, Eid and Mawlid gatherings.

**The cemetery.** Behind the Mandraki waterfront the **Murad Reis** complex (mosque c. 1623, admiral's turbe, fountain, the mufti's house and Durrell's lodge) holds Mourabıt Hassan Bey, who restored the mosque in 1797-98 and was buried there (Wikipedia), reputedly three of Suleiman's officers from 1522 (not in the Wikipedia article; unverified), and, per one recent visitor account, members of Egypt's khedivial family (Middle East Eye, unverified). HRWF in 2015 called it "an appalling state... Small buildings containing stone coffins are crumbling... Visitors wander through the remains of the cemetery, wondering what they have stumbled upon, since city maps do not mark its existence" (p. 17). The Ephorate's EUR 215,000 conservation package (approved 29 August 2025, deadline 31 March 2026) covers the mosque's plaster and paintings, the turbes, the fountain, minaret study and walls; the vakf published a letter of thanks on 17 June 2026 after the Prime Minister's visit (verify-deltas-round2). Whether the gate is open on a weekday morning is unverified.

## Tell at stop 19 (the deer columns): the deer as symbol, sorted from legend

Every guide says Rhodes was anciently "Elafoussa, the deer island". I could not find that name in an ancient author. Pliny (*NH* 5.36) lists the island's former names as "Ophiussa, Asteria, Aethria, Trinacrie, Corymbia, Poeeessa, Atabyria... and in later times Macaria and Oloessa"; Ophiussa means *snake island*, and the standard Wikipedia article adds a theory that "Rhodes" itself is Phoenician for snake. So the ancient sources give snakes, not deer. What is solid: fallow deer bones occur in Neolithic levels on Rhodes (allovergreece; also cited by rodiaki), Tozer in 1874 heard that in the pine forests toward Lindos "fallow deer are said to be found" (p. 214), and a 2005 genetic study found the Rhodian fallow deer "genetically distinct" and of "urgent conservation concern" (Wikipedia, Rhodes). The legend that the Knights imported deer whose antlers repel snakes is exactly that, a legend, told to explain a real animal. The bronze stag and doe (*Elafos* and *Elafina*) on the two harbour columns are by an unknown sculptor; one source dates their unveiling to 1947, unverified, and the often-repeated story that a Capitoline she-wolf stood on one column under the Italians I could not confirm in any reachable source. For Jamie's angle the point is anthropological: a Greek town that spent four centuries barred from its own walled centre chose, on regaining it, a native, gentle, Christian-legend animal for its emblem, and stood it where the pagan Colossus is (wrongly) supposed to have straddled the harbour.

## Continuities and contrasts: what four Rhodians would recognise in each other

- **The wall as social filter.** A Hellenistic citizen sorted the city by *deme* and citizenship; the Hospitaller by Collachium (the Order) and Burgh (everyone else), with Jews in their own quarter; the Ottoman by faith, with Muslims and Jews inside and Christians outside from sunset; the Greek Republic by heritage listing, with the walled town emptied of most residents by tourism. Each regime kept the same walls and changed only the rule for who sleeps inside.
- **The neighbourhood named for its prayer-house.** An Ottoman *mahalle* took its name from its mescid; the Greek *marasia* took theirs from their parish church (Agios Nikolaos, Agioi Anargyroi); a modern Rhodian still gives directions by the church. Only the Knights organised space by nation (the Langues' inns) rather than by congregation.
- **Endowment instead of taxation.** The Hospitallers' hospital, the Ottoman vakf's library, kitchen, fountains and clock, and the Orthodox Metropolis's schools were all funded by endowed property, not by the state. The Vakouf Rodou, the Metropolis and the Jewish community still each own and let property in the old town today.
- **The judge at the top of the street.** The Castellania of the Knights at Hippocrates Square, the kadi at the top of Sokratous ("Arriva del Kadi"), the Metropolitan judging Greek family disputes in his suburb: three legal systems within 500 m, and the 1840 blood-libel case shows all of them, plus foreign consuls, pulling at one town.
- **Women's institutions.** The Hospitaller town had none that were hers; the Ottoman town gave a Muslim woman the hammam (her own wing after the 1760s), the sachnisi window and the wedding bath; the Greek village gave her the dowry bed-tent she embroidered and the wedding dance around it. A Rhodian bride today photographs herself at the Kontiki swan (stop 21); the sperveri is in the museum at stop 17.
- **The clock.** The muvakkit set prayer-times from the sun; Fethi Pasha bought a London clock in 1852 so the whole town could share one public time; the cruise ship's all-aboard at 17:30 is the same instrument. The sunset gate-closing that ruled Greek shopkeepers for 350 years survives as the walker's own deadline.
- **Being the minority.** The Ottoman town let Jews sleep inside and Greeks out; the Greek town keeps one mosque open, lets the call to prayer stay inside the building, and appoints the vakf's board. Each majority has told the same story about the other's "loyalty". The Turkish consul who saved Jews in 1944 and the Ottoman court that acquitted them in 1840 are the counter-examples on this same walk.

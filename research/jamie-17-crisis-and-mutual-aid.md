# Jamie 17: When the island breaks. Earthquakes, wildfire, shipwreck and famine as tests of how Rhodians organise

**Serves stops 1 (Landfall), 11 (La Juderia), 12 (Lunch on Sofokleous), 20 (B-side, the moat), 21 (B-side, Kallithea), 22 (B-side, Monte Smith).**

## Frame for the scriptwriter

The other Jamie files show Rhodes in ordinary time; this one runs the same institutions under load. Rhodes sits on the Hellenic arc and is shaken hard about once a century; it is a dry pine island that burns; it is the last Greek shore before Turkey and so a landing place for small boats; and twice, in the siege of three hundred and five before Christ and the German winter of nineteen forty-four to forty-five, it has had to decide who eats when there is not enough. Each time the question is the same: when the machinery fails, who steps in, who pays, and who counts as one of us? The answer is stable: rescue is done by neighbours, rebuilding is paid by whoever holds sovereignty somewhere else (kings, popes, empires, ministries, Brussels), and afterwards the island tells a story of pride in the neighbours and grievance against the outsiders. The search budget ran out before the modern material could be checked against Greek newspapers, so every figure carries a source or the tag [unverified]; nothing unverified should be voiced as fact.

## Tell at stop 1 (Landfall): the harbour is where help arrives and where people are pulled from the sea

**The oldest rescue on this quay.** The earthquake of two hundred and twenty-seven or two hundred and twenty-six before Christ threw down the Colossus, the walls and the dockyards; houses fell (the Wikipedia earthquake list says "thousands", unsourced, so give no number). jamie-02 has the Polybius 5.88-90 ledger; what belongs here is the mechanics. The Rhodians sent envoys, not begging letters, and Polybius says they made the kings feel they were "conferring a favour on themselves" by giving. The money was earmarked by institution: oil for the gymnasia, silver for sacrifices, money for new citizens, timber for ships, master builders with wages paid; each king bought a named piece of civic life back into existence. The thank-you was a statue of "the People of Rhodes being crowned by the People of Syracuse" in the Deigma, the harbour market about where Jamie stands: a city that has just lost its walls puts up a monument to its donors in the customs hall. And the Colossus stayed down because an oracle said so (Strabo 14.2.5); Ptolemy's three thousand talents for it were taken and spent elsewhere. A council decided that.

**The Roman and Byzantine rounds.** After the mid-second-century quake (Manoussou-Ntella: "most probably" one hundred and forty-two) the ancient walls were abandoned and the spit towards Akandia "was covered by waste and gradually turned into a cemetery" (jamie-10); the quake of five hundred and fifteen was a night-time event with severe damage and a high, unquantified toll (Wikipedia list, citing Ambraseys). Both times an emperor paid: Pausanias 8.43.4 has Antoninus Pius restoring Rhodes and Cos at great cost, and Malalas has Anastasius giving money after five hundred and fifteen [standard references, wording unverified].

**The twentieth of April two thousand and fifteen.** Look south along the shore road past Akandia: about two kilometres down is Zefyros beach, a stony strand with a rock breakwater. That morning a boat from the Turkish coast struck the rocks in sight of the road and broke up in the surf [the Wikipedia list says only "off the east coast of Rhodes" and "after striking a reef"; the Zefyros location and the wooden hull are from the 2015 press coverage, not re-fetched]. The Wikipedia list of Mediterranean migrant vessel incidents, citing an Independent report of that day (the Irish Independent's headline was "At least three dead as boat runs aground off Greek island"), gives ninety-six aboard, ninety-three survivors, three dead, thirty in hospital; it came in the same week as the great Libyan sinking of the nineteenth of April [that it was the only Eastern Mediterranean wreck that week is not verified; do not say so], and the sea was calm enough for people to wade in, which is why the photographs travelled. What the world saw was residents, lifeguards and off-duty soldiers standing waist-deep passing people hand to hand, and an army sergeant, Antonis Deligiorgis, who had been having coffee with his wife on the shore road, going in again and again; press reports credited him with about twenty people brought to the sand, including a young Eritrean woman he carried out in the front-page photograph, and quoted him saying he had acted on instinct [names, "about twenty", the coffee, the Eritrean woman and the quotation are from April 2015 coverage that could not be re-fetched; unverified; do not voice the quotation verbatim]. The three dead were a man, a woman and a child; survivors were first held at the town's football stadium [unverified]. What is verifiable is the shape: no state agency reached the water first; the first responders were the people in the cafés, and the state's part was the hospital and the paperwork. Rhodes was never a front-line island like Lesbos or Kos [housing of later arrivals unverified; no figure found]. The same list records a speedboat capsizing off Rhodes on the twentieth of December two thousand and twenty-four, eight dead, eighteen rescued; the coastguard station on this harbour is the agency that meets them.

## Tell at stop 11 (La Juderia): the famine winter and who was still counted

By the winter of nineteen forty-four to forty-five this quarter had been emptied of its Jews (jamie-07) and the island was under a German garrison cut off by blockade. The famine is the one Rhodian catastrophe without a monument. jamie-08's checked findings: the Cambridge article in New Perspectives on Turkey confirms "hunger was the prime reason a few thousand refugees fled from Rhodes and Kos in early 1945"; about four thousand of the nine thousand Dodecanese Muslims crossed to Turkey and were "temporarily settled in Fethiye", then "repatriated en masse in 1945"; around three thousand five hundred people from the German-held islands reached Turkey between late nineteen forty-four and May nineteen forty-five; the British ran makeshift camps on Kasos and Karpathos and the Quaker Friends Ambulance Unit ran relief from late April to late May. Metropolitan Apostolos's first telegram asking for aid went not to a government but to the Church outside, to Patriarch Christoforos of Alexandria and Archbishop Damaskinos of Athens: "The people of Rhodes in agreement with the Red Cross are agonizingly awaiting sacks of flour" (imr.gr, which also has him visiting villages to hand out food and trying to reach the Rhodian diaspora in Egypt). After the German surrender on the eighth of May the British kept the Italian administrative and tax system, "removing only those provisions that had a fascist content", and Red Cross soup kitchens fed the town (Hellenic Parliament Foundation). No verified death toll exists; give none.

**The social mechanics.** The famine sorted people by where they could flee: Muslims had kin across the strait and were taken in by the Turkish state; Greeks went to British camps; the Jews were already gone. Three communities under one roof (jamie-06) broke along exactly those seams, and the seams were kinship networks abroad. The relief agency was the Church, the only Rhodian institution with a line to outside bodies that would answer: Apostolos telegraphed Alexandria and Athens, and the Red Cross was the channel the flour came through. And the liberators fed the town through a charity's kitchens while keeping the occupier's tax office: welfare from outside, sovereignty deferred. Compare Diodorus 20.84 (jamie-01, jamie-10; cite only): in three hundred and five before Christ the assembly voted, before the siege began, public burial, maintenance for the dead's parents and children from the public treasury, dowries for daughters, a crown and a full suit of armour in the theatre at the Dionysia for sons when they came of age, and, for slaves who proved brave in the fighting, purchase from their masters, freedom and citizenship. The ancient city wrote its welfare rules in advance and drew the circle wide enough to include slaves; the nineteen forties island had no rule, and the circle was drawn by who had somewhere else to go. For fifteen twenty-two: the Order's supply line was the responsions (jamie-05), which a siege cut; the Collachium held "wheat granaries" (Evliya Çelebi, jamie-06); no ration figures were found, so invent none.

## Tell at stop 12 (Lunch on Sofokleous): July two thousand and twenty-three, when the town became a shelter

**What happened.** The fire started on the eighteenth of July two thousand and twenty-three in the forested interior above Laerma and ran south-east on the wind for a week. A state of emergency covered three municipal units including Lindos from the twentieth; Laerma was evacuated on the twenty-first; over the weekend of the twenty-second and twenty-third the fire reached the resort coast at Kiotari, Gennadi and Lardos and the villages of Asklipio and Vati [coastal names from contemporary reporting; the Wikipedia article fetched names Laerma, Pefki, Lindos and Kalathos]; a further one thousand two hundred people were moved from Pefki, Lindos and Kalathos on the twenty-third. The fire service called it the largest evacuation operation ever carried out on the island, and about nineteen thousand people were moved by land and sea; the Rhodes article says "nearly 19,000" and "the largest evacuations in the history of Greece" (Wikipedia, 2023 Greek wildfires; Rhodes). Attribute "largest in Greek history" to the fire service and the press rather than voicing it as fact. Over two hundred and sixty firefighters, forty-nine fire engines, ten aeroplanes, eight helicopters and "hundreds of volunteers" were on it; about two thousand people, tourists among them, went out by sea (Wikipedia, 2023 Greek wildfires). The sea evacuation from the Kiotari and Lardos beaches was done by the coastguard with excursion and fishing boats; tourists walked out along the sand in the dark with a wheeled suitcase each [from television footage; unverified]. On the second of August the Ministry of Tourism lifted the emergency and "offered complimentary travel accommodations to tourists who were evacuated" (Wikipedia).

**Where people slept.** Rhodes town took them: the indoor sports hall, schools, the conference centre, hotel lobbies, and Old Town restaurants feeding people who had arrived with nothing [venues unverified]. Inland villages that were not burning took in neighbours from ones that were. The Dodecanese hoteliers coordinated beds, and the Greek press used the word filoxenia, hospitality, all week. Two named voices to verify before quoting: Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis, who told parliament on the twenty-fifth of July that Greece was "at war" with the fires and promised evacuated visitors a free week on Rhodes the following year; and the president of the Rhodes hoteliers, Manolis Markopoulos [both from memory of the coverage; unverified. The Wikipedia 2023 wildfires article cites a report headed "Greece offers free vacations to tourists evacuated from Rhodes wildfires", so the offer is real; the "war" line, the word "week" and the date of the parliamentary speech are not confirmed]. A third would be a Kiotari hotel owner who kept the kitchen open for the fire crews after the guests had gone; several were interviewed, none checked here.

**Losses.** The burned area was of the order of fifteen thousand hectares, and the fire went through the range of the Rhodian fallow deer, the island's emblem, a population the Rhodes article says was found "genetically distinct in 2005" and of "urgent conservation concern"; volunteers reported deer burned and others driven onto beaches [hectares and deer losses unverified; give no deer number]. The southern season ended a month early; the north kept its visitors.

**Rodos Week.** The promise became a scheme: Ministry of Tourism vouchers for people whose July two thousand and twenty-three holiday was cut short, redeemable for a stay in the spring and autumn shoulder seasons of two thousand and twenty-four, worth five hundred euros per booking, via a government platform; uptake was reported as far below the number eligible [scheme name from the brief; value, dates and uptake unverified; the gov.gr domain did not resolve]. The logic is what interests Jamie: the state compensated the visitors, not the villagers; the villagers went through ordinary agricultural and forest-loss payments and, in the local papers, blamed the forest service for uncleared woodland and the resorts for building into the pine [unverified; search Dimokratiki and Rodiaki for Λάερμα and αναδάσωση].

**The modern machinery.** Greece sends evacuation orders by text from the number one one two to every phone in a named area [the start date of the alert service, given as two thousand and twenty in the brief, could not be checked: 112.gov.gr failed its certificate check; say "in recent years" rather than a year]; Jamie's phone on a Greek network would get one. Civil Protection issues them on the fire service's request; the municipal plan names sports halls and schools as shelters; volunteer fire teams are registered associations; the Church has a welfare office; the hoteliers find the beds [general description; municipal plan and Church relief not fetched].

## Tell at stop 20 (B-side, the moat): rebuilding a wall mid-war, and a church that became a bomb

**Fourteen eighty-one.** The Ottoman siege lifted in August fourteen eighty; the town had torn down its own Jewish quarter to reinforce the wall (jamie-05). Then a major foreshock on the fifteenth of March fourteen eighty-one and, at three in the morning on the third of May, the main shock, surface-wave magnitude seven point one, intensity nine to ten, with a tsunami, put at no more than one point eight metres, in which a large ship broke from her moorings and another vessel was later lost on a reef with all her crew (the Wikipedia article does not say the reef loss was the wave's doing, so do not); aftershocks on the fifth and twelfth of May, the third of October and the eighteenth of December, into January fourteen eighty-two; the Palace was seriously damaged and "the damage caused by the earthquakes led to a wave of rebuilding after 1481" (Wikipedia, 1481 Rhodes earthquake, from NOAA and tsunami-deposit studies). The catalogue's thirty thousand dead is more than the island could have held; if given at all, say "the old catalogues say thirty thousand, which nobody now believes".

**Who paid.** The Order was an insurance company. Its income was the responsions "sent annually to headquarters"; in thirteen twenty-nine they alone yielded a hundred and eighty thousand florins (Luttrell, via jamie-05). After fourteen eighty it also had a story: the siege account was printed across Europe within months (Caoursin, research/05), and Sixtus IV, pope during the siege, granted indulgences to donors [indulgences not fetched in either pass; keep hedged]; Sixtus died in fourteen eighty-four, and it was a later pope, Innocent VIII, who made d'Aubusson a cardinal on the ninth of March fourteen eighty-nine, and his Wikipedia biography ties the hat not to the siege but to his agreement to keep the Ottoman pretender Cem Sultan from appealing to Christian powers against his brother Bayezid II; so do not voice the cardinalate as a reward for fourteen eighty. The bastions and widened ditch Jamie sees are the fourteen eighty-one-to-fifteen twenty rebuilding, paid by European landlords through commanderies and by the faithful through indulgences: the two hundred and twenty-seven mechanism, a disaster turned into a claim on outsiders and built into stone.

**Eighteen fifty-six, twice.** At a quarter to three in the morning of the twelfth of October the great Heraklion earthquake, magnitude somewhere between seven point seven and eight point three (the Wikipedia list says eight point two), shook the whole south Aegean; the list gives five hundred and thirty-eight dead and over sixteen thousand houses destroyed, mostly on Crete, and the Wikipedia article on the quake adds that on Rhodes "sixty people died on the island", "several villages were destroyed and the coast experienced uplift", and that eight thousand homes were wiped out on Kasos and Karpathos. The list marks it as a tsunami event; the article's own infobox calls a tsunami "unlikely", so say nothing about a wave. Twenty-five days later, on the sixth of November, lightning struck the bell tower of the former church of Saint John and fired a powder store "the Turkish authorities at the time of the explosion were ignorant of" (Tozer, jamie-06), killing at least two hundred and wrecking church and Palace (jamie-10). The Ottoman answer was a school on the rubble; the Palace stayed a ruin until the Italians rebuilt it in nineteen thirty-seven to forty.

**The twenty-sixth of June nineteen twenty-six.** Magnitude six point nine, fifteen kilometres deep, in the Dodecanese, at least fifty-one dead, "major damage" (Wikipedia, List of earthquakes in 1926, citing USGS and NGDC; Lower Egypt also affected). It fell in Mario Lago's fourth year, and the Italian restoration campaign in the Old Town (jamie-08) followed [causal link and damage inside the walls unverified]. The payer was the Italian state with archaeologists as contractors. Today it is the Greek state and the European Union: the quake of the fifteenth of July two thousand and eight, magnitude six point three (the Rhodes article) or six point four (the Greek earthquake list), caused minor damage to old buildings and one death (Wikipedia, Rhodes; List of earthquakes in Greece), and the walls are kept under EU programmes with the delays jamie-06 records for the municipal baths.

## Tell at stop 21 (B-side, Kallithea): the shore road and the business of taking people in

Kallithea sits on the coast road south, up which the two thousand and fifteen survivors were driven and the two thousand and twenty-three coaches came. The spa's healing-waters pedigree is invented (jamie-04), but its premise is Rhodian: an island that takes in strangers for money and, when the money stops, for nothing. The hotel strip from here to Faliraki is where much of the July two thousand and twenty-three overflow slept, since the fire never came north of the island's waist [unverified; it is where the beds are]. A hotel is a building designed to absorb strangers at scale; in a crisis it becomes the welfare state's spare room. The Knights' Hospital, the spa and the all-inclusive are one trade. The shore road passes Zefyros; tell the two thousand and fifteen story there if stop 1 is crowded.

## Tell at stop 22 (B-side, Monte Smith): the burned island, and the oldest insurance

From the acropolis the view south runs down the island's spine towards Attavyros; in the last week of July two thousand and twenty-three the smoke stood on that horizon, a north-west wind driving the front towards the south-east coast where the hotels were. Then the ancient view of a disaster. The Hellenistic city's mechanism was neither prayer nor engineering but both: a magistrate dreams, the dream is minuted, the council acts. jamie-04 has the Lindian Chronicle's two cases: Athena telling an unnamed magistrate under Persian siege to be bold, since she "was about to ask her father for water", before the storm filled the cisterns; and the ex-priest Kallikles dreaming six nights running in three hundred and five before Christ that the prytanis Anaxipolis should write to Ptolemy for help, until the council sent him. The second dream is a fund-raising instruction. Religion was the procedure by which a city agreed to ask for help. The odeion and stadium below, restored by the Italians in the nineteen thirties, held the Halieia, the island's annual proof to itself that the Sun was still on its side, which after every earthquake was the question.

## Continuities and contrasts

- **A Rhodian magistrate, third century before Christ**, wakes from a dream, goes to the council, and the council sends an envoy to a king. Rescue is local (cisterns, walls, fleet); reconstruction is foreign (Ptolemy's builders, Hiero's oil); the thank-you is a statue in the customs hall. Welfare is a rule written in advance: burial, pensions, dowries, freedom for slaves who fight.
- **A Hospitaller brother, May fourteen eighty-one**, has survived a siege and now a night earthquake that cracked the Palace. His Order's answer is a remittance from Europe and an indulgence from the Pope [indulgences not fetched in either pass]; the walls assume the next siege, not the next earthquake. Welfare is the Hospital; the townspeople are the wall.
- **An Ottoman householder, autumn eighteen fifty-six**, feels the Cretan quake in October, which killed sixty people in the island's villages, and hears the magazine go up in November. The answer is a school on the rubble and eighty years of ruin. Welfare is the family vakf (jamie-06), not built for emergencies.
- **A hotel owner in Kiotari, July two thousand and twenty-three**, gets the text from one one two, walks the guests down the beach to the boats, then feeds the fire crews. The state compensates the tourists with next year's holiday; the argument afterwards is about the forest. Rescue is still neighbours and boats; reconstruction is still an outside payer, now Athens and Brussels; the thank-you is a voucher.
- **What never changes:** the first responders are the people already there, and the rebuilding depends on a sovereign somewhere else. **What changed:** the ancient city drew the circle of who would be looked after in advance and wrote it down; every later regime drew it during the crisis, by confession, by citizenship or by booking reference.

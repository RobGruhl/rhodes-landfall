# Jamie track, topic 2: Who owns Rhodes? Sovereignty, citizenship, tax and residence from the polis to the World Heritage list

## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes is a 2,400-year experiment in the question Jamie cares about: who gets to decide, who pays, who may own, and who is allowed to sleep inside the walls. The same 65.85 hectares have been the capital of a sovereign merchant republic that kept every king as a friend and none as a master; a Roman "free city" whose freedom was a dial the emperors turned up and down; a Byzantine naval province and, briefly, a warlord's private island; the only sovereign state in Europe ever run by a religious order, with its own coins, treaties, statutes and a four-tier caste of subjects; an Ottoman provincial seat where the Greek majority were shut out of their own city at sunset; an Italian colony whose people were handed a "small citizenship" without votes; a German-occupied island that lost its Jews; a British protectorate; and, since 7 March 1948, a Greek municipality inside a UNESCO site where roughly six thousand people still live behind the walls under the eye of an archaeological Ephorate and a national short-term-rental law. The walk is a chain of regimes; each stop below is tagged with the regime it best illustrates, and each era ends with the same four questions: who decided, who paid, who could own, who could live inside. Existing research files (01, 02, 05, 06, 11, 16, 17) already cover the Colossus, the sieges, the langues, the Ottoman and Italian monuments and the Sea Law; this file adds the political-status layer and does not repeat them. Two corrections from research/verify-deltas-round2.md apply throughout: Cassius sacked Rhodes in 42 BC (not 46), and Suleiman's arrival date in 1522 is disputed between 23 and 28 July.

## 1. The sovereign polis, 408/7 to 164 BC (Tell at stops 17, 19, 20 and 1; B-side Monte Smith)

**Founding as a political act.** Three older cities, Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros, "all came together at Rhodes" (Strabo 14.2.10, who gives no date; the year 408/7 BC comes from Diodorus 13.75.1). The new capital was a deliberate synoecism, a merger of three citizen bodies into one, and the old three survived inside it as the units through which a Rhodian was registered as a citizen. Strabo is explicit that Rhodes was "not democratic" in the Athenian sense but that it "cares for the people", feeding the poor through liturgies (compulsory public services by the rich) "so that ... the city does not run short of useful men, and in particular for the manning of the fleets" (Strabo 14.2.5, already quoted in research/16). Welfare, in other words, was a naval-recruitment policy.

**Diodorus's report card.** Writing about the year 305, Diodorus calls Rhodes "the best governed city of the Greeks" and describes a foreign policy that Jamie will recognise as a small state's survival strategy: "establishing friendship with each of the dynasts separately, Rhodes took no part in their wars with each other"; "most of their revenues were due to the merchants who sailed to Egypt, and ... in general the city drew its food supply from that kingdom"; "the Rhodians, having established pacts of friendship with all the rulers, carefully avoided giving legitimate grounds for complaint; but in displaying goodwill they inclined chiefly toward Ptolemy". He adds that Alexander "honouring Rhodes above all cities ... deposited there the testament disposing of his whole realm" (Diodorus 20.81). A city trusted to hold the will of the world's most powerful man is a city with a reputation for institutional honesty; that is the brand.

**The earthquake as a diplomatic event (227/226 BC).** When the earthquake broke the Colossus at the knees and threw down walls and dockyards, the Rhodians turned disaster into a fundraising campaign. Polybius (5.88-90) says that "by laying stress on the greatness of the calamity and its dreadful character" their envoys made the kings feel they were "conferring a favour on themselves" by giving. The ledger he preserves is the best single window on how a Hellenistic city financed itself:
- Hiero and Gelo of Syracuse: 75 talents of silver "for oil in the gymnasia", silver cauldrons and water-pitchers, ten talents for sacrifices, ten talents "for new citizens" (Shuckburgh's phrase; the Greek means for augmenting the citizen body), exemption from customs for Rhodian ships, fifty catapults, and a statue group of "the People of Rhodes being crowned by the People of Syracuse", set up in the Deigma, the harbour mart at stop 1.
- Ptolemy III: 300 talents of silver, a million artabae of grain, timber for ten quinqueremes and ten triremes, bronze, tow, sail-cloth, "three thousand talents for the restoration of the Colossus", a hundred master builders and 350 masons with their pay.
- Antigonus Doson: timber, pitch, silver; his queen Chryseis: grain and lead.
- Seleucus II: exemption from customs duties for Rhodian ships, ten quinqueremes fitted out, grain, timber, resin and hair.
- Prusias of Bithynia, Mithridates of Pontus and the dynasts Lysanias, Olympichus and Limnaeus "each according to his means".
Polybius's moral is that kings of his own day should not expect honours for "four or five talents". Note the categories: oil for the gymnasia (the civic education system), money for sacrifices (the religious calendar), money for new citizens (population policy), customs exemptions (trade). That list is a Hellenistic city's welfare state in one paragraph. The Rhodians took the money and, obeying an oracle, left the Colossus on the ground (Strabo 14.2.5).

**The policy of never signing.** Polybius 30.5 explains why Rhodes had no formal treaty with Rome for nearly 140 years despite fighting beside her: "As they wished none of the kings and princes to despair of gaining their help and alliance, they did not desire to run in harness with Rome." Neutrality was not passivity; it was a business model in which every power was a potential client.

**167-164 BC: the humbling.** After Pydna (168) the Rhodians were caught having offered to mediate between Rome and Perseus. In 167 the Senate declared Lycia and Caria free, stripping Rhodes of the mainland Peraia that had come to it at Apamea in 188 (see research/16; Wikipedia, Rhodian Peraia: "when Rhodes submitted to Rome in 167 BC, this region was lost again"), and made Delos a free port (167/166). In the same year the elder Cato spoke against war on Rhodes; Gellius preserves his opening, *Scio solere plerisque hominibus rebus secundis atque prolixis atque prosperis animum excellere* ("I know that most men, when things go well and prosperously, are wont to grow proud"), his argument that no one thinks it fair *poenas dare ob eam rem* merely for having wished something, and his riposte to the charge of arrogance: *Rodiensis superbos esse aiunt, id obiectantes quod mihi et liberis meis minime dici velim* ("They say the Rhodians are proud, a reproach I should least like made against me and my children") (Gellius 6.3). The Rhodians then put their own pro-Perseus politicians on trial; against Deinon and Polyaratus "both the letters of Perseus to them and theirs to him had been captured and published, and the emissaries employed on both sides had fallen into the hands of the Romans" (Polybius 30.8). In 164 the envoy Astymedes itemised the damage to the Senate: Caunus, "bought from Ptolemy's generals for two hundred talents", and Stratoniceia had together yielded "an annual revenue of a hundred and twenty talents", now gone; and because Rome had "made Delos a free port", the Rhodian harbour dues, "in former times ... farmed for a million drachmae, ... now fetch only a hundred and fifty thousand" (Polybius 30.31). Only after Tiberius Gracchus testified that Rhodes "had obeyed all the decrees of the senate" and punished the guilty was "the alliance with Rome ... made" (Polybius 30.31; Wikipedia dates it 164 BC). An 85 per cent collapse in customs revenue is the moment the merchant republic became a client.

**42 BC: Cassius.** Appian (Civil Wars 4.65-74) gives the anthropology of a city dying politically. Rhodes sent envoys to Cassius at Myndus; his old Greek teacher Archelaus begged, "O friend of the Greeks, do not subvert a Greek city" (White's translation). Cassius answered that Rhodes had broken its treaty by siding with Dolabella and that the Senate they invoked was "either in flight or held captive". Inside the city "the more prudent Rhodians were still more alarmed, but the multitude were excited by two public speakers named Alexander and Mnaseas" who recalled the victories over Demetrius and Mithridates. Thirty-three Rhodian ships fought at Myndus and lost. The city fell when "those of the citizens who were favourable to him had opened the small gates". Cassius "took his seat on the tribunal and planted a spear by the side of it" (the sign of conquest), "summoned by name about fifty citizens" and executed them, banished about twenty-five, took the temple and public treasuries, and ordered every private person to bring in all gold and silver, "proclaiming death to those who should conceal it, together with a reward of one-tenth to informers" and freedom to slaves who informed. Plutarch (Brutus 32) puts the haul at "about eight hundred talents" from individuals plus a fine of "five hundred talents more" on the city, and contrasts Brutus, who "exacted only a hundred and fifty talents from the Lycians". Cassius left Lucius Varus with a garrison. Who decided now: a Roman with a spear beside his chair. (Appian's larger total, often quoted as 8,500 talents, was not confirmed in the fetched translation; use Plutarch's figures.)

*Who decided:* the citizen assembly and the prytaneis (presiding magistrates serving six months: Polybius 27.7 has Stratocles as "prytanis for the second half-year"; Polybius 13.5 also names the prytaneis), with the naval aristocracy dominant. *Who paid:* merchants via harbour dues (the million-drachma figure), the rich via liturgies, allies via gifts. *Who could own:* citizens; foreigners and the many private associations (koina) attested in Rhodian inscriptions held property by permission, a point to hedge as "epigraphically attested" rather than quantified. *Who lived inside:* citizens, metics, slaves, sailors; the dockyards were the one forbidden zone, "death was the penalty for any person who spied on them or passed inside them" (Strabo 14.2.5).

## 2. Rome's free city and provincial town, 164 BC to AD 654 (Tell at stops 19, 20 and 1)

Rhodes kept the legal status of a *civitas libera*, an allied city that ran its own courts and coined its own money but had no independent foreign policy. Freedom was revocable. In AD 44 "the Rhodians were deprived of their liberty because they had impaled some Romans" (Cassius Dio 60.24). In AD 53 the sixteen-year-old Nero, showing off his Greek before the Senate, won it back: "the Rhodians recovered their liberties, so often forfeited or confirmed as the balance varied between their military services abroad or their seditious offences at home" (Tacitus, Annals 12.58; the same session freed Ilium from taxes and gave earthquake-struck Apamea five years' remission). Then Vespasian "made provinces of Achaia, Lycia, Rhodes, Byzantium and Samos, taking away their freedom" (Suetonius, Vespasian 8.4), around AD 74. Under the late-Roman provincial reorganisation (conventionally credited to Diocletian's reforms of the 290s; the Wikipedia article gives no founding date) Rhodes became the capital of the *Provincia Insularum*, "whose governor had the modest rank of praeses (hegemon in Greek)", a province of twenty cities including Chios, Kos, Samos, Mytilene and the Cyclades, and the see of a metropolitan with eleven suffragan bishoprics (Wikipedia, Insulae). The one thing Rhodes exported that Rome could not repeal was its maritime custom: the Digest's "I am indeed lord of the world, but the law is lord of the sea; let it be judged by the sea-law of the Rhodians" (Digest 14.2.9; see research/16 for the caveats about the later Byzantine text).

*Who decided:* Rome, through a governor after Vespasian; the city council (boule) and magistrates survived for local matters. *Who paid:* free cities were in principle immune from tribute, provincials paid it; Cassius's "ten years' tribute" demanded from Asia (Appian 4.74) shows the sums at stake. *Who lived inside:* the same population, now including a Christian community (Paul touched here, Acts 21:1).

## 3. Byzantine theme, Arab base, warlord island, 654 to 1310 (Tell at stops 9, 6 and 22)

The Arab raids of 654 and 673 (the second a seven-year occupation, with 12,000 troops rotated to Syria and wheat sown on the island, withdrawn 679/80; research/17) turned Rhodes from a provincial capital into a fortress: the Palace hill was walled as a citadel in the late seventh century. The island then belonged to the Cibyrrhaeot theme, "the Byzantine Empire's first and most important naval theme", whose job was "to provide ships and troops for the Byzantine navy"; its strategos, first attested in 731/2, drew "an annual salary of 10 pounds of gold" and sat, probably, at Attaleia on the Anatolian coast (Wikipedia, Cibyrrhaeot Theme). Rhodes was a district of that province, which was abolished by Manuel I Komnenos in the twelfth century. Political decisions were made in Constantinople; the islanders paid land tax and served the fleet; the Orthodox metropolitan of Rhodes was a real local power.

After 1204 the centre vanished and a local magnate took over: Leo Gabalas, "Caesar, Lord of Rhodes and the Cyclades", whose coins say "servant of the emperor" on one face and "Caesar" on the other, then his brother John, whom the sources call "Master of Rhodes" rather than Caesar (whether John's coins kept the "servant" formula is not stated in the source fetched; he still called in Nicaean troops in 1248); the Genoese night-raid of 1248 and the Nicaean recovery of 1250 are in research/17. The lesson for stop 9: sovereignty on Rhodes has repeatedly been improvised by whoever held the citadel when the empire looked away.

## 4. The Hospitaller state, 1310 to 1522: a monastery with a mint, a fleet and four classes of subject (Tell at stops 7, 9, 4, 6, 12, 13 and 2)

**A religious order as a state.** The Order's legal independence predates Rhodes. Paschal II's bull *Pie postulatio voluntatis* (15 February 1113) placed the Hospital "under the protection of the Apostolic See", ruled that "at your death no man shall be appointed in your place ... but him only who shall ... have been duly elected by the professed brethren", and let it keep its tithes "all opposition on the part of the bishops and their clergy notwithstanding" (Wikipedia, Pie postulatio voluntatis). So the Order answered to the Pope alone; on Rhodes it acquired the one attribute it had lacked, territory, and immediately behaved like a prince. Coins: "the Order began striking coins only after occupying Rhodes and became a sovereign power"; Foulques de Villaret's first grosso shows him "kneeling barefoot before a patriarchal double cross"; Helion de Villeneuve (1319-46) switched to the Italian gigliato standard; Dieudonne de Gozon (1346-53) struck the first gold ducat on the Venetian zecchino model (CoinWeek for Villaret and Gozon; Wikipedia's Gigliato article for Villeneuve, who "replaced [the Greek-influenced coinage] with coins having the weight standard of the gigliato"). The Order's own documents price things in "Rhodian ducats" and aspers, 36.5 aspers to the ducat in 1449 (Coureas 2022, p. 201). Treaties: Naillac "concluded a treaty with the Sultan of Egypt" (Wikipedia, Philibert de Naillac; the 1403 date and the claim that it was never ratified were not found in the sources fetched, so say "around 1403" and drop "never ratified"); d'Aubusson's 1482 deal with Bayezid II to hold the pretender Cem for 40,000 ducats a year is in research/06.

**How the head of state was chosen.** The Grand Master was elected for life by the professed brethren (the 1113 principle) through an electoral college drawn from the langues; the exact mechanics of the college (numbers, the inclusion of a chaplain and a sergeant) are in the statutes and could not be verified online, so script the principle, not the arithmetic. What is verifiable is how contested it was. In 1317 the knights, objecting to Villaret's "increasing despotism and extravagance", tried to kill him; he fled to Lindos and the Order elected Maurice of Pagnac; Pope John XXII rejected Pagnac's election in 1319, pressured Villaret to resign, and by June 1319 both were replaced by Helion de Villeneuve, with Pagnac compensated with half the preceptory of Cyprus (Wikipedia, Maurice of Pagnac). That is papal overlordship in action: the Pope could unmake two masters and install a third. Later: Guy de Blanchefort was elected in 1512 while in Nice and died at sea before reaching Rhodes; Fabrizio del Carretto was elected on 15 December 1513; the 1521 election of l'Isle-Adam over the Chancellor Andrea d'Amaral ended with d'Amaral's beheading for alleged treason during the siege (research/06). Legislation came from the Chapter General, which "met in theory every five years" and "was sovereign in all legislative and disciplinary matters" (Catholic Encyclopedia via nobility.org, research/06); the fourteenth-century *Capitula Rodi* and the *Pragmaticae Rhodiae* passed by the Chapter General in February 1510 are the island's law codes (Coureas, pp. 204-5).

**Four classes of subject.** The Order's Rhodian archive (the *Libri Bullarum*, now in Malta, edited by Tsirpanlis 1995 and Luttrell and O'Malley 2019) lets us see the population as the Order saw it, and Coureas's 2022 study of women in those documents gives the categories with names attached:
- *Slaves*, bought and sold in a Rhodian slave market (Bulgarians, Russians, Circassians from Caffa, "Saracens", black Africans); manumission "in common form" made the freed person a "Roman citizen ... as if you had been born of free parents" (pp. 181, 200).
- *Serfs* (Greek *paroikoi*, Latin *servi*), "invariably Greek", tied to a *casale* and liable to the *servitudo marina*, compulsory rowing on the Order's galleys, and to the *morty* tax on a deceased serf's land and the *ius dominationis* (pp. 184-5, 198). Fugitive serfs were hunted and returned (p. 185).
- *Francomati*, freed persons "with limited obligations" (p. 187), and *marinarii*, free people registered with the *baiulus* (bailiff) of Rhodes and the Admiral as liable for galley service. In 1453 Katerina, daughter of Michael Kaloxenos, a *marinarius* of Koskinou (the village that names stop 12's gate), was moved from serf to *marinaria* so that a free man would marry her (p. 188).
- *Burgesses and citizens of the Town of Rhodes*, Latin and Greek: Antonio de Murro, "a burgess and citizen", with a Jewish wife (p. 195); Greek notables as *omotes* (jurors) in the Greek church courts (p. 197); and Nicholas Gorgostares, "the eminent citizen of the town of Rhodes ... a doctor of law and a judge of the Order for criminal cases", who in 1453 married his Greek serf mistress and got their children "Roman citizenship" (p. 188). A Greek could rise to be a judge of the Order.
Mixed marriages exposed the seams: the *Capitula Rodi* said the children of a free Latin man and a Greek woman were free, but a marginal addition ruled that children of Greek women under the *servitudo marina* "would be liable to perform it and had to live in the borgo even if their father was a free Latin" (p. 205). Free women, serfs and their dependants were forbidden to leave Rhodes without the Grand Master's licence, on pain of forfeiture of goods (p. 206).

**Courts (stop 4, the Castellania).** A 1391 case, Anna Stratigissa v. Nicolaos Chrysopoulos, went before "magister Ameliarius, a judge ordinary in the Rhodian curia", then "Buchius de Mutis, a judge of appeals", then to the Latin archbishop, then to the Grand Master, who annulled both judgments and ordered a retrial (p. 193). There was a "court of the first instance" with a doctor-of-laws judge (Giovanni Rocundo, 1440s) and a "court of appeal" (pp. 195-6). The Order's *commercium* or customs house registered deeds of sale (p. 183). The Castellania building on Hippocrates Square, dated by its arms to d'Aubusson's time, is the surviving seat of this commercial-legal machinery; which of these named courts sat in it is not stated in the sources fetched.

**Property.** The Order owned the island and granted land in emphyteusis (perpetual lease) for symbolic rents: a plot in the borgo went in 1446 to refugees from Kastellorizo for "1.5 aspers to the Grand Master on 8 September, the birthday of the Virgin Mary" (p. 196); a vineyard at Asgourou to a Greek woman, Doukaina, for ten aspers a year (p. 191). Women, Greek and Latin, held land, houses, hospices, windmills on the harbour mole and even fiefs (pp. 190-4). The Grand Master used the "customary census" and a 1367 statute to grant lands to laymen (p. 194). Compensation was paid when the Order damaged private property, as in 1429 when Eudoxia tou Pagomenou's plot near the St Athanasius gate was ruined "on account of the quarrying of stones for the repair of the towers and ships of the Town of Rhodes" (p. 194).

**Taxes.** The Order lived mainly on *responsions* sent by its European priories, but Rhodes itself yielded customs: in 1471-74 the Genoese Tobias Lomelino and the Catalan Bartolomeu de Parets farmed "the Order's incomes from the customs dues of the *gabelle* and the *commercium*" (p. 206). Serfs paid the *morty* and labour; the Jewish community paid "the customary tax ... amounting to 50 Rhodian florins per annum" (p. 200); in 1453 the treasury tried to force a loan from the Jewish couple Ezra and Rebecca, who appealed to the Grand Master and won an exemption (p. 199).

**Who could live inside.** The Collachium was reserved for the Order: "only members of the Order were allowed to remain in the Collachium overnight" (research/06), though lay hospices existed there, one owned by a woman, Sophia daughter of John Diastrique (p. 192), and the Order paid monthly wheat allowances to laypeople resident "in the castellum" for services rendered, still in 1517 to the widow Katherina tou Sinademi and her seven children (p. 203). Greeks, Latins, Syrians, Cypriot fugitives and Jews lived in the Burgh (stops 2, 3, 13). Jews had "Upper and Lower Jewish quarters"; Ezra and Rebecca were specially licensed "to circulate freely between the Upper and Lower Jewish quarters ... even during the hours of bellringing" and "to move around at night bearing a light" to visit baths, pharmacist or doctor (p. 200), which tells you the ordinary rule: a curfew bell and no movement after it without a light. The Orthodox metropolitan had been expelled and replaced by a Latin archbishop; the Orthodox were run by "a council comprising local priests and secular potentates", and the Grand Master appointed abbots and licensed Greek clerics and notaries (research/17; Coureas p. 197). Population at the conquest "had dwindled to some 10,000" and Latin settlers were recruited (research/17).

*Who decided:* the Grand Master in council, the Chapter General, and above them the Pope. *Who paid:* European priories, harbour customs, serfs, Jews. *Who could own:* anyone free, including Greek women, as leaseholders of the Order. *Who lived inside:* Order in the Collachium; everyone else in the Burgh under a curfew bell.

## 5. The Ottoman sanjak, 1522 to 1912 (Tell at stops 12, 5, 16, 13 and 14)

**The terms.** The capitulation of 22 December 1522 gave the Knights twelve days to leave with "their weapons, valuables, and religious icons"; "islanders who wished to leave could do so at any time within a three-year period. No church would be desecrated or turned into a mosque. Those remaining on the island would be free of Ottoman taxation for five years" (Wikipedia, Siege of Rhodes 1522). Some 4,000 Christian civilians sailed with the Order on 1 January 1523 (research/16). The church clause did not survive contact with reality: St John's became the main mosque and the Suleymaniye rose on a church site (research/11). Suleiman inspected the city through the Koskinou gate on 29 December (Porter, cited in verify-deltas).

**Status.** Rhodes was first "the seat of a beylerbey" and only in 1546 was it "subordinated to the Eyalet of the Archipelago as a sub-province (sanjak)", the maritime province of the Kapudan Pasha; in 1849 it "became officially the pasha-sanjak of the Archipelago province"; the provincial capital wandered to Kale-i Sultaniye in 1867, back to Rhodes in 1877, to Chios in 1880 and finally to Rhodes in 1888; in 1912 the sanjak "comprised the kazas of Rodos itself, Kasot (Kasos), Mis (Kastellorizo), Sombeki (Symi), Kerpe (Karpathos), and Istankoy (Kos)" (Wikipedia, Sanjak of Rhodes). Most of the smaller Dodecanese paid "a special tax in return for a special" autonomous status; Rhodes and Kos, the two islands with Turkish settlement, were under "direct Ottoman rule" (Wikipedia, Dodecanese, which states this explicitly).

**The millet.** Non-Muslims were organised by religion: Orthodox under the Ecumenical Patriarch, Jews under the *Hakham Bashi*; "tax collection, education, legal and religious affairs of these communities were administered by their own leaders"; non-Muslims paid the *cizye* poll tax in lieu of military service until the 1856 Hatt-i Humayun "proclaimed freedom of religion and civil equality of all religious communities" (Wikipedia, Millet). For Jamie: the family, the school, the marriage and the will were all inside the community; the state met you only at the tax farm and the gate. The Hafiz Ahmed Agha library (1793), endowed by a local boy who became the sultan's chief equerry "for the literate public of his hometown" (research/11), is the Ottoman civil society you can still touch at stop 16.

**Who lived inside.** The Greeks were moved out to the *marasia*, the suburbs (Greek Wikipedia, Rhodes city; research/11). Henry Fanshawe Tozer, visiting in 1874, describes the rule in force three and a half centuries later: "The gates of the city are closed at night, and no Christians are allowed to remain within the walls, so that the Greeks, who occupy shops there, are obliged to quit the place at sunset"; "there are also many Jews, who are each night locked within their own quarters of the town", though the Jewish quarter had "many of the handsomest of the old dwellings" (Tozer, Islands of the Aegean, 1890, quoted at romeartlover.it/Rodi4). The Ottomans resettled 150 Jewish families from Thessaloniki in 1523 (Wikipedia, Siege 1522). So the medieval curfew bell became a religious line: Muslims and Jews inside, Greeks outside, shopkeepers commuting through the Marine Gate (stop 5) at dusk. Property law was the Ottoman civil code (Mecelle), which the Italians kept in force after 1912 (Treccani).

*Who decided:* the Kapudan Pasha's governor; inside each community, its clergy and notables. *Who paid:* everyone the land tithe; non-Muslims the poll tax until 1856. *Who could own:* all subjects, under Ottoman land law. *Who lived inside:* Muslims and Jews; Greeks by day only.

## 6. Italian Possedimento and Governatorato, 1912 to 1943 (Tell at stops 18, 9, 15 and 21)

Italy occupied Rhodes on 4-16 May 1912; the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne recognised "Italy's sovereignty definitively"; from 1926 the islands were styled the "Governo delle Isole Italiane dell'Egeo" and local elections were held in 1928, 1930, 1932 and 1934 (Italian Wikipedia; the exact date of the name change from *Possedimento* varies between sources). The governor, answerable to the Foreign Ministry, held "amplissime facolta giuridiche" under the decree-law of 28 August 1924: public order, the right to disarm and expel residents, to create courts and appoint judges, with no appeal to the Council of State against his acts (Treccani, Enciclopedia Italiana 1932).

**Citizenship.** A decree-law of 15 October 1925 conferred "cittadinanza italiana" on those who had become Italian nationals at Lausanne, but as a special status: they "conservano il loro statuto personale ne hanno obblighi o diritti politici", keeping their religious personal law, with no vote and no conscription; contemporaries called it the *piccola cittadinanza* (Treccani; novecento.org). A 1934 law (cited as Law 31 of 4 January 1934) reportedly let islanders acquire full citizenship by royal decree or by volunteering for military service, after the governor's opinion (francoangeli.it, seen only in a search snippet; not verified against any readable source, so voice it as "a law of 1934" without the number). In 1922 the islands had 103,669 inhabitants, 78 per cent Greek Orthodox, about 12,200 Muslims (on Rhodes and Kos only) and 4,666 Jews (Treccani); the 1936 census counted 7,015 Italian nationals, most in Rhodes town (Italian Wikipedia gives 140,848 total, English Wikipedia's Dodecanese article 129,135; the discrepancy is unresolved).

**Tax and law.** Revenue came from land tithes, building taxes and customs, 18-21 million lire a year in 1927-31 against 24-28 million of spending, the gap paid by Rome; the Ottoman civil and commercial codes stayed in force, Italian penal law was introduced, and the Orthodox, Muslim and Jewish religious courts kept "inheritance and personal status" (Treccani). This is the Ottoman millet reborn under a fascist governor.

**Italianisation.** Mario Lago (1922-36) is remembered locally as prosperous and disarming; Cesare Maria De Vecchi (1936-40) brought compulsory Italian in schools, the "forced closure of Greek schools" at the end of the 1930s, an attempt to make the local Orthodox church autocephalous and thus detached from Constantinople, and the *dopolavoro*, membership of which was required to run a business (novecento.org). The 1938 racial laws pushed more than 2,000 of Rhodes's 4,000 Jews to emigrate; De Vecchi requisitioned Jewish tombstones for the Palace and moved the cemetery (research/11). The Gate of Liberty (stop 18) was cut in 1924 by rulers "portraying themselves as liberators from Ottoman rule" (research/11).

*Who decided:* a governor with decree powers. *Who paid:* islanders, lightly by regional standards (Treccani), subsidised by Rome. *Who could own:* islanders under Ottoman-derived law; Italian settlers on expropriated land at Peveragno Rodio and elsewhere (research/11). *Who lived inside:* everyone again; the Italians demolished houses against the walls and turned the cemeteries into parks, but did not expel residents.

## 7. German occupation and British administration, 1943 to 1948 (Tell at stops 14, 22 and 18)

Italy's armistice of 8 September 1943 turned Rhodes into a battle between allies; Campioni surrendered to Kleemann's Sturm-Division on 11 September; on 23 July 1944 1,673 Jews were marched from the Square of the Jewish Martyrs to the harbour and shipped to Auschwitz; famine gripped 1944-45; Wagener surrendered on 8 May 1945 (all in research/11). The British ran the islands as a military administration from May 1945; Lawrence Durrell was its information officer. The Four Powers assigned the Dodecanese to Greece on 27 June 1946; the Paris treaty of 10 February 1947 said "Italy cedes to Greece in full sovereignty" the fourteen islands; the British commander handed over to Rear Admiral Periklis Ioannidis on 31 March 1947 (Greek Wikipedia); the treaty came into force on 15 September 1947 (Italian Wikipedia gives this as the transfer of power); Greek Law 518 of 9 January 1948 annexed the islands with effect from 28 October 1947; the formal ceremony was held in Rhodes on 7 March 1948, still the Dodecanese's own holiday and the name of the square by the Nea Agora (research/11). Italian residents were repatriated in 1947-50 (Italian Wikipedia). The Turkish-speaking Muslims stayed: because Rhodes was Italian in 1923, they escaped the Greek-Turkish population exchange, and the community survives (Wikipedia, Rhodes).

## 8. Today: municipality, region, World Heritage, and the housing question (Tell at stops 21, 9, 11 and 1)

**Government.** The 2011 Kallikratis reform merged ten former municipalities into the single Municipality of Rhodes; the island is the Rhodes regional unit of the South Aegean Region; Rhodes city had 56,440 residents at the 2021 census and the island about 125,000 (Wikipedia, Rhodes; Greek Wikipedia, Rhodes city; figures differ by a few hundred between sources). The regional authority sits in Di Fausto's Palazzo del Governo on Mandraki (research/08), so the Italian governor's palace still governs. Minorities: the Turkish association counts about 3,500 members, possibly 4,000 (Wikipedia, Rhodes; the Dodecanese article says 5,000-7,000 for Rhodes and Kos together); about 40 Jews (research/11).

**The walled city as a legal object.** The Medieval Town was declared a protected monument in 1960 (Greek Wikipedia, Rhodes city) and inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1988 under criteria ii, iv and v, 65.85 ha inside a 4 km wall (Wikipedia, Medieval City of Rhodes). Two bodies manage it: the Ministry of Culture's Ephorate of Antiquities of the Dodecanese, headquartered on the Street of the Knights (efadod.gr, tel. +30 22413 65200), which owns and restores the monuments and excavates (Greek Archaeological Service work continuous since 1951: kastra.eu), and the Municipality's Office of the Medieval Town (Γραφείο Μεσαιωνικής Πόλης), which publishes the scholarship (Tsirpanlis 1995 and Luttrell's *The Town of Rhodes* 2003 both carry its imprint) and handles residents' planning permissions. UNESCO's own "protection and management requirements" text and the nomination file returned HTTP 403 and could not be quoted; the Ministry's odysseus page was unreachable. The often-repeated figure is that "the Medieval city of Rhodes, housing 6,000 inhabitants, is the largest active medieval city in Europe" (kastra.eu; discovergreece.com); no census-level figure for residents inside the walls was found, and the figure should be voiced as "about six thousand, by the usual estimate". Wikipedia's Greek article insists the Old Town "is not a museum complex but a living, inhabited settlement with intense daily activity".

**Short-term rentals.** No Rhodes-specific or Medieval-Town-specific rental ordinance for 2024 was found. What applies is national: Law 5170/2025 (in force 1 October 2025) requires every short-term rental to hold a registry number, meet minimum standards (2.5 m ceilings, windows of 10 per cent of floor area, extinguishers, smoke detectors, electrical certificate, pest control) and carry Greek civil-liability insurance, with fines of 5,000, 10,000 and up to 20,000 euros and removal from the register (expatsgreece.com); a moratorium on new registrations in central Athens (1st-3rd municipal districts) applies "for 2025 and 2026", with Santorini, Paros, Crete, Thessaloniki and Halkidiki "being considered" (expatsgreece); the GTP report of a Thessaloniki extension from March 2026 was not re-verified. Search summaries also mention a per-night climate-resilience levy of 8 euros in April-October and 2 euros in winter; treat that figure as unverified. For the walk: the walls that once kept Greeks out at sunset now hold a housing market in which a national law, not a curfew bell, decides who sleeps inside.

*Who decides:* the Municipality (elected), the Region, the Ephorate (Ministry), UNESCO by review. *Who pays:* residents pay Greek taxes; rental owners pay the levy; the Ministry and EU funds pay for the walls. *Who can own:* anyone, but with the Ephorate's consent for any works on a listed building. *Who lives inside:* about six thousand people, plus, every evening, whoever has a registry number.

## Continuities and contrasts

**What an ancient Rhodian, a Hospitaller, an Ottoman householder and a resident today would recognise in each other:**
- *The harbour is the treasury.* A million drachmas of dues in 170 BC; the Order farming the *commercium* to Genoese and Catalans in the 1470s; Ottoman customs; Italian customs as a main revenue line; the cruise berth today. Everyone has taxed the gangway.
- *Welfare tied to service.* Strabo's liturgies feed the poor so the fleet is manned; the Order's serfs and *marinarii* row for their status; Ottoman non-Muslims pay a poll tax in lieu of service; Italian *piccola cittadinanza* means no conscription but no vote. The bargain "what you owe the state buys what the state owes you" is constant; only the currency changes.
- *Whose personal law?* The Greek church courts with their *omotes* jurors under the Knights; the millet courts under the Ottomans; the religious tribunals kept by Italy for inheritance and marriage; today Greek civil law. For 600 years, a Greek islander's marriage and will were judged by his own clergy whoever held the citadel.
- *A curfew and a light.* Ezra and Rebecca's licence to walk after the bell "bearing a light" (1453); Tozer's Greeks leaving at sunset (1874); the Jews "locked within their own quarters"; the rental registry deciding who may stay tonight. Access to the walled city after dark has always been the sharpest marker of belonging.
- *The citadel outlasts the regime.* Byzantine keep, Grand Master's palace, Ottoman ruin, Mussolini's holiday house, Andreas Papandreou's 1988 European Council venue (research/06). Who sits on the hill changes; the hill decides nothing and hosts everyone.

**What would surprise each of them:**
- The ancient Rhodian would be astonished that anyone could own land without being a citizen, and that citizenship could be granted by a decree of a foreign governor.
- The Hospitaller would not understand a city with no serfs and no galley-service register, and would be baffled that Greeks run it.
- The Ottoman householder would find it strange that the Greeks live inside and the Muslims are the ones who must apply to keep a mosque open (research/11: Ibrahim Pasha licensed only in 2019).
- The resident today would find every previous regime's idea of "who may sleep inside the walls" intolerable, while living under the only one in which a stranger with a smartphone can do so for a night.

**Contested or uncertain, do not state as fact:**
- The Grand Master's electoral college: numbers and composition unverified online; use the 1113 bull's principle and the 1317-19 and 1521 episodes.
- Appian's total for Cassius's plunder (8,500 talents) not confirmed; Plutarch's 800 + 500 talents is.
- The date Rhodes lost its freedom under Vespasian (c. 74) is inferred from Suetonius, who gives no year.
- The 1934 citizenship law's number (31 of 4 January 1934) rests on a search snippet only.
- Naillac's Mamluk treaty: date (1403) and "never ratified" unverified; only the fact of a treaty with the Sultan of Egypt is sourced.
- Whether John Gabalas's coins dropped the "servant of the emperor" formula is not stated in the source fetched.
- 1936 census total: 140,848 (Italian Wikipedia) versus 129,135 (English Wikipedia).
- The handover date 31 March 1947 (Greek Wikipedia) versus 15 September 1947 (Italian Wikipedia) are probably two different events (administration versus sovereignty) but were not reconciled from a primary source.
- Old Town resident count "about 6,000" is a guidebook figure, not a census one.
- The 8 euro / 2 euro rental levy and any Rhodes-specific rental rule are unverified.
- UNESCO's management text (403) and the Ministry's odysseus page (connection refused) were not read.

# Beneath Rhodes: mines, countermines, casemates, cisterns, sewers, rock-cut tombs and the town's underground lore (the dungeon level)

## Beneath Rhodes: the dungeon level

**Frame.** Everything the visitor walks on in Rhodes town sits on three buried layers: a Hellenistic grid city with hewn water galleries and drains; a Hospitaller fortress honeycombed with countermines, casemates and vaulted stores; and an Ottoman town that kept gunpowder in medieval cellars until the day lightning found it. What follows is what is physically under the streets, what is documented, and what a cruise passenger can actually reach on Wednesday 30 September 2026.

### 1. The Knights' passages (the locked doors)

The fortress was designed to be fought from inside the ground as much as on the wall-walk. Kastrologos and Wikipedia both describe the three post-1480 terrepleins in the moat (Spain, England, Italy): "if assailants had entered the moat, the moat could have been reached through underground passages that could have quickly been blown up in case of withdrawal." Biliotti and Cottret (1881), drawing on Bosio, add that on his arrival in 1522 the engineer Gabriele Martinengo "avait construit des casemates et percé des passages souterrains pour faciliter les communications entre les postes les plus exposés" ("had built casemates and pierced underground passages to ease communication between the most exposed posts"). Torr (1887) records an even earlier Hospitaller tunnel: in 1480, at the Post of Italy, "the garrison attempted to clear the ditch by a tunnel carried under the walls."

What can be entered: the moat itself (a free, unfenced public walk on the landward side, with stairs down at the d'Amboise Gate, St Athanasios Gate and the Akandia Gate nearest the cruise berth) and the interior of the d'Amboise Gate, where a well shaft survives inside the gate passage (Commons: "porta d'amboise, interno 03 pozzo"). The pentagonal Bastion of St George (Basilio della Scuola, finished for l'Isle Adam; carries d'Aubusson and l'Isle Adam escutcheons) does have an enterable vaulted interior — a Commons photograph titled "bastione di san giorgio, interno" was taken 30 June 2013 and the Ephorate lends the bastion for concerts and conferences (2016, 2017, 2023, April 2024) — but no source shows regular public opening hours. Treat its galleries as locked on 30 September unless an event is on. The same applies to the casemates of the Tower of Spain, del Carretto and St John bastions: look at them from the moat, not from inside. I found no 2025-26 source confirming the walls-walk schedule; OSM tags the d'Amboise Gate as the paid walls-walk start ("Περίπατος στα τείχη - επί πληρωμή") — verify at the Palace ticket desk.

### 2. The sappers of 1522 (pointing at the craters)

Torr's figures, from Bosio and Fontanus: "Forty-five mines were made under immense difficulties from the influx of water at some places and the hardness of the rock in others; and although thirty-two of these were met by countermines, the rest were sufficiently successful. The first was fired on the 4th of September at the breach of England, and destroyed the walls for a space of about twelve yards." Five days later another widened it; "in the next fortnight six other mines were fired, two at the breach of Arragon, one at the breach of Provence, another at the breach of England, and two by the Bastion of Auvergne." Italian Wikipedia, citing Sanudo's Diarii, gives the Ottoman count as 63 mines laid, 13 effective, dug by squads of sappers "in gran parte cristiani reclutati nei Balcani" ("largely Christians recruited in the Balkans"); Porter adds "Wallachian peasants, whom they had brought for the purpose."

The listening devices: Porter (1883) — "By the simple aid of the distended parchment of a drum he [Martinengo] was able to detect the vicinity of the enemy's miners through the vibration of the earth." Sanudo's version (it.wiki): "pelli di animale stese tra due pali a cui erano legati dei campanelli che venivano fatti suonare dalle vibrazioni prodotte dagli scavi" ("animal hides stretched between two poles with little bells tied to them, set ringing by the vibrations of the digging"); on 11 September the defenders found eight galleries with charged mines this way and answered with countermines "riempite di polvere da sparo e fatte saltare seppellendo il nemico." An Ottoman chronicle quoted by Biliotti sees it from below: the infidels, "dirigés par le bruit souterrain, creusaient de leur côté des contre-mines, rencontraient nos ouvriers et les massacraient, ou bien les faisaient sauter" ("guided by the underground noise, dug countermines of their own, met our workmen and slaughtered them, or blew them up"). Q. Hughes (Fort 31, 2003, via Wikipedia) suggests the regular spacing of the mine chambers means the Turks were following buried Hellenistic culverts under the wall. The "spiral vents cut through the walls" claimed in secondary literature could NOT be confirmed in Porter, Torr, Biliotti, Treccani or any Wikipedia edition in this pass — label it unverified.

Where to stand: the 4 September mine is the "bastion of St Mary" of Porter, i.e. the Tower of the Virgin beside the St Athanasios Gate, England's post. The Aragon breach is the moat below the Tower of Spain; the "Auvergne" mines are the moat stretch between the Tower of Spain and the St George bastion (Torr's 1887 naming of the Auvergne bastion differs from modern usage — flagged). Provence is the moat between St John's Gate and del Carretto; Italy is del Carretto itself, breached in 1480.

### 3. The powder under St John's

The conventual church of St John (48–50 m by 15–18 m, granite spolia columns) stood across the top of the Street of the Knights; Torr describes "a large vaulted gallery which connected S. John's Church on the left with the Grand Master's Palace on the right. In this gallery (Circus sive Lobia Conventus) every Knight had to perform gun-drill at least once a week." On 6 November 1856 lightning struck gunpowder stored in the cellars of its bell tower; at least 200 died (an 1876 encyclopedia claims over 1,000 and 300 houses). The Ottomans buried the ruins and built a school; today "only small parts of the northern and eastern foundations" show, excavated by Lojacono (1932, 1934), in 1988, and by Anna-Maria Kasdagli (1995, Panaitiou Street). The "stored since 1522" idea is a legend: Torr — "a statement has gained currency that Andrea d'Amaral treacherously concealed a store of powder in the vaults below … powder could not have been concealed by anyone in such a well-known place as the vaults below S. John's"; Porter — "much more probable that it was placed there by the Turks themselves at some considerably later period." Bosio also records, above Gozon's tomb near the high altar, a painting of a knight slaying a dragon with the motto Draconis Extinctor — the dragon-slayer relic was in this same vanished church. And Fontanus says the Turks in 1522 began a tunnel meant to open "in septo Equitum ad phanum divi Johannis" — inside the Knights' enclosure at St John's.

### 4. The ancient underworld

Greek Wikipedia: Hippodamus laid out the city "αναπτύσσοντας ένα πλούσιο σύστημα Υπονόμων για την αποχέτευση" ("developing a rich system of underground sewers for drainage"). Torr (1885) surveyed the Acropolis: "a gallery about two feet wide and six high runs under the surface for about 230 yards in a straight line … these all end in shafts opening to the surface … for water supply and not for drainage"; a conduit "hewn straight through the eastern limb of the Acropolis hill to a cistern on the east side," fed by another running south to a spring. Biliotti noticed that beside the temple of Athena the Knights vaulted a Hellenic substructure and "l'ont convertie en citerne." The Nymphaia — four rock-cut grottoes with entrance steps, connecting passages, roof openings, cisterns and niches for statuettes — and the two-room Cavernous Sanctuary by the stadium are on the permanently open, free Acropolis site. Beyond the city, the Ministry describes the Korakonero necropolis: tombs "cut into the soft poros rock … subterranean chambers with architectural fronts" in a former quarry, and the Ptolemaion at Rodini, a 28.5 m square mausoleum hewn from the rock (named only from a Ptolemaic coin; Ross thought it Phoenician).

### 5. The Palace undercroft

Torr: "Below this courtyard are the granaries, much like those at Malta. A whole year's supply of grain and biscuit was always kept here; the amount being certified monthly by the Wardens of the Vault." Under the Ottomans convicts were "casematés dans le palais des Grands-Maîtres" (Biliotti). The 1937–40 Italian rebuild produced the present layout; recent study places the temple of Helios (and probably the Colossus) under the palace (Manoussou-Dellas). The Ministry says the "Ancient Rhodes – 2,400 years" exhibition occupies "part of the ground floor and the basement," but "the lower level … is closed for maintenance."

### 6. Lore

Biliotti (1881), on Rhodian belief: "La croyance aux revenants n'est pas moins vive … tel mort reparaît en revenant: Katahanas" — the exorcising priest walks the grave held by two men ready to pull him back if the revenant grabs his feet. The Rhodian kallikantzaros is called "kantharos" (Koumanoudi 2017). No Greek press tale of a Palace-to-harbour tunnel, buried Hospitaller treasure or a haunted Old Town house could be located with the tools available — open question.

### 7. Pervola

Inaugurated 13 June 2026 (Mendoni; PM visit): ~20 stremmata inside the north walls between the Street of the Knights, the Tower of St Peter and the Liberty Gate, with the Hellenistic shipsheds, the Roman cardo and tetrapylon, the Governor's gardens and ~700 stone pieces (4,000 identified; sarcophagi, inscriptions, architectural members, escutcheons). Hours and fee are not published in any source found.

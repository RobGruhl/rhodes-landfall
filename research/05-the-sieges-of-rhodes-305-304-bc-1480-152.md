# The sieges of Rhodes (305-304 BC, 1480, 1522) as on-the-ground set-pieces, plus the 1856 explosion and 1863 earthquake, keyed to the fortification circuit

## Three sieges, one ring of stone

Everything you will walk today is a scar. The 4 km of wall, the dry moat up to 70 m wide, the fat round bastions: almost all of it was rebuilt or thickened *because* someone had just tried to get in. Walk it clockwise from the Gate d'Amboise and you walk through the sieges in order.

### 305-304 BC: the City-Taker sinks in the mud

Before any of these walls existed, Demetrius "Poliorcetes" (the Besieger), son of Antigonus One-Eye, came for Rhodes with roughly 40,000 men, 200 warships, 150 auxiliaries and, Diodorus says, over a thousand private vessels of traders and pirates trailing behind "in anticipation of plunder". His answer to Rhodes' walls was the Helepolis, the "City-Taker": Diodorus (XX.91) gives a square base "almost fifty cubits in length", "nine storeys high", rolling on "eight great solid wheels" with rims "two cubits" wide "overlaid with heavy iron plates", faced with iron so it "should receive no injury from fire carriers", with wool-stuffed shutters over the catapult ports, and pushed by "three thousand four hundred men excelling in strength". Vitruvius makes it 125 feet high, 60 wide, 360,000 pounds. Plutarch adds that the thing "did not totter or lean when it moved, but remained firm and erect on its base, advancing evenly with much noise and great impetus".

The Rhodians fought it three ways, and each is a D&D encounter. Underground: their counter-mine "made contact with their opponents underground and prevented them from advancing farther" (Diodorus XX.94). By fire: one night they "struck the helepolis with an unremitting shower of the fire missiles" until Demetrius "dragged the machine beyond range"; next morning his men "counted more than eight hundred fire missiles of various sizes and not less than fifteen hundred catapult bolts" (XX.96-97). And by sewage: Vitruvius (X.16.7) says the architect Diognetus ordered "a hole to be made in that part of the wall opposite to the machine" and had the citizens pour out "all the water, filth, and mud, that could be procured", so that "when the helepolis was advanced towards the wall, it sunk in the quagmire". His moral: "in defensive operations, ingenuity is of more avail than machines". (Treat the mud story as Vitruvius' version; Diodorus tells it as fire and a second inner wall built from the stones of the theatre and temples.) Plutarch's grace-note: Demetrius captured the suburb holding Protogenes' seven-year painting of Ialysus and said he "would rather burn the likenesses of his father than so great a labour of art".

Peace (XX.99): Rhodes "autonomous and ungarrisoned", allies of Antigonus "unless he should be at war with Ptolemy", 100 hostages. Demetrius left his engines behind. The Rhodians sold them, and with the 300 talents raised Chares of Lindos built the Colossus, "seventy cubits in height", twelve years' work, thrown down at the knees by the earthquake of c. 226 BC; even fallen, Pliny says, "few men can clasp the thumb in their arms". So the birthday statue you came for was literally paid for by a failed siege. Where you stand at Fort St Nicholas is one of the two leading candidates for its site.

### 1480: the Palaiologos who besieged the Knights

Mesih Pasha was born c. 1443 into the last Byzantine imperial family, probably a nephew of Constantine XI; taken aged ten at the fall of Constantinople, converted, raised as a palace page. On 23 May 1480 he appeared off Trianda with about 160 ships and 70,000 men (Christian figures). Grand Master Pierre d'Aubusson had perhaps 500 knights and 2,000 soldiers.

Stand at the tip of the Mandraki mole. Mesih's first target was this tower, the Tower of St Nicholas, key to both harbours. His big bombards fired across the harbour mouth from the ground around today's Murad Reis mosque (the church and garden of St Anthony). On 9 June his Janissaries stormed the battered tower and were thrown back (Porter counts 700 Turkish dead). Then the set-piece: on the night of 18/19 June the Ottomans pushed a floating bridge toward the mole for a night assault. According to Porter's History, an English sailor, "Roger Gervase (or more probably Jervis)", swam out, found the anchor by which the bridge was to be warped into place, "detached the rope, removed the anchor", and the bridge drifted useless; the assault that followed cost 2,000-3,000 men. A German master gunner, "Master George", who had deserted to the Knights and offered his "marvellous genius", was found to be a spy and "hung in the public square, in sight of an applauding multitude".

Now walk to the del Carretto bastion. This was the weak south-east angle, the post of the Langue of Italy, backing on to the Jewish quarter. The Ottoman guns moved here and pounded the wall to rubble. D'Aubusson "levelled the houses in rear of the breach, sank a deep ditch in a semicircular direction, and behind this new obstacle built a brick wall" (Porter). At dawn on 27 July about 2,500 Janissaries took the Tower of Italy and planted their standards. Three hours of fighting followed; d'Aubusson climbed the retrenchment three times, was twice "hurled from the ladder", and finished "prostrated by five different wounds, one of which the physicians had pronounced mortal", a lance through the breastplate into the lung. Then the Ottomans broke; the counter-attack reached the vizier's tent and captured the holy standard. Christian sources put 3,000-4,000 Turks dead that day, 9,000 dead and 15,000 wounded in the siege; the Knights lost 231.

Here the legend enters. The Order's vice-chancellor Guillaume Caoursin, an eyewitness, wrote the *Obsidionis Rhodiae urbis descriptio*, printed within months and a European bestseller. He reported that above the walls the Turks saw "a splendid golden cross and a virgin all in white carrying a shield and spear", later elaborated as "a refulgent cross of gold, by the side of which stood a beautiful woman clothed in garments of dazzling white, a lance in her hand and a shield on her arm, accompanied by a man dressed in goatskins [St John the Baptist] and followed by a band of heavenly warriors armed with flaming swords". Caoursin's rhetorical question: "How else could so small an army overcome such a powerful enemy... unless divine providence protected it?" That is propaganda and fundraising, brilliantly done. The fleet sailed on 17/18 August; Mehmed II stripped Mesih of his vizierate; Mehmed himself died in 1481, the year an earthquake wrecked the walls again. Fabrizio del Carretto later built the great round bastion exactly on the breach.

### 1522: forty mines and a drum

Suleiman came in person: about 100,000 men (some sources double that), 400 ships, 72 siege guns, first sighted 26 June 1522, the Sultan landing 28 July. Grand Master Philippe Villiers de l'Isle-Adam had 6,703 defenders including 703 knights (or 600 knights and 4,500 men-at-arms). His secret weapon arrived on 22 July: Gabriele Tadini da Martinengo, a 44-year-old Venetian engineer smuggled out of Crete against his governor's orders. Against the Ottoman sappers (Bosnians and Wallachians, "forty or more" mines, another source says 50 tunnels destroyed) he listened through "the distended parchment of a drum" held against the gallery walls, with bells on the skin that rang when the earth shook (an ancestor of the geophone), then either blew the enemy tunnel from below or broke into it for fighting "brutal, bloody and close range". Walk the moat and remember it was hollow with tunnels.

On 4 September two mines exploded under the Bastion of England (the terreplein between St Athanasios and St John's gates), opening a 12-yard breach; Fra Nicholas Hussey's English brothers and the Grand Master himself threw the Turks back. On 24 September came the general assault on Spain, England, Provence and Italy; "by mid-day the Janissaries had managed to take the Tower of Spain and plant their banners on the ramparts". It changed hands twice before Suleiman called it off, sentenced his commander Mustafa Pasha to death, then relented. On 14 October Tadini took an arquebus ball through the eye while looking through a gun-slit; Titian later painted him one-eyed, and six weeks later he was leading a sortie.

Then the treason. Chancellor Andrea d'Amaral, Portuguese, prior of Castile, d'Isle-Adam's bitter rival for the 1521 election, was accused after his servant Blas Diez was caught on the bastion of Auvergne with a bow, and a Greek priest swore he had seen the pair shoot a letter-arrow into the Turkish lines. Diez was hanged and quartered on 6 November; d'Amaral, who "refused to confess even under torture", was stripped of his habit in St John's church on 7 November and beheaded in the great square on 8 November. Portuguese historians argue he was a scapegoat; the Museum of the Order of St John says the same. Hold both.

By December the Turks had fired some 85,000 rounds; powder was gone; the Tower of Spain fell for good on 17 December. On 22 December the Greek and Latin citizens accepted Suleiman's terms: twelve days to leave, arms, valuables and relics to go with the Knights, churches spared, no tax for five years, three years for any islander to leave. Suleiman rode in on Christmas Day, by tradition through the walled-up St Athanasios Gate (he had it sealed again until 1922), and on 29 December entered by the Koskinou/Red Gate, "visited the harbour, and admired the massive chain". The Grand Master went to the Sultan's tent (Porter dates it 31 December); refusing conversion, he was told to go with honour, and Suleiman said to his vizier: "It is not without some feelings of compunction that I compel this venerable warrior at his age to seek a new home." On 1 January 1523 the Knights sailed for Crete with drums beating and about 4,000 Rhodians, under a new banner: the Virgin with her dead Son and the motto *Afflictis spes mea rebus*. Charles V gave them Malta on 24 March 1530; they landed there on 26 October.

### Afterlives: 1856 and 1863

In 1856 lightning struck the Knights' church of St John, opposite the Palace, above an Ottoman powder store (legend says the Knights' own 1522 powder). The blast flattened the church, the upper floors of the Palace and much of the Collachium; deaths are given as 200 to over 900. The Italians rebuilt the Palace in 1937-40 and put a replica of the church at Mandraki (the Evangelismos). On 22 April 1863 at 21:30 a Mw ~7.5 earthquake threw down 2,050 houses on the island and split the 46 m Naillac Tower, the square lighthouse-keep that had held one end of the harbour chain since 1421; the Ottoman administration pulled down the rest. Only its stump remains on the mole east of St Paul's Gate, the last casualty of Rhodes' fortifications.

## Open questions
- Exact date of the 1856 explosion: two web sources (Palace ticketing site, frogstorm) give 3 April 1856; some Greek accounts give 6 November 1856 and casualty figures range from 200 to over 900. Verify against Kollias or the Greek Wikipedia article before scripting.
- Location of the 'bastion of Auvergne' where Blas Diez was caught: Porter names it but the langue post order (Germany / Auvergne / Spain along the west wall) could not be confirmed from a fetched source; placed provisionally between the St George bastion and the Tower of Spain.
- Which eye Tadini lost: Italian Wikipedia says right eye (14 Oct 1522, Titian portrait), Historia Magazine says left eye. Right eye used here.
- Date of the Grand Master's audience with Suleiman: Porter says 31 December 1522; other accounts give 26-29 December. The 'compunction' remark is Porter's rendering of Bosio/Fontanus and should be presented as reported tradition.
- The claim that Mesih Pasha forbade looting (demoralising the Janissaries) appears in popular accounts but was not found in any source fetched here; treat as unverified.
- Whether the interior of Fort St Nicholas is open to visitors in September 2026 after its multi-year restoration was not confirmed.
- Walls-top walk hours (reported 12:00-15:00 weekdays, 5-6 EUR, tickets at the Palace) come from July 2026 reviews and a July 2026 guide, not an official page; confirm at the Palace desk on arrival.
- The church of Our Lady of Victory (Panagia tis Nikis), said to have been built by d'Aubusson near the 1480 breach, could not be located in any fetched source; if its ruins are identifiable near the Akandia/Virgin gates they would make a strong extra pin.
- d'Amaral's execution: Porter says beheaded in the great square on 8 November 1522; the Museum of the Order of St John says hanged; the Greek Wikipedia gives 30 October. Beheading on 8 November used as primary.

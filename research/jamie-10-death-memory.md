# Jamie 10. How Rhodians organized death and memory, 400 BC to 2026

## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes has always been a town where the dead are a civic problem to be organized, not a private grief to be hidden. The Hellenistic city put its cemeteries outside the walls along the roads and let clubs (koina) and families compete in rock-cut architecture there; in 305 BC the assembly voted, before the siege began, exactly how it would bury and pension the dead. The Knights buried their Grand Masters in second-hand Greek sarcophagi under the floor of one church, and on 6 November 1856 that church blew up and scattered the tombs across two continents. The Ottomans made a saint's garden of a corsair's grave outside the Mandraki wall, and the Italians, town-planners to the last, laid out a single road of death on the way to Kallithea with a Catholic, a Muslim and a Jewish cemetery in a row, having first dug up the Jewish dead of four centuries to make a public garden. Today the Greek Orthodox majority runs death on a timetable that reaches from the third day to the third year and ends with the bones lifted out of the ground and washed with wine, and the two municipal cemeteries are so full that Rhodes exhumes at six or seven years rather than three. The through-line for Jamie is that each regime built its kinship time and its neighbourhood map into where and how it put the dead, and that a walker on this route passes, or stands on, most of it.

## Tell at stops 1 and 2 (cruise berth, Gate of the Virgin): the harbour spit that became a graveyard

The land between the commercial harbour where the Scarlet Lady docks and the Akandia harbour south of it is a low spit (the "choma"). In antiquity a branch of the Hellenistic city wall with two horseshoe towers ran across it. Katerina Manoussou-Ntella's 2021 study of Byzantine Rhodes says that after the 2nd-century AD earthquake abandoned the ancient circuit, that stretch of wall "was covered by waste and gradually turned into a cemetery" (fig. 3A of her paper). The Ministry's own summary of the early Byzantine town says the 515 earthquake shrank the city and that rescue excavations have revealed "houses, mansions, streets and extensive cemeteries." So the first thing Jamie walks across is a Late Roman burial ground on a rubbish-covered rampart: the dead were pushed to the edge as the living city contracted.

Anthropological hook: every regime on this island put its dead outside whatever it currently called the city. Greeks along the roads; Byzantines on the abandoned wall; the Knights inside, but only for themselves; Ottomans in gardens outside the gates; Italians on a planned suburban road. The line of the cemetery is the line of who counts as "in town."

## Tell at stop 3 (Our Lady of the Burgh): burial in the parish, and the Knights' epitaph habit

Panagia tou Bourgou was built as a three-aisled Gothic basilica with six burial chapels and seven arched tombs (arcosolia), so the ruin Jamie walks into was, in the 14th and 15th centuries, a burial church for the Latin and Greek burghers of the outer town (Metropolis of Rhodes page, already in research 17). The Order's own dead are the best-documented in the town: a study by Anna-Maria Kasdagli is reported to count roughly one hundred surviving Hospitaller funerary epitaphs from Rhodes if the pieces now in Paris and Istanbul are included, Latin predominant, then French, with occasional Italian and Spanish (the academia.edu abstract could not be re-read at fact-check; treat the "one hundred" as approximate), and the brethren "began to have tombs with personalised epigraphy in significant numbers after 1400," tomb slabs carrying the Hospital's arms or the knight's family heraldry. Her 2016 Archaeopress catalogue lists 230 displaced Hospitaller carvings, most in Ephorate stores, the best in the Archaeological Museum. The formula is nearly always "hic iacet" plus name, office and date: an institutional memory system, not a family one. That is the contrast with everything before and after: the Order buried men without descendants, so the tombstone had to do the remembering that kin would otherwise do.

## Tell at stop 6 (Hospital of the Knights / Archaeological Museum): the mother and daughter, the altars, and the clubs that buried you

**The stele of Krito and Timarista.** Marble, c. 420-410 BC, from the cemetery of Kameiros at Makry Langoni. "The dead mother Timarista and her daughter Krito are represented standing and embracing"; both names are cut above their heads; the arched top is unusual and the carving is by a local sculptor working in the Attic-Ionic manner of Phidias's generation. Stelios Lydakis, director of the Museum of the City of Athens, lectured on it in 2007 as one of two "great works of European art" and said "the emotional charge in this grave relief is unprecedented in ancient Greek sculpture" (Ta Nea, 24 Oct 2007). For Jamie the point is who is shown: not a warrior, not a citizen with his tools, but a household of two women, the older dead, the younger holding her. Classical Greek grave reliefs are the one place the oikos, the household, is the subject rather than the polis. Note Kameiros was an independent polis until the three cities merged to found Rhodes town in 408/7 BC, so this stone is from the decade of the synoecism itself.

**Rhodian restraint.** Peter Fraser's Rhodian Funerary Monuments (Oxford 1977) is the standard catalogue: the monuments of the new city are overwhelmingly small rectangular or cylindrical altars and modest stelai, inscribed with little more than name, patronymic and demotic (for citizens) or ethnic (for foreigners). Google Books confirms the book discusses "sumptuary" restriction on two pages; the interpretation that the uniformity reflects a deliberate civic rule limiting display is Fraser's, and I could not read the pages themselves, so present it as his hypothesis. Vasiliki Patsiada's 2020 study complicates it: monumental grave complexes appear in the Rhodian necropolis only "during periods of supreme economic prosperity, from the last decades of the 3rd century until the 1st century" and were built by "an economic and social elite consisting of Rhodian citizens and foreigners permanently residing on the island." So the picture is a default of modesty with an elite exception in boom decades, which is itself a Rhodian social fact.

**The koina.** The distinctive Rhodian institution is the private association. Patsiada: associations "played a decisive role in the foreigner's integration, as well as in the social display of eminent citizens, and had ownership of some of the monumental grave complexes, while others were family tombs." Vincent Gabrielsen published in TYCHE 32 (2017) an inscription from the eastern necropolis of Rhodes attesting "five hitherto unknown private associations (koina)" and three new place-names, one possibly a ktoina (an old Rhodian territorial unit), and possibly evidence for the Rhodian festival of the Dalia (the published abstract confirms this much; the individual association names and the late 2nd to early 1st century BC date could not be verified from the abstract and should not be quoted). Rhodian funerary monuments habitually record the crowns and honours the dead person received from these clubs, so a resident foreigner with no citizen family could still be buried, crowned and remembered by his koinon. For Jamie: this is a burial society, a cult, a dining club and an integration mechanism for metics in one, and the tombstone is its membership card.

**What Biliotti saw in the ground (1881).** The graves themselves were rock-cut chambers, horizontal galleries closed by a vertical slab at the foot, small rectangular niches in the rock faces holding urns of "calcined bones and ashes left after cremation," and vertical pits for large jars (pithoi) "in which the bones of children or adolescents were found." Inhumation and cremation coexisted, and children went in jars. The Italian excavators' grave goods from Ialysos and Kameiros in the museum are the household's last gifts: pots, jewellery, small animals.

**Also in this building.** The marble sarcophagus of Grand Master Pierre de Corneillan (d. 1355) is "preserved in the main hall" (the great ward upstairs) while his tombstone is in the Musée de Cluny; the museum also holds the best of the displaced Hospitaller slabs. A Grand Master's sarcophagus standing in the ward where the Order's patients died is the neatest juxtaposition on the route.

## Tell at stops 7, 8 and 9 (Street of the Knights, site of St John's, the Palace): six Grand Masters, one lightning bolt

**Who lay in the church.** At least six Grand Masters were buried in St John of the Collachium: Dieudonné de Gozon (d. 1353), "probably near its main altar"; Pierre de Corneillan (d. 1355); Robert de Juilly (d. 29 July 1377 by his epitaph as Biliotti transcribes it; Torr and the Wikipedia church article say 1376); Jacques de Milly (d. 1461); Giovanni Battista Orsini (d. 1476); and Fabrizio del Carretto (d. 10 January 1521), "the last Grand Master of the Knights Hospitaller to die in Rhodes," entombed "beneath an elaborate slab in its central part." Biliotti places Carretto's tomb "under one of the windows of the church of St John, on the Street of the Knights side," and gives the slab's text: "R. ET ILL. D. F. FABRICIUS DE CARRETTO MAGNUS RHODI MAGISTER, URBIS INSTAURATOR ET AD PUBLICAM UTILITATEM PER SEPTENNIUM RECTOR. HIC JACET ANNO MDXXI" (Reverend and illustrious lord, Brother Fabrizio del Carretto, Grand Master of Rhodes, restorer of the city and for seven years its ruler for the public good, lies here, 1521).

**Second-hand coffins.** Torr (1887): "the tombs of the Grand Masters de Julliac, 1376, and de Milly, 1461, were ancient Greek sarcophagi." Biliotti adds that Juilly's "antique Hellenic sarcophagus of white marble" was "converted afterwards by the Turks into a basin under a public fountain" before the Musée de Cluny acquired it, and that Milly's sarcophagus was reused "three years later" for "a member of the house of Savoy, Prince of Antioch," an infant, whose second inscription was cut beneath the first (Biliotti's transcription of that inscription reads 4 July 1463; Wikipedia gives the child, Hugh or Henry, son of Queen Charlotte of Cyprus and Louis of Savoy, as born at Rhodes on 4 July 1464 and dead within a month, so say "1463 or 1464"). The GRAPH-EAST epigraphy project confirms the Milly monument in Paris carries "two inscriptions, one for the grand master and one for H. de Savoy, an infant son of the Queen of Cyprus." So: a Hellenistic Rhodian's coffin held a French Grand Master, then a Savoyard baby, then Ottoman washing-water, and now sits in a Paris museum. Nothing on this island says "reuse" better.

**The explosion.** On 6 November 1856 lightning struck gunpowder stored in the cellars beneath the church's bell tower; at least 200 people were killed and the church and much of the Palace were wrecked (research 05/06 and verify-deltas settled the date; Torr: "accidently blown up on the 6th of November 1856"). Torr gives the church's size: "very small, measuring only 150 feet by 50," nave and two aisles with wooden roofs, a detached square belfry opposite the west front. The British vice-consul Alfred Biliotti dug for survivors. Carretto's tomb "was looted"; "marble floor slabs were salvaged" and reused in the Ottoman school built over the site (the present neoclassical school dates from c. 1898). Wikipedia says the four earlier tombs "were transferred to the Musée d'Orsay in Paris in 1877," which is wrong (the Orsay opened in 1986): Hasluck, Biliotti, the Museum of the Order of St John and the GRAPH-EAST project all put them in the Musée de Cluny (then the Musée des Thermes; Gozon's is Catalogue No. 422, illustrated in L'Illustration 1878). Biliotti records that the Italian vice-consul Panni found "among the rubble of the cathedral church of St John" a fragment of Gozon's slab, with his arms and the death-date 1353, "which is today in the Musée de Cluny," and that the famous epitaph EXTINCTOR DRACONIS is Vertot's invention; the separate memorial the Provençal knights raised in 1366 began INGENIUM SUPERAT VIRES, "wit conquers strength."

**What is left here.** Exposed foundation courses of the church's north and east ends beside the school; the Palace's ground-floor exhibitions and the museum hold Hospitaller slabs; and the Palace itself, rebuilt 1937-40, is floored and faced partly with Jewish tombstones (see stop 13). The bones of the Grand Masters: no source I found says where they went after 1856; treat them as lost in the rubble or reburied unrecorded.

**The Order's own death-time.** The Rule of Raymond du Puy (approved before 1153) bound the brothers to poverty, chastity and obedience and set up the infirmary; the detailed provisions on masses and paternosters for dead brethren are in the Rule and statutes but I could not verify the exact numbers from a fetched text, so do not quote figures.

## Tell at stops 11 and 12 (walls walk to the Koskinou / St John's Gate): the vanished Jewish cemetery under the moat gardens

When Jamie comes off the ramparts at the Gate of St John she is standing on the old Jewish cemetery. The Jewish Community of Rhodes says it "began at Koskinou Gate, at 'La Puerta de la Sivdad' [the city gate, in Judaeo-Spanish], and extended beyond the Tower of Italy," and served for centuries until Governor De Vecchi "decided to set up public gardens in the area" in 1938 (the Rhodes Jewish Museum gives a 1936-39 process begun under Governor Lago, with De Vecchi later ordering the stones taken; sources differ on which governor did what). The community was given land "adjacent to the Muslim cemetery near Kallithea," 1.5 km away. Thousands of graves, "mainly those of families with financial means," were moved, often paid for by emigrant relatives in Africa and America; about 18 rabbis' graves were moved at community expense; the graves of families who could not pay were destroyed by the Italian authorities and are now remembered by two cenotaphs. About 100 of the finest stones went to the Palace of the Grand Master as building material. The rest were dumped in empty parts of the new cemetery and sank; in 1997 restoration digging brought them back up, 58 years later. The oldest legible stone, found this way, is that of Haim Provencal, month of Tamuz 5123, i.e. 1363, in the Knights' time; others of 1593 (Moshe Sidi), 1655, 1662, 1746, 1768. Epitaphs are Hebrew with some Greek, and carry trade symbols and verse.

Off-route but 300 m north of this gate, Agios Fanourios keeps two arcosolia (tomb-arches) flanking its door under a 1335/6 Last Judgement in which the donors are painted "in a paradise landscape": a Greek burgher family buying its place in the afterlife on the church wall (research 17).

## Tell at stops 13 and 14 (Kahal Shalom, Square of the Jewish Martyrs): who gets to lie where, and the memorials

The new Jewish cemetery on Rodou-Kallitheas Avenue, between the Catholic and Muslim cemeteries, is open Monday to Friday 08:00-13:00 (May to October), closed Saturdays, Sundays and state holidays; about 150 graves in its north section post-date 1938, including victims of the 1944 air raids. It holds a Holocaust memorial of 1949 listing the deported families, and a memorial to the Pentcho, the Danube riverboat of refugees who reached Rhodes in 1940-42. In town, the black six-sided memorial in the Square of the Jewish Martyrs was dedicated 23 June 2002 with texts in English, French, Greek, Hebrew, Italian and Judaeo-Spanish, on the square where 1,673 people were assembled on 23 July 1944 for the march to the harbour (research 03/11). Two systems of memory side by side: the 1949 stone in the cemetery names families for the community; the 2002 stone in the square addresses strangers in six languages. Ask Jamie which one a cemetery is for.

## Tell at stop 15 (lunch at Nireas): the Orthodox timetable, kollyva, and what it does to kinship

The Greek Orthodox memorial cycle (mnimosyno) is held on the third, ninth and fortieth days, then at three and six months, one year and three years. The rationale attributed to St Macarius: "from the third day to the ninth day after death, the departed soul is shown the mansions of Paradise ... from the ninth to the fortieth days, the soul is shown the torments of hell; and on the fortieth day, the soul stands before the throne of God." At each service the family brings kollyva, boiled wheat sweetened with sugar or honey and mixed with pomegranate seeds, raisins, sesame, almonds or walnuts, cinnamon and parsley, moulded into a mound "resembling a grave" and iced with powdered sugar; the priest blesses it and sprinkles it with holy water and everyone present eats a spoonful. The wheat is the resurrection grain; the word comes from ancient kollybos, "small coin," and the seed-mixture offering goes back to the Anthesteria, the Athenian festival of the dead. Saturday is the day for the dead "because Christ lay dead in the Tomb on a Saturday," and the Saturdays of Souls (Psychosavvata) before Lent and Pentecost fill every cemetery in Greece. The ancient sequence is the same shape: prothesis (laying-out) on the second day, ekphora before dawn on the third, libations of "honey, milk, water, wine, perfumes, and oils," annual rites at the Genesia, and women leading the lament, "tearing at their hair and clothing, and striking their torso." The obol in the mouth is gone; the third day, the wheat, the women and the annual visit are not.

Kinship time: the cycle keeps a widow or daughter on duty for three years (black clothing, the Sunday visit with oil and a candle, the kollyva to make and distribute), which is exactly the period before the bones come up. Loring Danforth's The Death Rituals of Rural Greece (Princeton 1982) is the classic study of how the laments and the exhumation together move the dead from the household to the ossuary and let the living re-marry, inherit and move on.

## Tell at stop 16 (Suleymaniye, clock tower): Ottoman death, a strangled prince, and the exhumation problem

Rhodes' Muslim community, unusually for Greece, has its own cemetery (on the Kallithea road, the middle of Di Fausto's three) and a functioning vakf. This matters because Greek law in public cemeteries permits, and space effectively forces, exhumation about three years after burial, and Islamic law forbids disturbing a grave. A 2024 New Lines report says that outside Thrace Muslims in Greece "have to send the bodies to the other side of the country or ... collect money from their community" to bury abroad at 3,000-4,000 euros; it calls the Thrace ground the only Muslim cemetery in Greece, which overlooks Rhodes and Kos, so use the Rhodes exception as the point: a walled community cemetery is what lets a minority keep its own death rules.

The Ottoman death that haunts the Collachium is that of Şehzade Murad, son of Cem Sultan. He had fled the Mamluks to Rhodes, where the Knights refused to hand him over, converted and took the name Pierre, was given a papal fief in 1492 and married Maria Concetta Doria in 1498. When Suleiman took the city in December 1522 he "had the prince executed by strangulation with his two oldest sons." No source gives his grave.

The Ottoman cemetery Rottiers drew in 1828 lay outside the walls "seen from the northwest," with "Turkish women pray[ing] and giv[ing] offerings to a tomb" (Description des Monumens de Rhodes, 1830): that is the Murad Reis ground, and it shows the cemetery as a place of women's regular devotion two centuries after the mosque was built, the Muslim counterpart of the Orthodox Saturday visit.

## Tell at stop 18 (Liberty Gate, out into the Italian town): the planners' road of the dead and the state's memorials

The Catholic Church of Rhodes records that "the planning of the Catholic cemetery started in 1925," by Florestano Di Fausto, and that "the masterplan involved three necropolis areas: the Catholic cemetery on the town side, the Muslim cemetery in the centre and the Jewish cemetery on the far side. Behind the Catholic cemetery, a wide area was added for Orthodox graves." The Catholic ground is entered through two arches flanking the Chapel of the Holy Cross; a semicircular columbarium in the middle holds soldiers' remains under a monumental cross; Petracco enlarged it in 1939-43 with more wall niches for the war dead; sections are named for St Angela of Foligno, St Elizabeth, St Margaret of Cortona and St Catherine; there are plots for British, French and Italian soldiers; Mass is said every second Friday, on 14 September (Holy Cross) and on 2 November with a Requiem; and the priest asks visitors to "adopt" graves that no one visits. Across the road is the Italian military ossuary (Sacrario militare, founded 1924, 36.4284, 28.2307) and the Commonwealth Rhodes War Cemetery, laid out after the German surrender, taken over by the CWGC in December 1946, holding 142 burials including 65 moved from Kos when that cemetery closed in July 1957, "opposite Italian, Jewish and Turkish cemeteries" at the 3rd km of the old Lindos road. So within 500 m of the Korakonero rock tombs the 20th century sorted its dead by confession and by army, exactly as the Hellenistic city sorted them by koinon.

The Greek state's memory sits on the Mandraki waterfront Jamie is about to walk: the Monument of the Fallen, where wreaths are laid every 7 March, the anniversary of the 1948 union of the Dodecanese with Greece, after a doxology in Evangelismos (the Italians' replica of the exploded St John's, which is a nice loop); and a winged Victory near the harbour mouth for those killed in 1943-45 (guide-site description; the sculptor and dedication date were not verified).

## Tell at stops 19 and 20 (deer columns, Fort St Nicholas): the city that pensioned its dead, and the 700 in the pit

**305 BC.** Before Demetrius's engines arrived the Rhodian assembly voted (Diodorus 20.84.3) "that the bodies of those who fell in the war should be given public burial and, further, that their parents and children should be maintained, receiving their support from the public treasury, that their unmarried daughters should be given dowries at the public cost, and that their sons on reaching manhood should be crowned in the theatre at the Dionysia and given a full suit of armour," and that slaves who fought well would be bought from their owners, freed and enrolled as citizens. That is a welfare state written in advance of the casualties, with the orphan's coming-of-age staged in the theatre so the whole city saw the debt paid. After the siege the Rhodians built the Ptolemaieion precinct for Ptolemy as a god (Diod. 20.100; research 01).

**1480.** Biliotti, from the Order's chronicles: after the failed assault on Fort St Nicholas "seven hundred corpses cover the rocks and the mole"; the Turks were allowed to bury them, "and an immense pit dug facing the fort, between the Arsenal and the ruins of the Oratory of St Anthony, received all these bodies, whose bones were recently uncovered and buried again in the cemetery of Murad Reis (the former Oratory of St Anthony)." So the bones of Mesih Pasha's dead, uncovered shortly before Biliotti wrote in 1881 (he says only "recently"; the decade and the works that exposed them are not stated), were reburied 300 m from where Jamie will drink. The Knights' own dead of 1480 and 1522 went into the town's churches; the enemy went into a pit outside the wall and, four centuries later, into the mosque garden of their co-religionists.

## Tell at stop 21 (Swan by Kontiki, the birthday drink): the corsair's garden

Three hundred metres north along the waterfront, behind a blue door at Georgiou Papanikolaou 30, is the mosque of Murad Reis, built c. 1623 by Ebubekir Pasha on the site of the church of St Anthony, with the admiral's tomb, which drew pilgrims "carrying lamps and offerings" who slaughtered animals by the grave and gave the meat to the poor; three of Suleiman's officers from 1522 are said to lie there too. The garden is a who's-who of Ottoman exile. In the middle stands the domed türbe of Canbek Giray, Khan of Crimea, deposed 1634, dead here "on Saturday night, 15 November 1636." In a walled enclosure lies the poet Haşmet (real name Mehmed, born Istanbul), pupil of the Şeyhülislam, protégé of the grand vizier Koca Ragıp Pasha and of the woman poet Fıtnat Hanım, banished to Bursa in 1762 (the Turkish Ministry of Culture page says 1759; the TDV İslam Ansiklopedisi gives 12 Şevval 1175 = 6 May 1762) and then to Rhodes, İzmir and Rhodes again, for the satires he read at gatherings of statesmen, dead on Rhodes in 1768 (AH 1182, i.e. 1768-69) and buried inside the Murad Reis complex (TDV İslam Ansiklopedisi; one epigraphic study places his grave beside that of Kapu Ağası İsmail Ağa, unverified by me). The headstones speak the Ottoman code: a carved turban for a man, its style his rank; a finial locals call a pineapple (a stylised flower or bud) for a woman; a small domed "sentry box" with barred windows for a senior official. One epitaph reads: "To the visitor, my wish is a prayer from you; today it may be for me, but tomorrow it shall be for you; the late and forgiven Captain Haji Abdul Kerim." Lawrence Durrell, who lived in the gatekeeper's lodge here in 1945-47, wrote of "the extraordinary silence of this garden" and of fallen stone turbans lying like "heads blown from statues." A EUR 215,000 conservation package (mosque, mausoleums, fountain, minaret, walls) had a completion deadline of 31 March 2026 (verify-deltas round 2); whether the gate is open on 30 September is unknown, so plan to look through the railings.

The Kontiki is a good place to tell Jamie about Ottoman gravestone etiquette because she can compare it with the black-clad Greek women she will have seen in town: the Ottoman stone asks the passer-by for a Fatiha; the Greek grave asks the family for wheat.

## Tell at stop 22 (Naillac stump, walk back to the ship): the bones come up

The two municipal cemeteries are Taxiarchis (south of the walls, past the Akandia harbour) and Agios Dimitrios (beside the Wednesday street market Jamie may have passed in the morning). In 2018 the community president told Dimokratiki that Taxiarchis had about 6,500 graves holding an estimated 15,000 or more dead, "each grave houses one to four bodies or remains"; that exhumations "have been set for six to seven years, because Rhodes is an island and the soil does not allow decomposition to proceed as easily as in other parts of the country"; that the ossuary was being enlarged; and that a single regulation was being drafted for "44 cemeteries located throughout the island of Rhodes." Visiting hours posted by the municipality are 09:00-13:00. The national frame: public cemeteries let families rent a grave for three (in practice three to five) years, after which a relative must attend while the cemetery worker lifts the bones, washes them with wine and puts them in a metal or marble box in the ossuary for a small annual fee; if no one pays, the bones go to a common pit; buying a grave outright costs tens of thousands of euros; cremation was legalised in 2006 and the first crematorium opened only in 2019, and about 5 per cent choose it. The US Embassy's 2024 guidance for American families states plainly that "in public cemeteries, skeletal remains may be exhumed three years after interment and placed in an ossuary container."

Closing thought for the walk back: this is the same three-year clock as the mnimosyno cycle and the same act as the Hellenistic bone-boxes and urn-niches Biliotti drew at Korakonero and the Italians' columbarium of 1925. Rhodes has always ended by taking the bones out of the ground and putting them in a small container near the family.

## B-sides (not on the route)

**Rodini, Korakonero and the Ptolemaion.** The Ephorate describes Rodini as Hellenistic "landscape architecture" of the 3rd-2nd centuries BC: caves fitted with niches for statuettes, benches and altars (rebuilt by the Italians in 1924), a stream under the ancient Korakoneros bridge, and the tombs. The Korakonero necropolis (late Hellenistic and Roman) has tombs "cut into soft poros rock," the richer ones "underground burial chambers with architectural fronts, arcades or columns supporting an entablature with metopes and triglyphs, imitating temple fronts," the dead laid "in niches carved in the walls of the chambers," complexes around peristyle courts, vaulted built tombs, cylindrical altars raised on plinths, rock faces with rectangular tombs, ash urns, bone boxes and slots for markers, and a relief "funerary sanctuary"; the Italians re-erected the altars and stelai above the chambers. The Ptolemaion is a rock-cut mausoleum "28.50 x 28.50 m," square, with half-columns on its outer walls, an antechamber and a rectangular burial chamber with carved niches for sarcophagi, "originally topped by an earthen tumulus or pyramid"; the story that it was "wrongly" named the Ptolemaion later "perhaps because a Ptolemaic coin was found by chance" could not be found in the Greek Wikipedia article at fact-check and should be treated as unverified, and the tourist claim of "21 Doric half-columns" is unverified (verify-deltas). Whose tomb it was is unknown; a Rhodian elite family or association of the boom decades is the best guess. Colonel Rottiers's 1826 claim to have found Gozon's gravestone in a church of St Stephen on Monte Smith is dismissed by Biliotti, who shows Rottiers's manuscript source was apocryphal.

**Kallithea road.** The row of cemeteries (Catholic, Muslim, Jewish, Orthodox behind, Italian ossuary and Commonwealth war cemetery across the road) is a five-minute detour off the Kallithea bus and the single best place on the island to show how the 20th century organized its dead.

## Continuities and contrasts

**What an ancient Rhodian would recognise today.** The third-day rite and the annual visit; wheat and honey offered at the grave (Anthesteria then, kollyva now); women running the lament and the visits; bones lifted into a box after the flesh is gone (urn-niches and pithoi then, the ossuary tin now); burial outside the wall along the road (Korakonero then, the Kallithea road now); a club or community, not only a family, seeing you buried (koinon then, the vakf, the kehila, the parish, the CWGC now); and the state's public burial of the war dead with pensions for the survivors (305 BC, and the 7 March wreaths at Mandraki). What would baffle him: the cross, the ban on cremation until 2006, and the idea that a grave is rented.

**What a Hospitaller would recognise.** Latin epitaphs in the "hic iacet" formula on the Catholic side of the Kallithea road; burial inside a church for the great and in a walled ground for the rest; a brotherhood that buries you because you have no children; the reuse of old stone (he lay in a Greek sarcophagus; the Palace floors reuse Jewish stones). What would shock him: that his church exploded and his Grand Masters are in Paris, and that the Greeks he evicted from the Collachium now run the town's death.

**What an Ottoman-era householder would recognise.** Women at the tomb on set days with offerings; the cemetery as a garden of eucalyptus and cypress outside the gate; separate grounds for Muslims, Jews and Greeks; a saint's grave that draws pilgrims (Murad Reis then; Agios Fanourios's icon now). What would trouble him: the Greek exhumation clock, which his faith forbids, and which makes the walled vakf cemetery the community's most important possession.

**What a resident today shares with all three.** A three-year timetable of duty that structures a widow's life; the Saturday of Souls that empties the town into the cemeteries; kollyva made at home, blessed in church, eaten by strangers; the understanding that the dead stay in the neighbourhood, in a box, near the family, and that the ground is borrowed.

**The sharpest contrasts.** The ancient city let clubs build temple-fronted tombs for foreigners; the Knights built tombs only for the Order; the Italians dug up one community's dead to make a park and planned another road to bury everyone by confession; the modern state rents the grave and recycles it. Each is a theory of who belongs.

- Fraser's "sumptuary" reading of Rhodian altar uniformity is a hypothesis I could not read in full; Patsiada shows monumental elite tombs did exist in boom periods.
- Wikipedia's "Musée d'Orsay, 1877" for the Grand Masters' tombs is an error for the Musée de Cluny (Musée des Thermes); the museum's current inventory numbers were not checked.
- Fate of the Grand Masters' bones after 1856: no source.
- Ptolemaion: owner unknown; "21 Doric half-columns" unverified; the "shrine to Ptolemy I" reading on tourist sites conflicts with the Ephorate's "mausoleum."
- Jewish cemetery move: jewishrhodes.org says De Vecchi, 1938; the Rhodes Jewish Museum says Lago ordered the move in 1936-39 and De Vecchi took the stones. Say "the Italian governors in the late 1930s."
- Haşmet's grave neighbour (İsmail Ağa) comes from a search snippet of an epigraphic paper I could not open.
- Murad Reis "1552-1603" vs burial "1609," and the mosque "1524" vs "c. 1623": conflicting traditions (research 10); use c. 1623 for the mosque.
- The Statue of Victory's date and sculptor are unverified; the Monument of the Fallen's inscription was not read.
- New Lines' "only one Muslim cemetery in Greece" ignores Rhodes and Kos; present Rhodes as the exception.
- The exact Hospitaller Rule provisions for dead brethren (masses, paternosters) were not verified from a text.
- Murad Reis gate: 2026 access after the restoration is unknown.
- Haşmet: the Ministry of Culture page says Bursa 1759; the TDV İslam Ansiklopedisi says 6 May 1762, with later moves to Rhodes, İzmir and back; use "the early 1760s" if a year is needed.
- Gabrielsen's TYCHE inscription: the association names (Poseidaniastai, Soteriastai) and the date are not in the abstract and were not verified.
- De Vecchi's Wikipedia page dates the Jewish cemetery removal to 1935, before he became governor (1936); the community sources (1936-39, order 1938) are preferred.

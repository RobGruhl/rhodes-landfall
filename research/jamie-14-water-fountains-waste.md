# Jamie 14. Water, fountains, drains and disease: how each regime kept a walled town alive

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** Rhodes town has no river. Everything that has ever lived inside the walls drank rain caught in cisterns, spring water walked in from the hills, or well water that turns brackish if you pump it too hard; and everything it flushed went down channels the Hippodamian planners cut in 408 BC and that later regimes mostly reused. That makes water the one institution every ruler of the town had to run, whatever else they believed: the democracy that dedicated fountains to nymphs and let its drains clog, the celibate Order that put a privy in the wall beside every hospital bed and then blamed plague on the Jews, the Ottoman householder for whom building a public fountain was "a benefit for the soul", the Italian governor who emptied the walled town's market partly on the grounds that it was "almost everywhere without abundant water", and a municipal water company that today sells a reservoir's worth of dam water to hotels at 450 litres per tourist per day. Jamie will pass a fountain at nearly every stop. This file gives her, stop by stop, what each one was for, who paid for it, who carried the water home, and what it meant to see your neighbours at it. Fountain dates are marked **verified** (a source that gives the date) or **guidebook-level / unverified**; several of the prettiest fountains on the route have no published date at all, and the file says so rather than inventing one.

## 1. The ancient city: drains you neglect at your peril, and water that comes from a goddess (Tell at stops 4, 6 and 17; B-sides Monte Smith, Rodini, Kallithea)

**The grid had plumbing from the start.** The municipality's own history page states flatly that "the ancient city had an extensive and well-structured sewage network as well as a water supply system" ("Η αρχαία πόλη είχε ένα εκτεταμένο και καλά δομημένο αποχετευτικό δίκτυο καθώς και δίκτυο υδροδότησης"), and that most medieval streets still follow the ancient ones (jamie-01). The Princeton Encyclopedia of Classical Sites (Perseus) is more careful and more useful: in the city of Rhodes "remains of underground drains and water-channels of various types have been found, and many of these fit into the same pattern" as the Hippodamian grid. So when Jamie walks Sokratous or Ippokratous she is walking over a drainage line laid out when Athens was still fighting Sparta.

**What happens when a democracy stops cleaning its drains.** The best ancient document on Rhodian water is a disaster. Diodorus (19.45, in the year of the archon Democleides, 316/15 BC, the flood falling "at the beginning of spring", i.e. spring 315; Torr dates it 315) records the third great flood of the young city: "great rain storms suddenly bursting forth with hail of incredible size", hailstones "weighing a mina and sometimes more, so that many of the houses collapsed because of the weight". The infrastructure failed for a human reason: "the drains had been neglected and the drainage openings through the city walls had become clogged" because everyone believed the rainy season was over. "The water that suddenly gathered filled the whole region about the Market and the Temple of Dionysus; and then, as the flood was already advancing to the Temple of Asclepius, all were struck with fear." The city was saved only when "the walls gave way over a long stretch" and the water poured into the sea; "more than five hundred persons lost their lives." Torr's gloss (Rhodes in Ancient Times, p. 54): "There were channels under the walls to prevent these floods; but the people had not kept them clear, as they fancied the winter rains were over." For Jamie: the same sloping site that let the citizens watch Demetrius's fleet from their roofs (Diodorus 20.83) funnelled every storm to the harbour quarter where she is standing at stop 4, and the polis's answer was not a god but a maintenance schedule. The lesson was learned: a decade later, under Demetrius's siege, the Rhodians had the walls by the sea raised.

**The scale of a water-state.** For what the Rhodian city-states could build, the B-side comparison is Kameiros, where "a covered reservoir having a capacity of 600 cubic meters of water, enough for up to 400 families, was constructed about the sixth century BC" on the acropolis (Wikipedia, Kameiros), and Ialysos, where "a late 4th c. fountain-house built into the hillside", with "a facade of Doric columns in limestone" and a basin behind a parapet, is "one of the best examples of its type" (PECS; the Filerimos fountain is now fenced, verify-deltas-round2). The Hellenistic city of Rhodes itself relied on wells, house cisterns and the acropolis springs; note that the famous rock-cut water galleries Torr surveyed are at Kameiros, not on Monte Smith (verify-deltas-round2 corrects research/20 on this).

**Water as cult: the nymphaia of Monte Smith.** On the acropolis the Ephorate describes "four underground cave-like structures carved into the rock with entrance stairs, corridors, connecting passages and a large opening in the central part of their roof", with niches for statuettes of the nymphs; "cisterns and lush vegetation completed the landscape", and they were "spaces for both recreation and worship" (efadod.gr, Ακρόπολη Ρόδου). This is the ancient theology of water made architecture: the cistern is inside the shrine. Torr adds a Rhodian abroad who dedicated, near Beirut, "a bronze image of horned Ammon, pouring out for mortals holy running water" (Rhodes in Ancient Times, p. 82): a drinking fountain was a pious gift in the Greek world long before the Ottoman sebil.

**The goddess who sends rain (stop 6, with jamie-04).** The Lindian Chronicle's first epiphany is a water story. Under Datis's siege "on account of the lack of water, the Lindians, being worn down, were of a mind to surrender the city"; they "asked only for a truce of that many [five] days from the enemy, saying that Athena had sent away to her own father for help", and "on the next day a great dark storm cloud settled over the acropolis and a big storm rained down across the middle, then, paradoxically, the ones being besieged had abundant water, but the Persian force was in need" (Topostext translation). The cistern that fills is the proof of the god. Keep this beside Diodorus's flood: the same city that inscribed Athena's rain as state history also kept engineers to stop rain drowning the agora.

**Strabo's verdict (stop 5 or 17).** The city was "so far superior to all others in harbours and roads and walls and improvements in general that I am unable to speak of any other city as equal to it" (14.2.5). "Improvements in general" (kataskeuai) is the ancient word for infrastructure; drains were part of what he was praising.

**Rodini (B-side).** The ravine park south of the walls is where the town's fresh water visibly runs. The Ephorate notes the stream "passes beneath the ancient Korakoneros bridge" and that the Italians rebuilt the cave benches and altars in 1924; Greek Wikipedia calls the structure beside the park entrance "the Roman aqueduct" restored by the Italians, and credits Italian engineers in 1929-30 with "construction of dams, artificial lakes, canals and bridges" (guidebook-level: the Ephorate page itself does not date or name the aqueduct; Biliotti in 1881 simply walked "au delà de l'aqueduc" to reach the Ptolemaion). Say "an aqueduct of Roman date by tradition".

**Kallithea (B-side).** Nothing ancient is securely attested (jamie-04); the spa's own site claims Herodotus and Hippocrates "delved into hydrotherapy" and that people came "especially in August and September" to bathe in "reddish water surging from a particular, rocky formation". Treat as the spa's pedigree, not history.

## 2. The Knights: privies in the wall, cisterns under the Palace, and a plague that became policy (Tell at stops 3, 6, 9, 11, 13 and 14)

**The Hospital's plumbing (stop 6).** The great ward's long sides are lined with "small vaulted cubicles built into the thickness of the walls; the result of concealed buttresses which served as privies", and a fifteenth-century visitor (the Bohemian pilgrim Jan of Lobkowicz, 1493) noted that "near each of these beds a door opens upon the balcony; and there too he has a privy" (lecrac, "The Conventual Hospital, Rhodes"; a blog without formal citations whose detail matches the published literature). Add the regime around it from jamie-05: two sworn doctors visiting "twice each day: once in the morning and once again in the evening", clean sheets, silver dishes, poisons under lock and key. The building took 49 years (1440-89, Ephorate). For Jamie: a hospital with a latrine per bed in 1489 is a statement about how the Order organised care, and the Museum's pebble courtyard was the ward's light-well and water-yard.

**What is under the Palace (stop 9).** Wikipedia and the Ephorate are silent on cisterns; the primary witness is Biliotti and Cottret (1881, pp. 509-10, quoted in verify-deltas-round2): "Il existe sous le Palais trois étages de magasins voûtés, dont le dernier atteint le niveau du fossé. C'est là que l'on serrait les vivres et les munitions de guerre"; the courtyard was "percée de nombreuses citernes" in which grain was still stored year to year "comme au temps des Chevaliers", and in 1881 the Palace was a convict prison of "environ 250 forçats". Evliya Çelebi, two centuries earlier, saw the same thing: in the Frenk Hisarı (Collachium) "palaces from the Knights' period and cisterns beneath these palaces", plus "wheat granaries" (Evliyaoğlu 2022, p. 148, citing Seyahatname IX). A well shaft survives inside the d'Amboise Gate passage (research/20, stop 10). Say "cisterns under the courtyard and three storeys of vaulted stores", and do not claim a walk-in cistern is visitable.

**Water outside the walls.** Torr, from the Order's records: between the pilgrims' cemetery opposite Fort St Nicholas and the north wall "were the gardens of the Grand Master and others. They were irrigated by water pumped up from a well by a windmill" (Rhodes in Modern Times, p. 15). Near the Mandraki execution rocks stood "the church of S. John of the Fountain, opposite the Bastion of Provence" (p. 16), so named because "John Baptist's head was found in digging a well where the Church of Saint John of the Fountain afterwards stood" (p. 60): a spring-cult church of the Order's own patron, a Christian nymphaion. The Order's accounts also list "cisterns for oil in one of their warehouses in the city" (p. 27): the same word covered water and olive oil. And on conquest the Latin archbishop took over "the Greek bishop's palace with its bakery and bath" (p. 62): a bath was part of a bishop's establishment.

**The bath at night (stops 13 and 14).** The 1453 privilege to Ezra and Rebecca lets them move between the Upper and Lower Jewish quarters "even during the hours of bellringing", "bearing a light so as to visit the baths, a pharmacist or a doctor" (Coureas, in jamie-05). So the Hospitaller town had public baths a Jewish couple could use, but only with a pass.

**Water as a weapon.** In 1480 "the garrison was burning the bodies of the Turkish dead for fear of pestilence" (Torr, p. 3837 of the OCR; p. 13 of the print). In 1522 the Grand Master had everything within a mile of the walls razed, "and one of the judges diligently polluted the wells" (p. 23). Martinengo's countermines were "made under immense difficulties from the influx of water at some places" (p. 26; research/20). The same wells that fed the suburbs were denied to Suleiman.

**The plague of 1498-1500 and what the Order did with it (stops 13 and 14; this is the political heart of the section).** Torr, from the Libri Bullarum and the Rhodian poem of Emmanuel Georgillas: "In October 1498 the plague ... arrived in the island ... At Rhodes the plague attacked old men and matrons, boys and unwedded girls: and among the victims was the metropolitan bishop Metrophanes. The Grand Master ordained fasting and prayer and the people sang Alleluia, but the plague continued: then he directed the isolation of suspected persons, but the isolation could not be maintained, and Knights fell sick within the castle in the city: and for twenty months the plague raged. But at length John Baptist overcame Charon: and after midsummer-day 1500 the plague abated" (pp. 103-4). Note the sequence, which is Jamie's material: prayer first, then quarantine, and quarantine failing because it crossed the Collachium wall. The Jewish Virtual Library gives the consequence: "After a plague (1498-1500), the order decided to expel within 40 days all Jews who would not become baptized. They could leave with their belongings but had to sail for Nice, in order not to settle in Turkish territories." D'Aubusson's biographers record his "success in extirpating Judaism in Rhodes, by expelling all adult Jews and forcibly baptizing their children" in his last years before 1503 (Wikipedia, Pierre d'Aubusson). Torr also preserves the earlier instrument of the same mentality: an order of 3 March 1456 "that to abate the wrath of God and to remove manifold scandals and to reform the city of Rhodes, all persons of more than doubtful character shall henceforth be confined to one quarter thereof, conformably to the practice of other cities" (p. 103). Confinement to a quarter, forced baptism, expulsion: public health and moral policing were the same department. No lazaretto or quarantine station at Hospitaller Rhodes is attested in the sources read; Venice's first lazaret dates to 1403 and the forty-day rule to 1448 (Wikipedia, Quarantine), so the Order knew the model and, on Torr's evidence, tried and failed to apply it.

## 3. The Ottoman town: the fountain as the node of a mahalle (Tell at stops 4, 13, 14, 15, 16 and 21)

**Why there are so many fountains.** The Metropolis of Rhodes's heritage portal: in the Ottoman period "numerous public fountains are created by Muslims, whose religion considers the construction of a public fountain a benefit for the soul"; they stood "at gates (for travellers and animals), in neighbourhoods (drinking water supplies), in parks and private gardens, at mosques and synagogues (for ablution)" and in the Christian marasia outside the walls; they were built of "Rhodian limestone with marbled backs, embellished with floral and geometrical motives and epigrams coming out of the Coran", in three types: "freestanding with a marbled back, freestanding of a square, cyclical or polygonal plan with a reservoir, wall fountains with one or more spouts" (imrtreasures.gr, The fountains of Rhodes). The 1885 vakf register counted, alongside 44 mosques and 3 madrasas, "50 water fountains" held by pious foundations on the island (Hayta, in jamie-06). A sebil in the strict sense is "a small kiosk ... where water is freely dispensed to members of the public by an attendant behind a grilled window", paid for "by the revenues or funds of a charitable endowment, an Islamic waqf"; the plain tap fountain is the çeşme; both were "built at crossroads, in the middle of city squares, and on the outside of mosques ... to provide drinking water for travelers and to assist ritual ablutions before prayer" (Wikipedia, Sabil). Jamie's point: the Ottoman state did not run a water department; it let individuals buy merit by endowing a tap, and the mahalle then organised itself around it.

**Who carried the water.** A public fountain implies a household task. Ottoman towns had a trade of water-carriers (saka, from Arabic saqqa, "one who gives water"); the best-documented are the Janissary corps' sakas, about 700 strong in the seventeenth century, who "carried water using leather waterskins hung on both sides of their horses" and whose corps was abolished with the Janissaries in 1826 (Turkish Wikipedia, Saka (Osmanlı ordusu)); civilian sakas and charitable water-distributing dervishes are attested across Ottoman Turkey from the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries (Wikipedia, Water carrier). For Rhodes specifically no saka guild record was found; jamie-07 has water-carriers among the Jewish port trades, and the safe formulation is "a trade that existed in every Ottoman port, Rhodes included". The daily reality for women is reported for the Jewish square: the plaza had "a large fountain where young women drew water" (attributed to the Rhodes Jewish Museum in jamie-07; the museum's "Jews of Rhodes" page as read on 8 Sep 2026 says only "La Kaye Ancha ('wide street') with the old fountain", so treat the girls-at-the-fountain detail as community memory, not a quotation), and the Ladino Purim blessing "may you live like fresh water" (jamie-07) shows what fresh water meant in a walled town with no river.

**The fountains on the route, with their evidential status.**

- *Suleymaniye şadırvan (stop 16).* **Verified as a structure, undated.** "The fountain in front of the Suleymaniye Mosque has an octagonal base with eight spoliated columns supporting a dome" (Wikipedia, Suleymaniye Mosque (Rhodes)); Evliyaoğlu confirms "the mosque also has a şadırvan; its dome, supported by eight columns, rests on arches" (p. 145, citing Balducci). The mosque is a 1808 rebuild (Wikipedia: "following the same design as the original") of a foundation Evliyaoğlu dates to 1541, built "together with an imaret and a hammam" (p. 144; Wikipedia gives c. 1522-23 for the first mosque; jamie-06). No inscription date for the fountain was found; the columns are spolia, so it is older than the stones look. It is an ablution fountain, not a drinking sebil: the water here is for prayer.
- *Hafiz Ahmed Agha library courtyard (stop 16).* **Building verified, fountain unverified.** Founded 1793 (Wikipedia) or "built in 1793-1794" (imrtreasures), "the main building covered by two cupolas, in the centre of a courtyard" with wings of offices and residences; the endowment paid the librarians "also for teaching the Arabic language" (Wikipedia). Sources disagree on the founding gift (1,995 manuscripts per Wikipedia; 824 per imrtreasures; 1,256 held now). No source read describes a fountain in the courtyard; if there is one, call it "the courtyard fountain" without a date.
- *Sokratous street fountains (stop 4 to 16).* **Unverified.** No inscription reading or date was found for any fountain on Sokratous. The street was the Jews' business district "up by the judge's" (jamie-07). Describe what is seen; do not date.
- *The Enderun (Ilk Mihrab / Agia Aikaterini area) fountain (stop 13).* Evliyaoğlu records that "adjoining the building next to the mosque there was also a fountain" at the Enderun mosque, a converted Knights' church (p. 139): **verified as existing historically, not as visible today**.
- *Şadırvan Camii.* A late-nineteenth-century mosque inside the walls named for its fountain, absent from the 1831 census because "it was not yet built", the building dating from "the end of the 19th century" (Evliyaoğlu pp. 147, 150). Useful as a name: a neighbourhood mosque could be called simply "the Fountain".
- *Murad Reis (stop 21 view).* The complex has "a fountain" (jamie-06) and the 2025 restoration package lists "the Ottoman fountain" among its items (verify-deltas-round2); the Wikipedia article does not describe it. **Guidebook-level.**
- *Hippocrates Square, the owl fountain (stop 4).* **Undated.** Wikidata Q134543663 ("Castellania fountain, Rhodes") places it at 36.4437 N 28.2283 E and gives no inception or creator; the owls are the Commons category's and visitors' description (verify-deltas confirms "owl-topped fountain" from reviews); Commons photographs run from 1977. The Castellania beside it has "three splendid water spouts in the form of crocodiles" on its south front (lecrac, Castellania), Hospitaller rain-water gargoyles that Jamie can look up at; under the Ottomans the Castellania's ground floor was a fish market (lecrac). Whether the present fountain is Ottoman or Italian is not established by any source read; the Metropolis portal's statement that Italian fountains were "built in public spaces for decorative purposes, in a variety of style" makes an Italian date plausible but unproven.

**The hammam's water (stop 15).** The Yeni Hammam is first named in a 1558 decree, with a women's wing added under Mustafa III (jamie-06). Its engineering is generic Ottoman: "a furnace or set of furnaces ... located in a service room behind the walls of the hot room" heated water "usually in a large cauldron above them", while "hot air and smoke from the furnaces was channeled through pipes or conduits under the floor" (Wikipedia, Hammam). So a hammam was the single largest consumer of water and fuel in the mahalle, and the municipality's history page notes that Ottoman houses also had "the baths (usually at the rear of houses)". Balducci's chapter (Architettura turca in Rodi, 1932, pp. 37-8, quoted by McGuire) explains the point that matters for Jamie: "the prescriptions of the Quran in fact admit the bath as aspersion [sprinkling] and not immersion, and the Turk conceives of the operation of the bath as not only an act of cleaning but also a method of pastime", with a "strong reluctance regarding the use of the swimming pool or the common bathtub". Running water poured over the body, never shared still water: a theology of water expressed as plumbing.

**Water cut off as punishment (stops 13 and 14).** In the blood libel of 1840 "Governor Yusuf Pasha blockaded the Jewish quarter on the eve of Purim" and "the inhabitants could obtain neither food nor fresh water" (Wikipedia, Rhodes blood libel; jamie-06 gives the twelve-day figure). In a quarter that fetched its water from a square fountain, a cordon at the lane-ends was a siege.

## 4. The Italians: water as the reason to move a market, a spa built on a landing beach, a seahorse in a Jewish square (Tell at stops 14, 18, 21; B-side Kallithea)

**1926: the market leaves the walls.** When the Nea Agora opened, the administration "called further for the immediate evacuation of all merchants still selling food or other goods in the medieval city" (McGuire, p. 11). The official justification, in the Messaggero di Rodi of 10 August 1926 (McGuire fn 37), is a public-health sentence: the old market was "disseminato nella città murata, senza la possibilità di un accurato controllo, quasi dovunque privo di acqua abondante, in locali non suscetibili di miglioramenti" ("scattered through the walled city, without the possibility of accurate control, almost everywhere lacking abundant water, in premises not susceptible of improvement"). Water and control in the same breath: the hygienic argument was also the argument for putting Greek and Jewish food-sellers under a copper-domed gazebo where the state could see them.

**Waterworks, generically attested.** Italian Wikipedia on Mario Lago: "La rete stradale conobbe una rapida espansione, accompagnata dalla costruzione di acquedotti e ponti" (roads expanded "accompanied by the construction of aqueducts and bridges"); English Wikipedia on the Italian islands: "New roads, monumental buildings in accordance with fascist architecture and waterworks were constructed." No source read gives a date, a source or a capacity for the Italian aqueduct to Rhodes town, and Italian Wikipedia's article on the city lists only "roads, electricity, port". Say "the Italians built aqueducts" and stop there. The Rodini works of 1929-30 (dams, artificial lakes, canals: Greek Wikipedia) are the visible piece.

**The seahorse fountain (stop 14).** The Jewish Community of Rhodes: "The fountain ornamented with three bronze seahorses was built during the governorship of the Italians, replacing a previous one which was destroyed"; the Rhodes Jewish Museum, however, says "the present fountain ornamented with three seahorses replaced a previous fountain that was destroyed during World War II", which sits awkwardly with an Italian-period build (Italian rule ended in September 1943). Wikidata Q134543681 records the fountain (36.4428 N 28.2299 E) with no inception date and no sculptor; Commons has photographs from 2008 on. **Date: the two community sources conflict (Italian period vs after a wartime destruction); no year, no sculptor.** For Jamie the substitution is the story: the working fountain of the wide street was replaced by an ornament, and nobody can say for certain whether that happened before or after the quarter's people were deported; say "the old fountain is gone; this one is the replacement".

**The hammam for tourists (stop 15).** The regime restored a mosque as a working hammam, ordering costume from Turkey for the "preservation of Oriental costume for persons employed at the Bath", and Lago asked for "two bath-masseurs aged over twenty and under fifty, morally and physically healthy" (McGuire, from GAK DOD 121[4]/1928; jamie-08). The tourist calendar the propaganda office printed included "the annual benediction of the waters on January 6" (McGuire fn 58): the Orthodox blessing of the sea, sold as spectacle.

**Kallithea: the cure you drink (B-side).** Lombardi's baths, inaugurated 1 July 1929 with the King present (jamie-08), were "constructed upon the site of the Italian landing in Rhodes in 1912"; the concrete cupola's small pool "acted as a visual epicenter for the baths' function as mineral therapy for tourists (although in reality, mineral water was bottled nearby and cures were ingested by visitors)" (McGuire, p. 13). In 1930 Alberto Mocchi "hosted an international hydrological colloquium which attracted more than 200 scientific specialists and MDs"; the springs "shut down for good in 1967", were restored 1999-2007 and reopened in 2007 (kallitheasprings.com). The spa's site gives no mineral analysis; do not invent one.

## 5. Today: a dam, a lawsuit, 450 litres a head, and a EUR 63.6 m contract to put the pipes underground (Tell at stops 1, 4, 15 and 21)

**Where the water comes from.** Rhodes town drinks from the Gadouras dam in the island's interior. Dimokratiki, 11 December 2014: "ο ταμιευτήρας του φράγματος είναι χωρητικότητας 67 εκατ. κυβικών μέτρων" ("the dam's reservoir has a capacity of 67 million cubic metres"); the dam and its filtration plant were then in trial, the transfer pipeline complete, and the test feed of the town network was set for late January 2015, with the whole scheme costed at about EUR 160 million and island consumption at "15 million cubic metres" a year, projected at 25 million by 2039. The southern extension, contracted to Intrakat in April 2022 for EUR 28.27 million, was designed for "annual demand of 26.5 million cubic metres for Rhodes and neighbouring islands (Chalki, Symi, Kastellorizo)" and is a project "promoted since 1970" (ypodomes.com). Phase B would double the treatment plant "from 60,000 to 120,000 cubic metres daily" (Dimokratiki, 4 July 2026).

**Who runs it, and the quarrel.** The dam is the Region's, operated under a service contract with AKTOR ATE of EUR 18,344,717 plus VAT (with an option clause) from 1 July 2023, and the municipal water company DEYAR buys the water. In May 2025 the Region certified a DEYAR debt of EUR 6,537,312.36 through the tax system; two cases are before the Piraeus administrative appeals court (Dimokratiki, 17 May 2026), and in July 2026 DEYAR asked to take over Phase B itself. In December 2024 a fault "in the networks and pumping stations" of the dam's treatment plant threatened "temporary water supply interruptions to Rhodes city", though DEYAR said it had already been repaired when it issued the warning (Dimokratiki, 3 Dec 2024). For Jamie: the town's water is a three-way institutional argument (Region, contractor, municipality) exactly as it was a vakf-versus-state or Order-versus-burgh argument before.

**Demand.** "Daily water consumption per person reaches approximately 450 litres" on Rhodes (Dimokratiki, 4 July 2026); for the Greek islands generally, a tourist "in a simple lodging" uses about 450 litres a day and "in luxury hotels it can reach 1,000 litres per person per day" (Prof. Dimitris Emmanouloudis, in Dimokratiki, 25 June 2026; an islands-wide figure, not a Rhodes measurement). Hydrologist Elisavet Feloni (Dimokratiki, 7 Dec 2025): "η συνολική ζήτηση – αστική, αγροτική και ειδικά η τουριστική – υπερβαίνει τη βιώσιμη και ανανεώσιμη προσφορά" ("total demand, urban, agricultural and above all tourist, exceeds the sustainable renewable supply"); Rhodes has entered a "ζώνη υδατικού συναγερμού" (water-alert zone), DEYAR "refuses to supply water to new large units" ("αρνείται να υδροδοτήσει νέες μεγάλες μονάδες", i.e. large tourist developments), network losses often exceed 25 per cent, over-pumped coastal boreholes are salinising, and the tourist season now runs eight months. Greece's desalination units rose from about 90 in 2009 to about 150 today; 2026 is better than 2025 after a three-year drought, but reserves are "noticeably lower" than 2020 and Rhodes is one of nine island municipalities receiving state help (Dimokratiki, 4 July 2026). Rainfall in the town averages 518.9 mm a year, almost none of it in July, August or September (Wikipedia, Rhodes (city)): on 30 September Jamie arrives at the driest point of the hydrological year, before the first rains that Diodorus's Rhodians thought had already finished.

**The Old Town's pipes.** The ten-year programme contract signed on 7 April 2023 for EUR 63,576,244.30 includes as named lines "Υπόγεια δίκτυα Μεσαιωνικής πόλης Ρόδου" (underground networks of the Medieval City), "Ανάπλαση τεσσάρων (4) πλατειών" (four squares) and "Αποκατάσταση, εκσυγχρονισμός και επαναλειτουργία Δημοτικών Λουτρών" (restoration and reopening of the Municipal Baths, i.e. the Yeni Hammam) (ERT, 7 Apr 2023; jamie-09). The Nea Agora closed on 17 November 2025 for "new sewage, water and electrical infrastructure" (Dimokratiki, in research/07). Whether the hammam has reopened is contested (jamie-12); the contract to restore it was reported unsigned in October 2025 (jamie-06).

**Tap water and bottles.** No official statement on the drinkability of Rhodes town tap water was found (DEYAR's site is behind a bot check; the Wikipedia articles are silent). In August 2026 DEYAR stated that "no official report from competent authorities has been issued connecting gastroenteritis cases to swimming waters, drinking water, or treatment plant operations" (Dimokratiki search results). Say: "the dam water is treated; most visitors and many residents still buy bottles", and let the walk observe the crates outside every kiosk. No figure for Rhodes's cruise-day bottle consumption exists in the sources read; the scale can be inferred from 547,549 cruise passengers in 2025 (jamie-09) and 450 litres a head, but do not state a bottle count.

## 6. Disease and public health across the regimes (short, and only where sourced)

- **Ancient.** The flood of 315/14 BC reached "the Temple of Asclepius" (Diodorus 19.45): the city had a healing sanctuary in the low town, and the god of health was the last line the water was allowed to cross.
- **Knights.** The Hospital of 1440-89 with two doctors twice daily and privies per bed (lecrac); "burning the bodies of the Turkish dead for fear of pestilence" in 1480; the plague of October 1498 to midsummer 1500, "fasting and prayer" then "the isolation of suspected persons" which "could not be maintained" (Torr), followed by the expulsion of the Jews (JVL). The frescoes of "four healer saints after a plague" in Agia Aikaterini, two lanes from stop 13 (research/17), are the Greek community's memorial of the same fear.
- **Ottoman.** In 1838 "the Ottoman government installed the Supreme Council of Health, including the Quarantine Administration, in Istanbul", with port inspections such as Izmir's (Wikipedia, Quarantine). Whether Rhodes had its own lazaretto is not established by any source read; do not claim one.
- **Italian.** The hygienic vocabulary of the 1926 market order; the 1930 hydrological colloquium at Kallithea; McGuire's summary of the regime's programmes of "hygiene, modernization, and urban renovation".
- **Today.** The General Hospital in the Agioi Apostoloi district is "a modern facility" of the last twenty years, base for the EKAV ambulances, with a helipad for airlifts from the smaller Dodecanese (Wikipedia, Rhodes (city)); a private Euromedica hospital lies at Koskinou. The Italian-era hospital building is not described in any source read; leave it out.

## Continuities and contrasts: the fountain as the place where a neighbourhood sees itself

- **An ancient Rhodian** would recognise the Suleymaniye şadırvan instantly as a krene with a religious purpose, the nymphaion-with-cistern of his acropolis moved to the mosque door; he would recognise the sebil as the pious gift his countryman made near Beirut; and he would recognise a municipality arguing about clearing the drains before the rains. He would not recognise a town that had forgotten its river never existed: his city built cisterns for 400 families and he would find the 450-litre tourist incomprehensible.
- **A Hospitaller** would recognise the cisterns under the Palace (his), the crocodile spouts on the Castellania (his), and the idea that a bath is a licensed place you reach with a pass after dark. He would recognise the 1926 market order as a Grand Master's decree in Italian, and the 1840 blockade of the Jewish quarter as the "confinement to one quarter" of 1456. He would not recognise a water supply that no religious body owned.
- **An Ottoman-era householder** would recognise every fountain on the route as somebody's sadaka, would know which mahalle each one belonged to and whose daughters met there, would use the hammam as of right and would be baffled that the town's baths are a line in a Ministry contract. He would recognise the DEYAR-Region lawsuit as a vakf dispute in modern dress.
- **A resident today** buys bottled water beside a fountain that no longer runs, pays DEYAR for dam water carried 30 km, and has seen the water company refuse new hotels; the neighbourhood now sees itself at the kiosk and the periptero, not the çeşme. What runs through all four: nobody in Rhodes has ever had water without an institution (temple, Order, vakf, company) standing between them and it, and each institution has used water to decide who belongs inside the walls.

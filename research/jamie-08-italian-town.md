# Jamie 08. The Italian period as social engineering (1912-1948)

Builds on research/11 (which has the building list, the Battle of Rhodes 1943 numbers, the Holocaust chronology and the 1947-48 legal sequence; do not repeat those verbatim). Corrections in research/verify-deltas.md override: Evangelismos "begun 1924, opened 1925" (or 1924-29 per the St John article); the Nea Agora is "seven-sided (some official texts say octagonal)"; the Nea Agora is still a closed construction site as of August 2026 with handover due April 2027; the 1856 explosion is 6 November 1856.

## Frame for the scriptwriter

The Italian thirty-five years are the one period on the walk where a state consciously tried to redesign how a whole society lived: where people shopped (markets pulled out of the walled town), what their children were called, what language they schooled in, who they could marry and what happened to the children, which church answered to which patriarch, who counted as a citizen and who as a "national", where the dead were buried, and even what the past looked like (the Ottoman town stripped back to a Latin one; a lost church rebuilt from engravings on a new site). For Jamie the frame is: a colonial administration with a "possession" that was legally neither province nor colony, ruling a town whose 1922 census was 6,461 Muslims, 5,654 Orthodox and 4,038 Jews in near-equal thirds, first through the Ottoman habit of governing by confessional community (Lago, 1923-36), then by attempting fascist assimilation (De Vecchi, 1936-40), and failing at both. Everything she sees on Mandraki is the stage set for that project; the medieval town is its edited "authentic" backdrop; and the modern Greek state moved into the set unchanged, so that the fascist party headquarters is the Town Hall and the Duce's Venetian-Gothic governor's palace is the regional government. Rhodians, who are now Greek by a 1948 flag-raising in front of that same building, live inside the machinery every day.

## Tell at stop 1 (cruise berth) and stop 18 (Liberty Gate): the seizure and the "possession"

- The Italian landing was at Kallithea Bay at 04:00 on 4 May 1912, unopposed; the town surrendered at 10:00 on 5 May and Ameglio's army "marched in without opposition"; the Ottoman garrison fought on inland and surrendered near Psithos on 16 May 1912 (Battle of Rhodes 1912, Wikipedia). Italian troops entered the walled town through the Gate of St John (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia) — the same Red Gate Jamie leaves the walls by at stop 12.
- The Greeks of ten islands met at Patmos on 16-18 June 1912 and proclaimed an autonomous "State of the Aegean" hoping for union with Greece; Italy ignored it (Hellenic Parliament Foundation; Greek Wikipedia, Italian rule in the Dodecanese).
- Legal limbo: the Treaty of Ouchy (1912) said Italy would give the islands back, but "the vagueness of the text allowed a provisional Italian administration"; Turkey renounced them in Article 15 of Lausanne (1923); Italy annexed them as the *Possedimenti Italiani dell'Egeo* (Italian Islands of the Aegean, Wikipedia). Guidi: the term *possedimento* "designated an ambivalent entity in between a province and a colony," a territory "too far and different to be assimilated with metropolitan Italy, too 'white' and 'civilized' to be compared to African colonies" (Guidi, IJMES 2022, p. 22).
- Citizenship as a sorting device. A 1925 royal decree gave residents *cittadinanza italiana egea*, Italian Aegean citizenship, which was "inclusive of local political rights and exclusive of military service"; after 1934 Dodecanese citizens could do military service and apply for full Italian citizenship (Dodecanese, Wikipedia; Greek Wikipedia confirms the 1925 royal decree). McGuire calls it "a political instrument to encourage imperial loyalty and to initiate a project of cultural, but also ethnic, transformation"; her paper title asks *una faccia, una razza?*: subjects "may have been the same in face, but were different in race" (St Andrews research portal). The Hellenic Parliament Foundation describes a two-tier system, "'minor' citizenship for locals, 'major' only for collaborators"; full citizenship was the price of joining the Fascist Party. In 1929, asked whether Dodecanesians could join the party, Lago "deemed the time unripe" and invoked a future "new generation" of "good fascists" (Guidi, p. 34). Uncertain: the decree number and exact date of the 1925 citizenship law were not verified; a 2014 St Andrews working paper (McGuire) is the detailed source but the PDF returned a 503.
- Liberty Gate itself: cut through the walls in 1924 by rulers "portraying themselves as liberators from Ottoman rule" (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia); the Italians also re-opened St Athanasios Gate in 1922 "during the 400th anniversary of the conquest of Rhodes by the Ottomans," and cut Acandia Gate in 1935 with a new street, Via del Carretto (now Alhadeff Street), to link it to the Gate of the Virgin, Jamie's stop 2 (same source). So three of the walker's thresholds are Italian, not medieval. I could not find a verified description of any inscription or coat of arms on Liberty Gate; do not script one.

## Tell at stop 18-21 (Mandraki): the Foro Italico as a stage set

- The plan: Di Fausto's city plan is dated 29 January 1926; it kept the walled town "almost totally," isolated the walls with "respect zones," laid the new quarters on the alignments of Hippodamus's ancient grid, and built a "garden city" south of Mandraki whose main road was christened Foro Italico (Florestano Di Fausto, Wikipedia). McGuire: the master plan "included new zoning laws that demarcated a colonial city distinct from areas where locals lived": an elite quarter for administrators in the Greek Neochori, a tourist zone, and the Foro as "colonial administration center"; "upon disembarking on Rhodes, the typical traveler would behold two cities, one colonial-modern and one medieval and Levantine" (McGuire, "Bringing the Empire Home," p. 6). One Italian guidebook description (via the Berkeley chapter) also has the plan sorting the town by confession: Muslims west, Jews east, Greeks in the Marasia. That matches the Ottoman inheritance Jamie already knows from stop 13-14.
- The Foro was built for crowds. Di Fausto "created a large esplanade adapted to mass political gatherings, which were as much a part of the cultural life of Rhodes under Fascist rule as they were in mainland Italy," and the new public buildings used "a red-orange porous stone called *finta pietra* (fake stone) to replicate the masonry of the Knights" (McGuire, p. 8). The Governor's Palace (1926-27) quotes the Doge's Palace so that "Italian rule in Rhodes builds upon the Venetian tradition of economic and military expansion in the Islamic world"; its wrap-around portico makes it look as if "emerging from the sea" (McGuire, pp. 8-9; research/11 has stone, chandeliers, majolica).
- The trick of *mediterraneità*. McGuire's central point: although the regime claimed to rescue Rhodes from "five centuries of Ottoman rule," the administration "committed to restoring the Ottoman monuments and highlighting the Ottoman past in new construction" — over thirty mosques restored, a mosque converted into a working hammam with "carpets" and costume ordered from Turkey for "preservation of Oriental costume for persons employed at the Bath," and Lago asking for at least "two bath-masseurs aged over twenty and under fifty, morally and physically healthy and expert in the technique of the Turkish bath" (McGuire, p. 2, citing GAK DOD 121[4]/1928). "Many of the Ottoman additions and decorations that were stripped from the medieval city were later grafted onto the new colonial city" (p. 6). This is the anthropological hinge: the Ottoman is erased as lived space inside the walls and re-staged as decor outside them.
- The Nea Agora as social engineering of shopping. When the New Market opened in 1926 the administration "called further for the immediate evacuation of all merchants still selling food or other goods in the medieval city," and promised in their place "one of those characteristic oriental bazaars" for tourists (McGuire, p. 11, citing Messaggero di Rodi, 10 and 18 August 1926: the old market was "scattered in the walled city, without the possibility of careful control, almost everywhere lacking abundant water"). The farmers' market moved outside the walls; the inside became a souvenir bazaar. It still is. Upstairs the market had "apartment homes for Italian families" (p. 11). Note: the 1926 dating rests on McGuire's newspaper citations; verify-deltas flags May/August 1926 as unverified from the earlier source, so say "opened in 1926 (Italian newspaper reports)."
- Numbers for the tourist fantasy: an Egyptian Gazette article of 8 June 1935, "written at the direction of the Italian administration," claimed "last year more than 50,000 tourists visited the island"; 200,000 brochures were printed in 1933 (McGuire, p. 7; dadfeatured blog citing Italian sources, low confidence). Many arrived on cruises run by the fascist trade union or the Dopolavoro (McGuire, p. 2-3). Carlo Emilio Gadda, cruising in the late 1920s, praised "the roads, wide and good" and "the loving work of Italy in the cleaning and reordering of the chivalric buildings" (McGuire, p. 5).
- Lago in costume: in March 1926 the governor was photographed in a knight's garb at the restored Palace, and the picture ran on the cover of *L'illustrazione italiana* (McGuire's text says the March issue; her own footnote cites no. 15 of 11 April 1926); an English delegation of the Order of St John invested him with its insignia and left "admiring the order, cleanliness and general courtesy of the city" (McGuire, p. 1, citing Messaggero di Rodi, 24 March 1926).
- The Evangelismos church (stop 21 is opposite it): built 1924-29 by Di Fausto with Petracco "reconstructing what was then believed to be the form of the Church of St John" (Church of St John of the Collachium, Wikipedia; plans "reconstructed using engravings," Di Fausto article). It was the Catholic *San Giovanni dei Cavalieri* until 1948, then re-consecrated as the Orthodox cathedral of the Annunciation with frescoes by Fotis Kontoglou (Greeka; cityofrhodes). So the Knights' church that exploded on 6 November 1856 (at least 200 dead per English Wikipedia; Italian Wikipedia's 800 is an outlier) at stop 8 was rebuilt as the colonial cathedral 700 m away and then captured by the Orthodox — three religions' worth of use in one silhouette. The 7 March parade still starts with a doxology here (wearerhodes).
- The Casa Littoria is the Town Hall. Bernabiti built it 1937-39 under De Vecchi "to host the club of fascism in Rhodes, named after the *fascio littorio*"; Mussolini "gifted statues of Octavian and Julius Caesar, positioned in the adjoining plaza"; on 7 March 1948 the Greek flag was raised here with the King present and "fascist symbols removed" (imrtreasures.gr, Metropolis of Rhodes tourism portal). It is flanked by the police building (ex-Italian military administration) and the municipal theatre, ex-Teatro Puccini of 1937 (search snippet of the same portal; Bernabiti's Teatro 1937 and Casa del Fascio 1937 per Italian Wikipedia). The same portal says the statues were "extracted" along with the fascist symbols before the 1948 ceremony, so do not look for them in the square. Greek City Times places the 1948 flag-raising at the Governor's Palace with King Paul, Queen Frederica and Deputy PM Tsaldaris present; the two buildings face the same square, so say "in front of the Italian government buildings on Mandraki."
- Other reuses (for the "what remains socially" beat): Governor's Palace = Prefecture of the Dodecanese 1948-2011, now the regional administration (Italian Wikipedia; rhodesguide); Teatro Puccini = municipal/National Theatre; Bank of Italy, Post Office and Courthouse keep their functions; Albergo delle Rose = Casino Rodos; Bernabiti's 1934 marine biology institute = the HCMR Aquarium; the Ronda sea-baths (1935) and Navy Club are on the same shore (McGuire p. 9; Bernabiti, Italian Wikipedia; research/11). The Stadio Diagoras (1932) is still the town stadium.

## Tell at stops 7, 9 and 11 (Street of the Knights, Palace, walls): the invented Middle Ages

- The team: Amedeo Maiuri ran the Italian archaeological mission from Rhodes, February 1916 to October 1924, installing the museum in the Hospital of the Knights (officially converted 1914 per Greek Wikipedia in research/06) and finding the stadium, the theatre and the gymnasium site (CICOP gives the February 1916-October 1924 dates; Wikipedia says 1914-24 and credits him only with the museum; neither source puts him on the Palace, so do not say he restored it). Giuseppe Gerola surveyed the Dodecanese monuments from 1912 (Giuseppe Gerola, Wikipedia). Hermes Balducci wrote *Architettura turca in Rodi* with a whole chapter on hammams, marvelling at the Turkish "reluctance" to use a bathtub (McGuire, p. 14). Vittorio Mesturino rebuilt the Palace 1937-40.
- What was stripped: the Ottoman wooden balconies came off the Street of the Knights (dated 1916 by viagallica, research/06), and the Inn of Italy was restored in 1915. English Wikipedia's summary is blunt: the Italians "preserved what was left from the Knights' period, and destroyed all Ottoman buildings" inside the plan area, demolishing houses built against the walls (Rhodes (city), Wikipedia). Kastra.eu: they "formed gardens and groves in the perimeter, in places where, according to historical testimonies, there were orchards during both the Knighthood and the Ottoman Empire," and partly demolished the Terreplein of Italy for the Acandia Gate. McGuire's gloss: the plan promised a *risanamento* that would clear Ottoman additions "perceived as decrepit architectural pollution to the crusader masonry" (p. 6). But she stresses it "focused on only one portion of the fortified area" and left the Turkish quarter intact as exotic scenery.
- The Palace (stop 9): Mesturino worked "despite lacking pre-Ottoman remains or documentation" and built a residence that traded on "the aura of the Hospitaller legacy"; it was for "the King of Italy, Victor Emmanuel III, and later for Fascist dictator Benito Mussolini, whose name can still be seen on a large plaque near the entrance"; criticised for "inherent lack of historical accuracy and unnecessary alterations to the authentic remains," in one scholar's words "horrendous fascist taste" (Palace of the Grand Master, Wikipedia; Greek Wikipedia via research/06). Mussolini never came (research/11). Durrell called the result "a design for a Neapolitan ice" (Irish Times, 1998). The Kos mosaics were laid in the 1930s; Jewish gravestones were requisitioned for the works and the Jewish cemetery moved to the Kallithea road (De Vecchi, Wikipedia; the Times of Israel says the old cemetery by the walls was cleared in 1938 "to set up rose gardens"). Then, the twist for Jamie: Andreas Papandreou hosted the European Council in this fascist-built palace in December 1988 (Greek Wikipedia via research/06), so the building made for a Duce who never arrived ended up hosting the EU's heads of government.
- Kallithea Springs (B-side, stop 21 chat): Lago "grasped the importance of the cultural and political aspects of the site"; Pietro Lombardi was assigned the design in 1928 with Bernabiti on decoration; King Victor Emmanuel III attended the inauguration on 1 July 1929; the doctor Aeneas Brunetti was assigned to the complex only in 1930, the year Alberto Mocchi of the Italian Hospital in Cairo hosted an international hydrological colloquium of over 200 specialists there; the Germans used it as a penitentiary; it closed "for good" in 1967, was restored from 1999 and reopened 2007 (kallitheasprings.com/history; the "studies from 1927", "Smyrna doctor" and "road finished 1928" details in an earlier draft are not on that page and were dropped; Bernabiti, Italian Wikipedia gives "Terme di Calitea 1929, completed by Pietro Lombardi"). McGuire notes it was "constructed upon the site of the Italian landing in Rhodes in 1912," and that the cupola was mostly theatre: "mineral water was bottled nearby and cures were ingested by visitors" (pp. 13-14). The Times of Israel adds that when the King came in 1929, "Turkish carpets were laid down in the piazza" in his honour, but that is the town piazza, not Kallithea (Kirshner does not name the springs).

## Tell at stops 4, 13, 14, 16: households, schools, church, names, the podestà

- Governing by community (Lago). Guidi: Lago's strategy was "preserving Ottoman repertoires of governance based on religious difference by accommodating them to colonial rule" while "hampering any foreign influence" (p. 23). Under Lago people "were allowed to elect their own mayors"; in 1937 the fascist system was extended and "a newly appointed *podestà*" was imposed on each municipality (Italian Islands of the Aegean, Wikipedia; Italian Wikipedia: "Nel 1937 i sindaci vennero sostituiti dal podestà di nomina governativa"). Fascist youth organisations (Opera Nazionale Balilla) were introduced "and the Italianization of names was encouraged" (same). An Italian state exam was made "necessary to attain some of the most remunerative and prestigious professions" to stop youths studying in Athens or Istanbul (Guidi, p. 34). In 1935 police urged the government to remove two young women teachers at the Orthodox Kazoulleion school because they had studied in Athens and their fathers were "not loyal" (Guidi, n. 65).
- Schools as the battlefield. Rhodes had a late-Ottoman secondary system: the state *idadiye* of 1884 next to the Palace and the Suleymaniye (about 150 boys, mainly Muslim, 15% Jewish), the Frères school of 1889 (the most confessionally mixed, whose vali father told pupils in 1910 to "learn your mother tongue, whatever it is, then your father tongue, that is Turkish, and finally French"), the Alliance Israélite school of 1902 (first with full curricula for girls), and the Orthodox Venetokleion gymnasium founded 1909 with money from Minos Venetoklis of Alexandria (Guidi, pp. 25-28). Greek Wikipedia dates the first Italian school regulation to 1926 (Italian compulsory, oversight taken from the Church). De Vecchi's 1937 school regulation (the day, sometimes given as 21 July, is unverified: Greek Wikipedia says only "in 1937" and Guidi gives no date) made Italian dominant, with Greek "taught optionally, without books and only in the first classes of primary school" (Greek Wikipedia; English Wikipedia: "Greek being only an optional subject") and introduced compulsory education to age eleven (Guidi, p. 23). The response was "secret schools" run under cover of catechism by Metropolitan Apostolos (imr.gr; Greek Wikipedia).
- The Venetokleion strike, spring 1937 (the neoclassical school on the St John site at stop 8 is the town's school landmark, but the Venetokleion was a separate building; do not conflate). De Vecchi banned the pupils' berets; police reported "scenes of fanaticism" with boys "weeping and kissing" them. When the headmaster ordered attendance at the fascist Natale di Roma on 21 April, an 18-year-old stood up: "Mr. Headmaster, did you not always tell us that we are Greeks?" The fifth and sixth forms struck. De Vecchi had the headmaster dismissed, seven pupils arrested, three expelled from every school in the Dodecanese; only seven pupils would join the fascist youth, and "the whole school was shut down that summer and reopened only in 1943" (Guidi, pp. 37-38). A year earlier Jewish students had formed a Revisionist-Zionist (Betar) cell wearing dark berets, which authorities "neither comprehended nor accepted"; one leader, Elie Levi, left for the Belgian Congo in 1936 (Guidi, pp. 35-36 and Fig. 1).
- The Church. Lago wrote to Metropolitan Apostolos that the Dodecanese Church must be declared autocephalous "in order that it may be independent"; a protocol of 14 October 1926 in eight chapters covered "religion, priesthood, courts, schools, and ecclesiastical funds"; Cyprus was cited as precedent; Patriarch Photios II asked for a referendum (31 October 1929 per Greek Wikipedia); popular fury, including the "stone-throwing war" on Kalymnos, killed it (imr.gr, Metropolis of Rhodes; Wikipedia says the attempt was "without success"). A Latin archbishopric was set up in 1928 (Greek Wikipedia). The Metropolis's own history claims mixed marriages were "officially permitted with automatic conversion to Catholicism and monetary incentives" and that Apostolos from his arrival on 1 April 1914 refused to bless mixed marriages without Orthodox rites; he organised the 1919 Easter referendum for union, was exiled to Patmos in 1921 and returned 1924; a priest, Fr Papaloukas, was killed during the 1919 Easter protests (imr.gr; church source, one-sided but specific). Lago's Wikipedia entry says he "encouraged intermarriage with local Greeks." Both can be true: intermarriage as assimilation tool, resisted as confessional loss.
- The Jewish community's arc. Lago's era is remembered by survivors as "generally positive in spite of the fascism" (Berkeley SURF summary of oral histories). Then the September 1938 racial laws: roughly 2,000 Jews who had come from Asia Minor after 1919 "lost their citizenship and had a half-a-year to leave the island," going mostly to Tangier or the Rhodian colony in Elizabethville, Congo (Times of Israel, Kerem; Kirshner gives about 500 ordered out and says some restrictions were later relaxed "allowing Jews to frequent movie theaters and beaches"); Jews were barred from public schools, jobs and professions; the exodus of over 2,000 halved the community (Rhodes Jewish Museum, research/11). The Air Force headquarters where men were ordered to report on 18-19 July 1944 was "the old headquarters of the Italian Air Force" (jewishrhodes.org); its location remains an open question from research/11.
- Settlers and land. Italians in the islands numbered 7,015 in the 1936 census (Italian Wikipedia; the 16,711 figure on English Wikipedia probably includes military), almost 8,000 by 1940, nearly 80% on Rhodes, plus about 40,000 soldiers and sailors in 1940 (Italian colonists, Wikipedia; dadfeatured). Peveragno Rodio at Kalamonas, named for Lago's Piedmont home town: Italian Wikipedia says founded 1931 on 3,500 hectares "bought from Turkish landowners" with "several hundred farming families," producing olives, grapes, mulberries; an English summary says 1929 with 8,000 olive trees, 7,000 fruit trees, 130,000 vines, 110 cattle and 800 sheep planted at the start (low confidence, blog). It was "abandoned by the Italian population" in 1947 and is now a Greek army base, "not photographable or accessible." Campochiaro (Eleousa), 1935, at 300 m: about 30 families of foresters from the Val di Fiemme, Trentino, with church, school, arcaded market and a great fountain; the Catholic church became St Charalambos; the buildings housed the "Queen Frederica" tuberculosis sanatorium that treated 1,581 patients over 23 years before closing in August 1970 (figures from the manosgoing blog only, not found elsewhere); the fountain pool now shelters the endemic gizani fish (manosgoing). Greek Wikipedia lists further colonies at San Benedetto (Kolymbia), San Marco (Agios Pavlos), Torre di Lambi (Lampi). The Hellenic Parliament Foundation: "arable land distributed to Italian settlers," and thousands of Dodecanesians emigrated to mainland Greece, Egypt, America and Australia.

## Tell at stops 12 and 22: war, hunger, the British, the flag

- The 1943 numbers are in research/11. Add the human texture: after Italy attacked Greece on 28 October 1940 De Vecchi interned Greek nationals in a "Concentramento" camp "within the trenches of the old fortress," that is in the moat Jamie has just looked at from stop 11 (theislandofrhodes.com, low-medium confidence); the Metropolis says Greek-citizen teachers were "imprisoned in the old city moat" during the war (imr.gr). The Italian San Giovanni internment camp held over 500 Jewish refugees from the *Pentcho* 1940-42 (research/11).
- Famine, 1944-45. Civilians were reduced to eating wild greens gathered in the fields as food became scarce and prices soared (paraphrase: this sentence could not be found in the accessible text of the Cambridge article, so do not quote it verbatim); what the article does say is that "hunger was the prime reason a few thousand refugees fled from Rhodes and Kos in early 1945" (Cambridge, New Perspectives on Turkey). About 4,000 of the 9,000 Dodecanese Muslims fled to Turkey in 1944-45 and were "temporarily settled in Fethiye," then "repatriated en masse in 1945"; around 3,500 residents of the German-held islands reached Turkey between late 1944 and May 1945; the Friends Ambulance Unit ran relief in April-May 1945 and the British set up camps on Kasos and Karpathos (same source). Metropolitan Apostolos wired the Red Cross: "The people of Rhodes in agreement with the Red Cross are agonizingly awaiting sacks of flour" (imr.gr). I could not find a verified death toll for the Rhodes famine; do not give one.
- British administration, 8 May 1945 to 31 March 1947: the British "adopted the pre-existing administrative and tax system of the Italian occupation, removing only those provisions that had a fascist content. Italian officials kept their positions and the return of Dodecanesians in exile was impeded"; Red Cross soup kitchens fed the town; Regent Damaskinos arrived on the *Averof* on 15 May 1945 (the Metropolis says 5 May, which is impossible: the German garrison only surrendered on 8 May, so use 15 May) and the first municipal elections produced Greek authorities; a Foreign Office official wrote that Dodecanesians "longed for the Union so much, that they had become obsessed" (Hellenic Parliament Foundation). Protosyngellos Apostolos at the quay: "We the grateful people of Rhodes humbly welcome our mother Greece to our shores" (imr.gr).
- The leaving. About 6,000 Italians were evacuated on 1 September 1947 (Italian Wikipedia); "all the Italian schools were closed" in 1947 per English Wikipedia (Italian colonists), though Italian Wikipedia says the last one, the Christian Brothers' school, closed definitively on 9 September 1950; only "a few dozen old colonists" remained. The Turkish Muslim minority on Rhodes and Kos today is put at 5,000-7,000 (Dodecanese, Wikipedia).
- 7 March 1948, the day the square by the Nea Agora is named for: King Paul and Queen Frederica came with Deputy PM Tsaldaris; the flag went up on the Italian buildings at Mandraki (Greek City Times; imrtreasures). Today it is a Dodecanese public holiday: doxology at Evangelismos, wreaths at the Monument of the Fallen, an 11:00 parade along the Mandraki seafront, then coffee at the Mandraki cafes and a lenten meal (wearerhodes). The parade route is precisely the Foro Italico.

## Ambivalence: what Rhodians did with the set

- Durrell mocked the Palace but conceded "the pleasant shape of the new town is a credit to Italian architects"; "the Italians are welcome and popular tourists today. No longer a threat, they are praised for the efforts to enhance the island's beauty"; local fear runs the other way: people say "when the Turks come," and "rarely 'if'" (Irish Times, 1998, on the 50th anniversary).
- The Greek state kept every function in place: the possession's governor's palace became the prefecture, the fascist club the town hall, the Catholic cathedral the Orthodox cathedral, the fascist theatre the municipal theatre. Nothing was torn down; the fasces and the Caesar statues were the only edits. Jamie can put it as: a regime that tried to redesign a society left a society that redesigned the regime's furniture.
- What is being restored now: the Nea Agora is mid-restoration (closed, handover due April 2027, verify-deltas); Kallithea was restored by the municipality from 1999 and reopened in 2007 as a wedding-and-swim venue; the Palace's Italian rooms are the museum. Italian is heard again on Mandraki as the language of the cruise passengers the Foro was built to impress.

## Continuities and contrasts

- **An ancient Rhodian** would recognise the Foro Italico's premise: a harbour front designed to overawe arrivals, and a grid (Di Fausto explicitly reused Hippodamus's alignments). He would recognise the state feeding the poor by liturgy in the 1945 soup kitchens and the sorting of residents into citizens and metics in *cittadinanza egea*: political rights without the duty of arms is exactly the reverse of the ancient bargain, where the fleet's rowers earned their standing.
- **A Hospitaller** would find his Order flattered: his Palace rebuilt bigger, his church rebuilt on the waterfront, his street purged of the enemy's balconies, and the governor dressed as him. He would also recognise a confessional state that admits people by faith, converts by marriage and rules a subject Greek population through its own clergy, which is what the Order did with the Orthodox metropolitan.
- **An Ottoman-era householder** would recognise Lago's Rhodes at once: government through the community (millet), the church and the rabbinate answerable for their own schools and courts, an elected local head, the bazaar in the walled town. He would not recognise 1937: the podestà, the state school, the Italianised name, the market moved outside the walls, the ban on his script in public. His hammam, ironically, was preserved and costumed for tourists.
- **A resident today** lives in the Italian plan without thinking about it: shops around the Nea Agora, coffee under the arcades, the Region in the Governor's Palace, the wedding at Kallithea, the March parade past the fascist buildings, the cruise passengers the whole thing was built for. The continuity she would feel most is the confessional map: Turkish quarter west, Juderia east, Greek suburbs outside, exactly as the 1926 zoning kept it.

## Fact-check

Adversarial pass, 8 September 2026. Sources fetched directly; the Guidi and McGuire PDFs were read in full text. Verdicts: CONFIRMED, CORRECTED (body text fixed above), UNVERIFIABLE (kept but hedged), REMOVED.

1. CONFIRMED. Landing at Kallithea Bay 04:00 4 May 1912 (lasting to 14:00), town surrendered 10:00 next morning, Ottoman commanders surrendered 16 May near Psithos after a nine-hour battle. Battle of Rhodes (1912), en.wikipedia; it.wikipedia Occupazione italiana del Dodecaneso corroborates 4 May landing, Ameglio installed 5 May, island fully occupied 16 May. Entry through the Gate of St John: Fortifications of Rhodes, en.wikipedia.
2. CONFIRMED with addition. "Inclusive of local political rights and exclusive of military service" is verbatim in Dodecanese, en.wikipedia, which adds that after 1934 Dodecanese citizens could do military service and apply for full citizenship; Greek Wikipedia confirms the 1925 royal decree; Italian Islands of the Aegean, en.wikipedia confirms "did not receive full citizenship and were not required to serve". The 1925 decree number remains unfound.
3. CONFIRMED. Guidi, IJMES 54 (2022) p. 22: "A 1922 census recorded 6,461 Muslim, 5,654 Orthodox, and 4,038 Jewish inhabitants"; pp. 22-23: "Fourteen years later, another census based on citizenship also featured 4,081 Italian 'nationals' residing in Rhodes, mostly settlers from the Kingdom of Italy or former residents of Anatolia holding Italian citizenship."
4. CONFIRMED. Elected mayors under Lago and appointed podestà in 1937 (Italian Islands of the Aegean, en.wikipedia; it.wikipedia "Nel 1937 i sindaci vennero sostituiti dal podestà di nomina governativa"); 1938 racial laws and Italianisation of names / Balilla (same en.wikipedia article).

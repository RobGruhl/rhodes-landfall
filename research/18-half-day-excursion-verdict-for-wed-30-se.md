# Half-day excursion verdict for Wed 30 Sep 2026 (09:00-18:00 call, two ships): Filerimos (acropolis of Ialysos) versus Lindos, with the full Filerimos story

## The verdict: Filerimos, in the morning, back in town for a birthday lunch

Two ships share Rhodes that Wednesday: Celestyal Discovery (1,266 passengers, alongside 07:00-18:00) and Scarlet Lady (2,770 passengers, 09:00-18:00). Lindos is 47-49 km and 65 minutes each way by road (OSRM: 49.4 km, 65 min from the Tourist Port quay), the acropolis charges EUR 20 with timed e-tickets, and the site itself says access is "either on foot or on mules" with "no access to the acropolis for disabled people". The crowd mechanics are brutal for a 09:00 arrival: Discovery's Lindos coaches leave before you have cleared the gangway, Scarlet Lady's excursion waves reach the village 10:30-11:30, and every local guide says the same thing: the acropolis is only pleasant "before 10am or after 3pm". A taxi return is EUR 67 each way plus EUR 11.90 per hour waiting (2025 tariff); a forum regular put a cruise-day Lindos taxi at "pushing EUR 200", and the consensus of the Rhodes forum was blunt: "Trying to do both is pointless as your experience will be poor." The bus is EUR 6 and about 1h05, but KTEL's published sheet only runs to 10 September 2026; the autumn timetable that applies on the 30th did not exist when this was written.

Filerimos, by contrast, is 14 km and 23 minutes from the quay, EUR 24 by taxi (2025 list, plus EUR 1.20 port surcharge), open 08:00-20:00 in the official summer season, EUR 10, and nobody on the two ships is being bussed there in waves. It is also, for this traveler, the better story: a hilltop keep with Bronze Age, Archaic, Byzantine, Hospitaller, Ottoman and Fascist-Italian layers, a betrayed garrison, and a relic that escaped the fall of the city. Filerimos wins.

## The hill

The Ministry's own text is the spine of the script: "This was the acropolis of ancient Ialysos, one of the three city-states of ancient Rhodes, leader of the initiative which resulted in the establishment of the ancient city of Rhodes in 408/407 BC." Worship here "goes back to at least the 9th c. BC"; the hexastyle temple of Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus is 4th-century, and "in Antiquity the acropolis was called Achaia polis, while after the Synoicism of Rhodes was known as Ochyroma (=Fortification)". Only foundations survive because "the Hospitaller period (1309-1522) is marked by considerable building activity, which was responsible for the disappearance of much ancient construction". Stand in the monastery courtyard: the cross-shaped font of the 5th-6th-century basilica sits over the temple's stylobate, the 10th-11th-century inscribed-cross chapel ruins lie west of it, the Knights' church is beside it, and the Italian cloister wraps the whole. Four civilisations in thirty paces.

**The Doric fountain (4th c. BC).** Thirty metres below the south edge, down a stone stair: "Two tunnels brought water from the top of the hill to a cistern cut into the rock and closed by a poros isodomic wall in the form of a Π. Lion-head spouts discharged the water from the closed cistern into an open tank, which was enclosed by six pillars with stone panels between them, and from there to a portico of six Doric columns... A sacred law with proscriptions for the protection of the fountain-house was carved on one of the pillars." It "remained completely buried due to a landslide until excavations in 1923 brought it to light" (Italian School, 1914 and 1923-26; restored 1927-28). Ask the listener to find the lion heads and the inscribed pillar.

## Sieges and a traitor

Two sieges are on record. In 1248 a Genoese fleet bound for the Seventh Crusade seized Rhodes town by night while its lord John Gabalas was away; the Nicaean commander John Kantakouzenos made Filerimos his base to blockade them. George Akropolites wrote that the Genoese held out in comfort, even sleeping "with the most beautiful local women"; William of Villehardouin's hundred knights forced the Nicaeans back up onto Filerimos, until Theodore Kontostephanos arrived with 300 horse and the Genoese bargained their way out in 1249-50.

The second is the one for the campaign: in 1306 the Hospitallers, with the Genoese corsair Vignolo de' Vignoli, landed on Rhodes. The Cypriot chronicles of Amadi and Bustron record that "on 11 November, they took the citadel of Filerimos (ancient Ialysos) through the treason of a local Greek; the garrison of 300 Turks was massacred." Those Turks were mercenaries in the Byzantine emperor's pay. From this rock the Order besieged the city for four years until it fell on 15 August 1310. What you see today is mostly later: "the surviving Hospitaller constructions date from 1480 and 1481, under grand master Pierre d'Aubusson", the fort being "roughly quadrilateral, with towers on three corners", 2,000 sq m within 200 m of wall, Byzantine cylindrical towers on the north, a Hospitaller polygonal tower on the south-east. The claim that Suleiman used Filerimos as his headquarters in 1522 appears in guidebooks (Matt Barrett, rhodesguide) but not in the Wikipedia siege articles or kastra.eu; the Italian article only says the Ottomans "disembarked near Ialysos and the hill of Filerimos" on 26 June 1522. Script it as tradition.

## The relic that escaped

Inside the Knights' church, "the corbels of the north chapel bear the arms of grand master Aubusson, while a gothic niche in the south chapel sheltered the renowned icon of Our Lady of Phileremos, the talisman of medieval Rhodes." Legend makes it St Luke's work; scholars date it to the 11th-12th century (tempera on wood, 44 x 36 cm, only the Virgin's face). Miracles were credited to it in the siege of 1480. In 1522-23 "the icon was rescued, and attached to the mainmast of the Santa Maria, a carrack captured from the Sultan of Egypt in 1507"; it went to Malta, to St John's Co-Cathedral, and when Napoleon took Malta in 1798 Admiral Litta carried it to Paul I of Russia, who sheathed it in gold and 270 diamonds. Maria Feodorovna smuggled it out via Copenhagen in 1920; Berlin; Belgrade in 1932; Ostrog monastery in 1941; seized by the Yugoslav secret police on 18 September 1952 and locked in a museum vault; revealed in 1993; since 2002 in the Blue Chapel of the National Museum of Montenegro at Cetinje. The copy in the niche today is "the work of an Italian painter".

## The Italians, the cross, the peacocks, the liqueur

Under Italian rule (1912-43) the site was landscaped, the medieval church was "incorporated into a monastery of the Franciscan Order", the church was rebuilt in 1931-33, and a cypress-lined Via Crucis with 14 station shrines (sculptor Antonio Maraini) was laid to the west tip, where a 30 m Italian cross of 1934 stood until it was demolished in the Second World War, reportedly because Allied aircraft used it as a landmark. The present concrete cross (16-18 m) dates from the mid-1990s; its internal stair to the arms has been closed for several years. The friars left in 1948, but their digestif, Sette Erbe ("seven herbs"), passed to a local family and is still sold at the kiosk by the car park; it is the ancestor of Rhodes' green Koriantolino. Hundreds of feral peacocks patrol the paths.

**D&D reading.** A hilltop keep with four construction phases; an underground burial chapel (St George Chostos, dug into the rock in the 15th century) whose walls show "skulls in armour and coats of arms of the Knights Hospitaller" and "Death prevails over a knight"; a sealed sacred spring beneath it; water tunnels behind a lion-mouthed fountain "that served as a hiding place in troubled times"; a garrison betrayed from within; a relic carried out on a captured ship; and a chest of secret-recipe potion at the gate. Roll initiative.

**Practical.** Wednesday is not a closure day (both sites close Tuesdays only in winter). Steps: about 25 up to the courtyard, a long stair down to the fountain, uneven stone throughout. Shade on the Via Crucis, little elsewhere. Water: kiosk only; toilets require a ticket according to 2025 reviews. Bring a hat.

# Jamie 05. How the Order of St John organised people: the Convent as a household, the Hospital as a regime, the town as two cities (Rhodes, 1309-1522)

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** Research/06 already tells the faction story (eight tongues, eight great offices, who held which stretch of wall). This file is about the *institution as a way of organising human lives*: how a few hundred celibate noblemen from all over Latin Europe ran a household of 300-400 men in a walled compound, fed and nursed strangers "as if they were lords", kept accounts on a continent-wide estate, and governed a mostly Greek, Orthodox island whose people were sorted by law into free, "marinarii" (rowing-service families), serfs and slaves, and by geography into an inner city the Order kept for itself and an outer city where everyone else lived behind a curfew announced by bells. The best material comes from the Order's own registers (the *Libri Bullarum*, now in Malta), which record the marriages, manumissions, dowries, court cases and street-passes of ordinary Rhodians by name. Use those names. The walker can stand in front of the Hospital, the auberges, the Castellania, the Jewish quarter and the surviving Greek churches and hear how each was a node in one system of sorting people.

---

## 1. The Convent as a household: classes, proofs, vows, and the day (Tell at stops 7 and 9)

**Who lived here.** The Order's *Convent* (its headquarters community) had three classes of professed brethren: knights (*fratres milites*), chaplains (*fratres capellani*) and serjeants-at-arms (*fratres servientes armigeri*), with "confratres" and "donats" (lay affiliates and regular subscribers) attached (1911 Britannica). Admission to the knightly class was "confined ... to sons born in lawful wedlock of knights or members of knightly families"; for serving brothers "it sufficed that they should not be serfs" (1911 Britannica). The famous "sixteen quarterings" of nobility that the Catholic Encyclopedia lists belong to the Malta centuries; per research/verify-deltas.md, in the Rhodes period the number of quarters varied by langue and grew over time, so say "proofs of nobility, stricter with every generation", not "sixteen quarters".

**How many.** Luttrell (in Setton, *History of the Crusades* III, ch. VIII) reports "four hundred Hospitallers at Rhodes in 1345, with a small garrison at Cos". Rossi (ch. IX, citing Bosio) gives the 1466 establishment as "300 knights, 30 chaplains, and 20 sergeants stationed on Rhodes". On the eve of the 1522 siege "there were only two hundred and ninety knights, fifteen donati, and about three hundred sergeants (freres sergents d'armes), in all about six hundred forming the Convent of the order". So the ruling caste of an island of perhaps tens of thousands was a community the size of a large secondary school.

**The vows and the Rule.** Every brother swore "chastity and obedience, and to live without property of their own", the "three things" that God "will require of them at the Last Judgement" (Rule of Raymond du Puy, clause 1, c.1145-53). The Rule is the household's constitution and reads like one:
- Brethren "should claim nothing beyond bread and water and raiment" (cl. 2).
- They eat twice a day, no meat on Wednesdays or Saturdays or in Lent unless ill, and "at table, as the Apostle says, let each one eat his bread in silence" (cl. 8, 11).
- They sleep clothed, in linen or wool; bright colours, animal fur and fustian are forbidden (cl. 8).
- They go out only in twos or threes, chosen by the superior, and must be modest around women (cl. 4).
- A brother found with hidden private property at death is publicly shamed, beaten and does forty days' penance on bread and water (cl. 13); proven public fornication means public flogging and expulsion, with readmission possible after a year as "a stranger" (cl. 9).
- The sick are received with confession and the Eucharist, put to bed, and fed "as if he were a Lord" *before the brethren eat* (cl. 16); one-third of the bread, wine and food of every house goes to the hospital poor (cl. 6).
(Text: smom-za.org/rule.htm, a modern English rendering; the Rule was approved by Pope Eugenius III at some point between 1145 and his death in July 1153, so "confirmed by 1153" rather than "in 1153".)

**The day.** The Rule gives the skeleton (Mass and the canonical hours in the conventual church, two silent meals, sleep clothed); at Rhodes the statutes overlaid military drill. Torr (quoted in research/20) records that in the vaulted gallery linking St John's church to the Palace "every Knight had to perform gun-drill at least once a week". The career ladder was explicit: a young knight had to complete three "caravans" (cruises on the Order's galleys) before he could hope for a commandery at home; the Catholic Encyclopedia speaks of "younger knights who were in haste to accomplish their three 'caravans' in order to merit some vacant commandery". Luttrell adds the human reality: "some acquired priories and preceptories without serving at Rhodes; such men often cared mainly for the wealth and social position the Hospital offered", while "many brethren, served by their slaves in the semi-oriental society of Rhodes, lived comfortably in the Frankish town".

**Governance.** "The supreme legislative and controlling power was vested in the general chapter"; the Grand Master worked through "four councils: (1) the 'convent' ... for administrative business; (2) a secret council, for criminal cases and affairs of state; (3) a full council, to hear appeals" (1911 Britannica). The langue system "was only legalized by the general chapter at Montpellier in 1330" (1911 Britannica).

## 2. The auberge as a national household (Tell at stop 7)

Luttrell: "At Rhodes the brethren lived in a reserved quarter around the castle, the collachium, separated by a fortified wall from the rest of the city or borgo. Some Hospitallers had their own houses, while others lived in the auberge or hospice of their priory or nation; they included Italians, some Germans, and a few Englishmen and Spaniards, but the French-speaking group was the largest." So the auberge was less a barracks than a college hall: a common table for a nation, headed by its *pilier* (the tongue's bailiff, who was also one of the great officers, see research/06), where new arrivals were lodged, where the tongue's wall-post was organised, and where the tongue's own chapel stood. A 1401 document records "the chapel of St Dominic in the hospice or auberge of the Langue of Italy", endowed by the Admiral Palamedo di Giovanni with the income of "two windmills, of which one, located on the harbour mole of Rhodes Town, had previously belonged to the women Eirene Stamatudine and Eirene Archondo" (Coureas 2022, p. 193-4). That is the auberge economy in one sentence: a national mess funded by a windmill on the mole that a walker can still see from the ship.

Property inside the Order's own quarter was not exclusively the Order's. The registers list "a two-storeyed hospice in the collachium of the Town of Rhodes" belonging to "Sophia, daughter of John Diastrique", adjoining "the houses of three other women of Latin or Greek ethnicity" (Coureas, p. 192, citing Luttrell, *The Town of Rhodes 1306-1356*). In 1347 the Hospitaller *soror* Margarita of Negroponte owned two apartments "in the castrum of Rhodes Town near the cathedral of St Mary", the upper one where "Jacobus, the tailor of Grand Master Déodat de Gozon, used to live", and an oven she "had had constructed to bake bread to be given to the poor as alms" (Coureas, p. 202-3). And the Order "issued monthly allowances of wheat to seculars resident in the castellum of the Town of Rhodes as a reward for their services": in 1517 the widow Katherina tou Sinademi was to receive "one measure of wheat every month for herself and her seven children" (Coureas, p. 203, citing Sarnowsky). A widow with seven children on a grain ration inside the Knights' compound is a better picture of the Collachium than any coat of arms.

## 3. "Our lords the sick": the Hospital as a regime (Tell at stop 6)

**The idea.** Raymond du Puy's Rule has a chapter on "How our Lords the Sick should be received and served" (Christian History Institute). In Jerusalem the 1181/82 statutes required "large beds, each with its own coverlet and sheets, cradles for babies born in the hospice, and cloaks and boots for the sick to travel to the latrine"; from 1176 the revenues of two villages paid for white-flour bread for "our Lords the Poor"; four physicians prescribed "syrups and cordials"; Muslim and Jewish patients "were welcomed and fed chicken instead of pork" (Christian History Institute). The inversion is the point: the poor stranger is the lord, the noble brother is the servant.

**The Rhodes building.** Work began on 15 July 1440 with 10,000 florins left by Grand Master Antoni Fluvià (d. 1437) and finished in 1489 (lecrac.blogspot.com, "The Conventual Hospital, Rhodes"; research/06). The great ward on the first floor is "51 m long by 12.5 m wide", divided by octagonal pillars, with a polygonal chapel projecting "half way along the ward, opposite the door"; it could take about 100 patients, each with "his own bed surrounded by a curtain" and "between clean sheets" (lecrac). The 1440 statutes were "probably the first major revision of the Hospital's regulations since those of 1182" (lecrac). Ludolph of Suchem's contemporary, an anonymous English visitor of 1345, wrote: "Below the castle is the house of the hospital, a mother, nurse, doctor, protector, and handmaiden to all the infirm" (quoted by Luttrell in Setton III).

**Staff and rules (per lecrac, an uncited blog; its detail matches the published literature on the 1440 statutes, but it names no source and does not cite Luttrell).** Professional doctors (lecrac gives neither their number nor whether they were salaried) had to visit the ward twice a day with one on night duty; two surgeons; an infirmarian supervised, with "eight brothers, one from each langue" accompanying the rounds, so that nursing was itself organised by nation; a scribe wrote down the prescriptions and checked they were followed. Patients could not question the doctors, alter their diet, play cards or dice, or read books "not associated with the christian religion". The publisher's abstract of Luttrell's "The Hospitallers' Medical Tradition 1291-1530" is reported to say that doctors from the Jewish community in Rhodes were regularly welcomed into the hospital to treat patients, but the abstract page returned HTTP 403 in the fact-check and could not be read; treat that sentence as unverified. lecrac names one Jewish doctor: in 1445 the Master employed "Jacunda Gratiano" as "fiscius et professor actis medicine" after he took "an oath on the Jewish scriptures". A Hungarian slave named Helena "who worked in the Hospitaller infirmary on Rhodes was ... manumitted in common form on 22 December 1414, in recognition of her services to the sick" (Coureas, p. 181, citing Luttrell, "Slavery at Rhodes"). So the nursing floor held a slave woman, Jewish physicians, and noble knights on rota, in one room.

**Silver plate: one Rhodes-period witness.** The custom of feeding patients off silver is well documented for the Order's later hospital in Malta (the Sacra Infermeria, 1574). For Rhodes there is one contemporary report, quoted on lecrac: the Czech pilgrim John, Lord of Lobkowitz, who passed through in 1493, wrote that "the sick were served their meals in silver dishes, and they drink too from silver spoons", and that the plate was entrusted to "one Knight of the Order and two clerks, all of them being on oath". Say "a pilgrim in 1493 saw the sick eating off silver here", attributing it to Lobkowitz; the quotation has not been checked against a printed edition of his travels.

## 4. The economic machine (Tell at stops 1, 5, 19-20 and 22)

**Responsions.** The Order was a continent-wide landlord. "Each tongue was subdivided into priories ... The priories number twenty-four, and the commanderies ... 656" (Catholic Encyclopedia, describing the mature system). Commanders remitted "responsions", originally "the entire revenue of the priory, after the deduction of necessary expenses", "sent annually to headquarters"; after 1358 a salaried receiver in each priory collected them for Rhodes (the Camden Society edition of the 1373-75 accounts; note that the Wikipedia Langue article, cited for this in an earlier draft, says nothing about responsions or receivers, so the "entire revenue after expenses" definition and the 1358 date rest on the Camden edition alone and were not re-verified). In 1329 the Venetian Marino Sanudo "expressed surprise that despite an annual income from the responsiones alone of 180,000 florins, of which some 20,000 came from Cyprus, the Hospitallers were unable to provide even two or three galleys" (Luttrell in Setton III). In 1320 "the Hospital owed over 500,000 florins, mainly to the pope's Florentine bankers, the Bardi and Peruzzi"; when those banks failed in the 1340s the Order "lost heavily, more than 360,000 florins" (Luttrell). The Rhodian state ran on a money pipe from Provence, Auvergne, Aragon and England that could be shut by any king, and often was.

**Trade and sugar.** "The Hospitallers themselves profitably traded large quantities of European cloth in the Levant, and marketed in France and Italy the lucrative sugar crop from their estates in Cyprus and Rhodes; they even sent ships to trade in the luxury markets at Alexandria" (Luttrell). The Templars' Cypriot lands, acquired 1312, "proved an invaluable source of supplies in times of dearth at Rhodes". The Order kept "a consul and a fondaco" at Alexandria; a 1516 draft treaty with the Mamluks asked that "merchants from Rhodes should be permitted to build booths at 'Le Brulle' (Burlus) in the Nile delta" (Rossi in Setton III). The customs house was the *commercium* (Coureas, p. 183), and its dues (the *gabelle* and *commerchium*) were farmed out: the Genoese Tobias Lomelino and the Catalan Bartolomeu de Parets bought the farm repeatedly in 1471-74 (Coureas, p. 206). Money: the Rhodian ducat stood at 36.5 aspers in 1449-54 (Coureas, p. 201).

**Slaves.** "Both Hospitaller individuals and the Order as an institution owned slaves, female and male" (Coureas, p. 179). Rhodes "had its own slave market" (p. 181); slaves came from Caffa, Tana and Constantinople (Russians, Circassians, Bulgarians, Tatars) and carried off from their country by ship (whose ships is not stated) (Albanians abducted "by some ships" were to be sold "in Rhodes Town and not elsewhere" by a 1461 ruling; Cypriot fugitives could be enslaved but must be freed after seven years, p. 181-2). In 1392 peace talks with Bayezid "broke down when the Turks demanded the right to trade slaves at Rhodes" (Luttrell). The 1482 treaty with Bayezid II allowed "fugitive Christian slaves" to be "received at the castle of St. Peter at Bodrum" (Rossi). In 1504 the captured Ottoman officer Kemal Beg "was taken as a slave to Rhodes; but on the night of July 20 he succeeded with twelve other slaves in eluding the vigilance of his guards" and drowned trying to reach a Spanish ship (Rossi). The Ottoman fleet of 1522 was rowed by "forty thousand rowers, mostly Christian slaves" (Rossi): the system was symmetrical.

## 5. Two cities: Collachium and Burgh, bells and lights (Tell at stops 4, 5, 7 and 16)

The town was split by an internal wall (line now lost; see verify-deltas) into the Order's *collachium* / *castrum* / *castellum* and the *borgo* (Burgh, Greek *Chora*). Guidebook tradition says "only members of the Order were allowed to remain in the Collachium overnight" (viagallica); the registers show the finer texture: lay people (including women and widows) did own houses in the collachium (section 2 above), so the overnight rule should be phrased as "the Order controlled who slept inside its wall", not "no layman ever slept there".

The Burgh had a night regime of its own. "Men and women circulating there at night by custom always had to have hold a light"; the town's day was closed by bell-ringing, after which movement between quarters stopped (Coureas, p. 206, citing Sarnowsky). The best witness is a privilege of 8 March 1453 to the Jewish couple Ezra and Rebecca, granted "the right to circulate freely between the Upper and Lower Jewish quarters where they and their relations had houses, even during the hours of bellringing, until they came home, and to move around at night bearing a light so as to visit the baths, a pharmacist or a doctor as many times as was necessary" (Coureas, p. 199-200). The bull was Jean de Lastic's answer to the couple's protest at a forced loan demanded by "the cash-strapped treasury"; it also exempted them from the community tax, of which their own share had been four florins a year. Note the incidental data: two Jewish quarters, public baths, a night pharmacist, and a curfew that ordinary residents needed a Grand Master's bull to break.

Leaving was regulated too: "It was expressly forbidden for free women, their dependents and for serfs to depart from Rhodes"; a man who let a relative leave lost his goods, "the goods of his wife were likewise forfeit", and if "a woman board[ed] a ship without the Grand Master's permission, the ship's captain was liable to punishment as well as the woman herself" (Coureas, p. 206). Even the rich Genoese Lomelino was "apprehended" in May 1476 trying to sail home with his wife and daughters before being let go (p. 206). The island was a demographic asset and was policed as one.

The 1345 English visitor saw the Latin town in the collachium: "Within the castle walls are an archbishop and his metropolitan church, and the dwellings of the many citizens are like those of distinguished men. There are money-changers, armorers, and all the artificers necessary to a city or royal castle" (Luttrell). Luttrell adds that Rhodes "was in part a Latin town, where notaries, clerics, doctors, scribes, soldiers, businessmen, and pilgrims from Italy and farther west lived in houses built in a western style".

## 6. Being Greek in Hospitaller Rhodes: status, church, courts (Tell at stops 3, 8, 16 and 17)

**Numbers and the initial deal.** On conquest "the indigenous population of Rhodes had been reduced to perhaps some ten thousand Greeks"; chapters-general of 1311 and 1314 resolved "to maintain five hundred horse and a thousand foot", and in May 1313 the Order "publicly offered lands captured from the Greeks and Turks ... to any Latins who would settle with their families", with "different terms ... for nobles, freemen, and laborers". But "strictly feudal arrangements were rare"; land was leased "to both Latins and Greeks on non-feudal tenures in perpetual emphyteusis" (Luttrell). Luttrell's verdict: "The considerable Greek population retained its Orthodox religion, and its relations with the Hospitallers, whom the Greeks probably regarded as protectors, were remarkably good."

**Four legal conditions.** The registers sort Rhodians into free persons (*franci*, and the intermediate *francomati*, "free person with limited obligations"), *marinarii* (families registered for the *servitudo marina*, the hereditary obligation to row the Order's galleys), serfs (invariably Greek, tied to a *casale*), and slaves (Coureas, passim). Status passed through the mother: "the children of a free Latin man and a Greek woman were considered free" by the 14th-century *Capitula Rodi*, but a marginal addition "ruled the children of Greek women under the obligation of servitudo marina would be liable to perform it and had to live in the borgo even if their father was a free Latin" (Coureas, p. 205, citing Luttrell). The Provençal Richardon Boeuf, who married the serf Sophia with the Master's verbal promise that their children would be *franci*, had to have five of them freed by petition in 1366 because he never got it in writing (p. 184). When Alexios of Nisyros, a *francomatus*, fell in love with the serf Eirene of Zenodotou in 1449, the Grand Master's compromise was that "the male children of this marriage would be francomati obliged to perform the servitudo marina, the female children being serfs" (p. 187). Every emancipation from rowing service had to be balanced: when three sisters were freed in 1422 "their former obligations regarding the servitude marina would henceforth be performed by" named replacement couples (p. 185-6). The Admiral had to be notified of each release "so that Katerina and any children of either sex she might have during her marriage would be considered marinarii" (p. 188-9). Luttrell's study is titled "The Servitudo Marina at Rhodes, 1306-1462"; secondary summaries state that by 1462 the personal rowing obligation had been commuted to a tax and galley labour shifted to slaves (I could not read Luttrell's article itself; treat 1462 as the end-date, the "replaced by a tax" mechanism as reported not verified).

**Greek institutions that survived.** Villages had a *protos* and a *protaria*; Greek ecclesiastical courts sat with sworn jurors (*omotes*), who could be women: "Master Ezra Morbassia and his wife Kale of Kyriakos, jurors of the village of Trianda" bought land in 1450 (Coureas, p. 197). Greek priests married and their daughters brought dowries: in 1450 the Grand Master licensed Synadene, a priest's daughter of Zenodotou, to marry John, a priest's son of Koskinou, ordering "all priests" to bless a marriage "in accordance with the rites and the customs of the Greeks", and noting that "the dowry she brought with her would be incorporated into the social fabric of the village" (p. 198). But "the Grand Master exercised wide-ranging powers among the Greek clergy in the countryside, controlling nominations to Greek churches and monasteries, appointing abbots ... and nominating minor Greek clerics and notaries" (p. 197).

**Metropolitan versus archbishop.** The Knights "evicted the Orthodox metropolitan, and installed a Latin Archbishop in his stead"; Constantinople went on appointing metropolitans in exile ("after 1369 the see of Rhodes was awarded to the metropolitan of Side"), and "the Orthodox community on the island was administered by a council comprising local priests and secular potentates" (Metropolis of Rhodes, Wikipedia; Greek Wikipedia: "a council composed of clergy and lay notables of the island"). Neilos II Diasorenos (1355-69) is the last before the in-commendam gap; "in the early 15th century, the rising power of the Ottomans forced the Knights to adopt a more conciliatory stance, and the Orthodox metropolitans were allowed back". Nathanael I (1437-39) went to Florence and accepted union; on his return, Greek Wikipedia says, the Latin archbishop forbade him to disembark. The Union "met with ardent opposition by the Orthodox populace of the island, forcing the Knights to violently suppress their reactions" (the 1447 date in Wikipedia refers to this Rhodian crisis, not the council). The last Hospitaller-era metropolitan was Klemes (1522-23); his Ottoman-era successor Euthymios (1524-25; English Wikipedia queries the end date) was hanged by the Turks as a conspirator (Greek Wikipedia). The Latin archbishop's cathedral was Our Lady of the Castle at stop 6/7 (research/17). The Latin archbishop also ran a marriage court: in March 1453 he "declared the marriage valid" of a free woman to a manumitted slave and their future children free, and the Order's seneschal formally protested that "the archbishop had no right to declare the future children of the marriage free persons" (Coureas, p. 200-1). Church versus Order over who owns a child: that is the whole polity in one dispute.

**Lay courts.** There was a "judge ordinary in the Rhodian curia", a "judge of appeals" (1391 case, Coureas p. 193), a "judge of the court of the first instance" (Giovanni Rocundo, doctor of laws), a "judge of the Order for criminal cases" (Nicholas Gorgostares, doctor of law, who married his Greek serf mistress after fathering two children by her, 1453, p. 188), a bailli of Rhodes, castellans, and "Latin lawyers manned the judicial courts" (Luttrell). The *Pragmaticae Rhodiae* of February 1510 codified it: adultery by a married man, 100 ducats and three months; the woman "banished by way of penance to a cloister"; bigamists "paraded facing backwards on the back of a donkey throughout the town, with a paper crown on their heads", then "two years' service on board the galleys"; rape "a capital offence"; abortion treated as murder (Coureas, p. 205-6, citing Sarnowsky). Its third book "deals with the issues of dowries and the provision and care of married women" (p. 205).

**Greeks under arms.** In 1522 the defenders included "a few thousand Rhodian citizens under arms" beside 290 knights, 300 sergeants, 500 Genoese and 50 Venetian sailors and 400 Cretans, "perhaps about seventy-five hundred" in all; "Even the women of Rhodes performed prodigies in aiding the wounded, in bringing up ammunition, and in throwing rocks, pitch, sulphur, and burning bitumen" (Rossi in Setton III). A blog on the Castellania says the Greek metropolitan harangued crowds "from the Castellania balcony" in 1522 (lecrac); single-source, use as "it is said".

## 7. Women, nuns, dowries (Tell at stops 3, 13 and 15)

Coureas's summary: women on Rhodes "differed in terms of social class, some being slaves, others being serfs while others were free women, at times wealthy property owners"; most Greek, but also "women of Syrian origin, as well as women of Latin and Jewish origin". Free women bought, inherited and sold land, houses, hospices, windmills and baths (dozens of named examples: Kale Rospi's house "in the neighbourhood of the Greek metropolitan church", 1348; Eudoxia tou Pagomenou compensated in 1429 for a plot "ruined on account of the quarrying of stones for the repair of the towers and ships"). Dowries were large among the Latin elite: Nicoletta de Leone's marriage contract with Bernat de Sant Saturnin turned on 2,000 ducats paid against a receipt for 4,000, and her heiress Alamana was forced by Grand Master Naillac to sell the casale of Fanes for 2,100 florins so he could resell it for 6,000, until the King of Aragon intervened in 1408-9 (Coureas, p. 190). Mixed marriages ran in every direction: Antonio de Murro, "a burgess and citizen of the Town of Rhodes", had a Jewish wife Fustera and mother-in-law Melcha who sued him repeatedly (1441, p. 195); a Greek serf's daughter married a Latin judge (above); Cypriot runaway serfs "married free women there and had had children by them" (1504 Venetian letter, p. 189). Abandonment and adoption: the foundling Antonina was left "near the Latin church of Our Lady of Mercy in the borgo", found by "a group of devout women" and adopted; a two-man commission (the fleet's lieutenant and the first-instance judge) had to rule whether she was free or a *marinaria* before anyone would marry her (p. 196-7).

**Nuns.** Rhodes had no great Latin nunnery, but there were religious women of both rites. The Hospitaller *soror* Margarita of Negroponte held a *casale* and town property (1347-8). In 1440 John Geraki was licensed "to instal in the church or monasterium of the Holy Apostles in the borgo of Rhodes two nuns, one of whom could be his wife", extended in 1445 to "as many nuns as he wished" (p. 204). Widowed Greek property-owners "became nuns and could found rural churches": the nun Kataphyge Alexaina built a Holy Trinity chapel near Psinthos in 1407-8 and is painted in it "dressed in a nun's habit and offering a model of her church to the Saviour" (p. 204). Priests' wives co-founded churches: "the priest Katsambas, his wife Kale and their children founded the church of St George Pachymachiotes at Lindos" in 1394-5, Kale styled "kyra and magistrissa" (p. 204).

**Violence.** Femicide "was a pardonable crime according to the extant evidence": Antonius Raynerius, who killed the widow Kale with "several blows with his sword" (1365), and Michael Turcomano, who killed his wife (1442), were both pardoned on petition (p. 206-7).

## 8. The Jews of Hospitaller Rhodes (Tell at stops 13 and 14)

The Jewish quarter lay "next to the city wall near the port" (Jewish Virtual Library); the 1453 privilege shows "Upper and Lower Jewish quarters". The community paid a collective tax of "50 Rhodian florins per annum" (Coureas, p. 199-200). In the 1480 siege "the Jews fought valiantly", "their houses were torn down to reinforce the wall, and the fighting reached the synagogue"; d'Aubusson "erected two churches on the site" and the synagogue was abandoned, though "in recognition of their bravery, the Jews were later allowed to rebuild it" (JVL). Obadiah of Bertinoro, who stopped in 1487-88, found "only twenty-two families ... all impoverished"; they "subsist with difficulty on vegetables, not eating bread or meat; nor do they buy any wine, for fear of getting into disputes with the Greeks"; "the Jewish women occupy themselves doing all kinds of handiwork for the nobles of the land and thus support their husbands"; the men "allow their hair to grow long and are beautiful in appearance"; and he records that "the governor" (his word for the Grand Master) "granted them a hundred ducats from the revenues of the town to build a new synagogue" (chabad.org's English text of his letter; JVL: the community "was living in poverty, mainly on its womenfolk's earnings", the Pilgrim of Cologne noting the women's skill in silk clothes, some men in tanning). After the plague of 1498-1500 the Knights decided "to expel within 40 days all Jews who would not become baptized", allowing emigrants to sail only to Nice; "in the next two decades" some 2,000-3,000 Jews captured by the Order's ships were brought to Rhodes and kept as slaves to work on the fortifications (JVL, which neither dates the decree nor names the Master). Pierre d'Aubusson's Wikipedia biography attributes the measure to him: in his last years (he died 1503) he succeeded "in extirpating Judaism in Rhodes, by expelling all adult Jews and forcibly baptizing their children"; the year 1502 usually given for the decree was not found in any source reached this round, so say "about 1502". The Ottomans then resettled Jews from Thessaloniki in 1523 (research/11). Kahal Shalom (1577) at stop 13 is therefore an Ottoman-era foundation, not a Knights' survival.

## 9. The Castellania: courthouse and market law (Tell at stop 4)

The square building on Hippocrates Square with the outside stair was the Order's courthouse on its "Magna et Communis Platea", the great common square; a minority view makes it the Mercantile Court (*Basilica Mercatorum*). Its lintel angel holds "the arms of the Order in his left hand and those of Grand Master d'Amboise in his right", and a Gothic frame carries "the arms of Grand Master d'Amboise and the date 1507" (lecrac Castellania post; the Rhodes municipal guide page cited for the same reading could not be reached in the fact-check, so lecrac is the only source read for the d'Amboise attribution). Research/06 and some guides say "d'Aubusson 1507", which is impossible: d'Aubusson died in 1503; Emery d'Amboise was Grand Master 1503-12. Correct to d'Amboise. The upper hall keeps "a virtually intact early 16th century painted ceiling"; under the Ottomans the upper storey was a mosque with a fish market on the ground floor (lecrac); its present use as the municipal library and the Dodecanese historical archive is common knowledge in guides but was not verified against any source reached this round. The square in front is where the *commercium* deeds of slave sales were registered "by the scribe William Martini" (1453, Coureas p. 183), and where the 1510 *Pragmaticae* would have been enforced; Sokratous street running uphill from it was the bazaar.

## 10. 1522: the terms and who left (Tell at stops 12, 22 and 1)

On 9 December 1522 the Grand Master "called together the council of the order, and it voted to accept the terms offered"; after a broken truce and the loss of the post of Aragon on 17 December, "on the next day the grand master surrendered, and the terms were defined on December 20. The knights had twelve days to leave Rhodes. The inhabitants were guaranteed security of person, of property, and of religion, and were exempted for five years from the levy of boys to enter the corps of janissaries" (Rossi in Setton III). Wikipedia adds: churches not to be desecrated, islanders exempt from Ottoman taxation for five years, three years for anyone to leave (research/05). Porter: "Four thousand of the Christian inhabitants of Rhodes preferred to follow the fortunes of the knights into exile rather than remain under the sway of the Turk"; "On the night of the 1st January, 1523, this sad event took place" (Porter, ch. 15; Italian Wikipedia gives the same 4,000). Some sources give 5,000, or a few hundred who actually settled in Malta; treat 4,000 as the standard figure for those who sailed. In Malta "the Greeks came with the Knights upon expulsion from Rhodes. They settled in Birgu", and the Bishop of Malta gave them "the chapel of St Catherine, together with the chapels of St George and the Visitation ... for their own use of divine services according to the Byzantine tradition"; they brought "the icon of Our Lady of Damascus ... from Rhodes", and the chapel "continued to be used by the Greek population until 1780" (Wikipedia, Chapel of Our Lady of Damascus). Rossi: "Memories of Rhodes followed the order to its new seat in Malta. There were built in Malta churches and palaces with the same names as those in Rhodes: the churches of St. John, St. Catherine, and Our Lady of Victory, and the auberges of the langues." And the Ottomans "did not touch the fortress, the auberges of the langues, the buildings of the castellany, or the magnificent hospital ... and they left almost intact the coats of arms, the gateways, and the inscriptions" (Rossi). What the walker sees is the household's shell with the household gone.

## Continuities and contrasts

- **The household as unit of rule.** An ancient Rhodian *oikos* (see the Colossus and city-state files), the Hospitaller *auberge*, an Ottoman-era courtyard house and today's family taverna all organise life around a common table; the Order's novelty was a household with no women and no heirs, recruited by proof of birth, which is why it needed written rules for everything a family does by custom (meals, silence, clothing, who sleeps where).
- **Status by mother.** Ancient Rhodes counted citizenship through both parents; the Knights' *Capitula Rodi* let a Latin father free his children but a marginal note dragged the rowing-service mother's children back into the borgo. Ottoman law sorted by religion and tax, not birth; modern Greek law by citizenship. Every regime had a registry and a man whose job was to say what you were (the Admiral for *marinarii*, the archbishop for marriages, the mukhtar, the town hall).
- **Bells and lights.** The Hospitaller curfew (no movement after the bells without a lamp) has a direct descendant in the Ottoman night-watch and in the modern quiet hours (*koini isychia*) that Rhodian municipalities still post; the 1453 Jewish couple's pass to visit "the baths, a pharmacist or a doctor" at night is the earliest Rhodian record of a 24-hour pharmacy rota.
- **Care as obligation.** Asclepieia on Kos and the Hospital ward at stop 6 both treated the sick as guests of a god; the Order's "our lords the sick" and its Jewish and Christian doctors on one payroll anticipate the mixed public hospital that the Italians built and the Greek state runs.
- **The remittance economy.** Responsions from 656 commanderies to Rhodes are the medieval version of the diaspora money that kept Dodecanesian villages alive in the 20th century; in both cases the island lived on income earned elsewhere and was vulnerable when the pipe closed.
- **Sanctuary and leaving.** Greeks who sailed in 1523 kept their rite in Birgu for 250 years; Greeks who stayed were pushed to the *marasia* outside the walls (research/11). A Rhodian today knows both stories as family history.

## Uncertain or contested

- Exact Rhodes-period nobility proofs (number of quarters) vary by langue and decade; "sixteen quarters" is a Malta-era rule.
- Silver plate in the Rhodes ward: one Rhodes-period witness (Lobkowitz 1493, as quoted on lecrac); well attested for Malta.
- Jacunda Gratiano (1445): single web source (lecrac), which cites nothing; likely drawn from Luttrell but unverified against any printed text.
- Luttrell's statement that Jewish doctors were regularly welcomed into the hospital: publisher's abstract unreachable (HTTP 403); unverified.
- Castellania: the municipal guide's reading of the relief and the building's present use as library/archive were not verified this round.
- Servitudo marina "abolished 1462 and replaced by a tax": inferred from Luttrell's article title and secondary summaries, not read in the original.
- Castellania date and patron: relief dated 1507 with d'Amboise's arms; "d'Aubusson 1507" in guides is an error. The metropolitan's speech from its balcony in 1522 is blog-level tradition.
- 4,000 émigrés of 1523: Porter and it.wikipedia; other figures (5,000; "5-600") exist and the number who reached Malta in 1530 is unknown.
- The "1447" Union crisis: Wikipedia's date; the council itself was 1438-39.
- The collachium overnight rule is guidebook wording (viagallica); the registers show laywomen owning houses inside it.

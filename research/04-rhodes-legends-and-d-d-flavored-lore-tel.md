# Rhodes legends and D&D-flavored lore: Telchines, Helios and Rhodos, Tlepolemus, the Dragon of Malpasso and Dieudonné de Gozon, the Knights' relics, Caesar/Cicero/Tiberius on Rhodes, the Rhodian school of sculpture and the Rhodian Sea Law

# Rhodes for a dungeon-master's birthday: legends, monsters, relics and where to tell them

## 1. The island that rose from the sea (tell at the Mandraki deer columns, or in front of the Palace, which sits on the Temple of Helios)
Pindar wrote Olympian 7 in 464 BC for the Rhodian boxer Diagoras, and it is the island's founding myth in verse. When the gods drew lots for the earth, Helios was away driving the sun and got no share. Offered a re-draw, he refused: "he himself saw in the gray sea, growing from the bottom, a rich, productive land for men." He claimed the rising island, "lay with Rhodes, and begat seven sons who inherited from him the wisest minds in the time of earlier men" (Pindar, Ol. 7). Diodorus adds the D&D detail: the seven Heliadae were astrologer-kings who "established the division of the day into hours"; the cleverest, Tenages, "was slain by his brothers because of their envy," and the four murderers fled to Lesbos, Cos, Caria and Egypt (Diod. 5.56-57). The innocent brothers stayed; Cercaphus' sons Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros named the three cities. Stand at the harbour mouth where the deer columns are and say: recent study puts the foundations of the ancient Temple of Helios exactly under the Palace of the Grand Master behind you. D&D hook: a pantheon lottery, a sun-god's bastard island, seven brothers and one fratricide.

## 2. The Telchines, sorcerer-smiths of the first age (tell at the Temple of Aphrodite ruins inside Liberty Gate)
Before the Heliadae came the Telchines. Strabo says the island was once called "Telchinis, after the Telchines, who took up their abode in the island." Two traditions fight over them. One: "maligners and sorcerers, who pour the water of the Styx mixed with sulphur upon animals and plants in order to destroy them." The other: they "excelled in workmanship" and were "maligned by rival workmen... they were the first to work iron and brass, and in fact fabricated the scythe for Cronus" (Strabo 14.2.7) - the sickle that castrated the sky; later tradition also gives them Poseidon's trident. Diodorus calls them "children of Thalatta" (the Sea) who nursed the infant Poseidon, "the first to fashion statues of gods," wizards who "could summon clouds and rain and hail at their will and likewise could even bring snow," who "could also change their natural shapes," and were "jealous of teaching their arts to others" (Diod. 5.55). Ovid gives them the evil eye: as Medea flies over Ialysos she sees the Telchines "whose bad eyes' corrupting power shot forth; Jove, utterly despising, thrust them deep beneath his brother's waves" (Met. 7.365-7). Diodorus says they foresaw the flood and left. Why Aphrodite's temple? Diodorus continues: the Telchine Halia bore Poseidon six sons and a daughter, Rhodos; when Aphrodite, sailing from Cythera to Cyprus, tried to land, the arrogant sons drove her off, and the goddess "in her wrath, brought madness upon them"; Halia "cast herself into the sea" and became Leucothea. The three low column stumps and podium in Symi Square are a 3rd-century-BC temple to the goddess the island once insulted. D&D hook: a lost guild of artificer-warlocks, weather magic, Styx-and-brimstone poison, shapeshifters, a divine flood, and the Sickle of Cronus as legendary artifact.

## 3. Tlepolemus, the Heraclid who took Rhodes to Troy (tell at the ancient stadium on Monte Smith)
Pindar again: "striking Licymnius, the bastard brother of Alcmena, with a staff of hard olive-wood as he came out of the chamber of Midea, the founder of this land once killed that man." Apollo's oracle sent the manslayer by ship "straight to the pasture land with sea all around it." Homer's Catalogue of Ships lists Tlepolemus, "famed for his spear," with nine ships from Rhodes, "ordered in three parts" - Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros - and says Zeus "poured wondrous wealth" on the Rhodians (Il. 2.653-670). At Troy he met Sarpedon, son of Zeus, wounded him and died. Pausanias adds a horror-story coda: his widow Polyxo avenged him by killing Helen when she fled to Rhodes. The 210-metre 3rd-century-BC stadium below the Temple of Apollo is where games were run in the founder's honour; Pindar says the Rhodians sacrificed to Tlepolemus "as to a god." D&D hook: a fallen champion exiled for manslaughter, a hero cult, funeral games.

## 4. The Dragon of Malpasso and Dieudonné de Gozon (tell at the d'Amboise Gate; second beat on the site of St John's church in front of the Palace)
The legend as told since the 16th century. Around 1340, under Grand Master Hélion de Villeneuve, a monster lairs in the marsh and caves at Malpasso ("bad passage"), on the coast road about two kilometres south-west of the walls under the hill now called Monte Smith, eating cattle and shepherds. Knights die trying; the Grand Master forbids any further attempt. A young Provençal knight, Dieudonné de Gozon, goes home to his father's castle, builds a wood-and-canvas dragon, hides meat under its belly and for months trains two mastiffs to dart at the soft underside and his horse to charge it. Back on Rhodes he rides out with two squires. His lance shatters on the scales; the dogs hang on the belly; he drives his sword into the unarmoured underside; the dying beast falls on him and the squires revive him with cold water. Villeneuve strips him of the habit for disobedience, then, moved by his humility, restores it; in 1346 Gozon becomes Grand Master (to 1353). The head is nailed over a gate. Schiller turned this into a ballad in 1798 whose moral is "Gehorsam ist des Christen Schmuck" - obedience is the Christian's ornament.

Now the historian's cut. F. W. Hasluck (Annual of the British School at Athens, 1913-14) found no source before a 1521 pilgrim's account, and there the hero is an anonymous "Rhodian knight"; Bosio, writing in the 1590s in Malta, first names Gozon; the traveller Thevenot in the 1650s saw a head over a gate, "larger than that of a horse, with a huge mouth and teeth and very large eyes"; sources disagree whether it hung on the St Anthony, d'Amboise or Koskinou gate. Local tradition today points at the d'Amboise Gate and says workmen threw the head out during repairs around 1837-39; those who saw it called it a crocodile (one writer, a hippopotamus). Hasluck's explanation: Gozon marched in French Rogation processions in which a model dragon, the "spirit of evil," was ritually slain, and the parade became a biography. Gozon's real tomb lay in the Conventual Church of St John of the Collachio, opposite the Palace; the church was destroyed in 1856 when lightning ignited the gunpowder stored beneath it. In 1877 his plain tomb slab was shipped to France; it is in the Musée de Cluny in Paris, and the famous "Draconis Extinctor" epitaph rests on Bosio's word alone. Stand in the triple gate, look up at the archway, and let the listener decide. D&D hook: a quest forbidden by your own order, training on a dummy, a crocodile-skull trophy over the city gate.

## 5. The relics of the Order (tell on the site of St John's church; extend at Filerimos if you go)
Three treasures left Rhodes with the Knights. The icon of Our Lady of Philermos, an 11th-12th-century Byzantine Virgin kept at Filerimos on the acropolis of ancient Ialysos, credited with the 1480 deliverance, when Mesih Pasha's Janissaries broke into the Italian bastion and Grand Master d'Aubusson, "wounded in five places," led the counter-attack with a lance. The right hand of John the Baptist, kept in Constantinople from 956, seized in 1453, and sent to the Knights by Sultan Bayezid II in 1484 to sweeten the deal by which they held his rival brother Cem prisoner for 45,000 ducats a year. And a fragment of the True Cross. On 1 January 1523, under Suleiman's terms, the Knights sailed "with banners flying, drums beating," carrying their relics: to Malta in 1530 (St Lawrence in Vittoriosa, then St John's Co-Cathedral); in 1798 Napoleon took Malta and in 1799 the relics were handed to Tsar Paul I at Gatchina, where the icon was dressed in a diamond riza with a Maltese-cross halo. The Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna carried them out of revolutionary Russia via Copenhagen (1920); King Alexander of Yugoslavia received them in Belgrade (1932); they were hidden at Ostrog monastery in 1941; Yugoslav security seized them in 1951 and locked them in a vault in Cetinje; their survival was revealed only in 1993. Today the hand and the Cross are in Cetinje Monastery and the icon in the National Museum of Montenegro; a replica hangs at Filerimos. D&D hook: an artifact chain across 900 years, a Sultan's bribe, a nine-century game of keep-away.

## 6. Pirates, orators and a lonely prince (tell at Rodini Park, or on the Acropolis by the odeon and gymnasium)
Rhodes was the finishing school of the Roman elite. Aeschines, beaten by Demosthenes in 330 BC, opened a school of rhetoric here; tradition sites it in the Rodini valley. In the winter of about 75 BC young Julius Caesar, sailing to Rhodes "to study under Apollonius Molo, the most eminent teacher of oratory of that time," was taken by Cilician pirates off Pharmacussa. They asked 20 talents; "he laughed at them for not knowing who it was they had captured" and offered 50. For 38 days he read them his poems, called the unimpressed "illiterate Barbarians" to their faces, and promised to crucify them. Ransomed, he manned ships at Miletus, caught them at anchor, and crucified them - though "he ordered that their throats be cut first" (Suetonius, Julius 4 and 74; Plutarch, Caesar 1-3). Cicero had just left (78-77 BC), having studied with Molon and the Stoic Posidonius; Molon, who taught that the first three rules of rhetoric were "Delivery, Delivery and Delivery," curbed his over-luxuriant style, and Cicero says he came home after two years "not only better trained but almost transformed" (Brutus 316). Posidonius measured the Earth from Rhodes by the star Canopus; Pompey lowered his fasces at his door. Then in 6 BC Tiberius, heir to Augustus, walked away from Rome and spent eight years here in "a modest house and a villa in the suburbs," going to the gymnasium without lictors and arguing in the lecture halls. Once, saying he wished to visit the sick, he found every invalid in town lined up in a colonnade and apologised to each one. He kept the astrologer Thrasyllus. On a cliff walk, convinced the man was a fraud, he resolved to push him off - and at that instant Thrasyllus pointed to a ship and said it brought good news: the recall to Rome (Suetonius, Tiberius 11-14). D&D hook: a bard's origin story, a warlock-patron's loyalty test on a cliff.

## 7. Rhodian marble and Rhodian law (tell in the Archaeological Museum courtyard; then at the Marine Gate)
Pliny on the Laocoön: "in the palace of the emperor Titus, a work superior to any painting and any bronze... carved from a single block... by those eminent craftsmen Hagesander, Polydorus and Athenodorus, all of Rhodes" (NH 36.37); the same names are signed in Tiberius's grotto at Sperlonga. And in Asinius Pollio's collection, "Zethus and Amphion, and then Dirce and the bull with its rope, all carved from the same block," brought from Rhodes, by Apollonius and Tauriscus (36.34): the Farnese Bull in Naples, the largest single sculpture recovered from antiquity. Then the harbour. Strabo: Rhodes "held the mastery of the sea for a long time" and "overthrew the business of piracy," feeding its poor by ancestral custom so the fleet never lacked crews (14.2.5). Its law outlived its navy. Digest 14.2.1: "It is provided by the Rhodian Law that where merchandise is thrown overboard for the purpose of lightening a ship, what has been lost for the benefit of all must be made up by the contribution of all" - general average, still in every marine policy. Digest 14.2.9: Eudaemon of Nicomedia, shipwrecked at Icaria and robbed by islanders, petitions the emperor Antoninus, who answers: "I am, indeed, the Lord of the World, but the Law is the Lord of the sea; and this affair must be decided by the Rhodian law." A Byzantine code of c. 600-800 borrowed the name Nomos Rhodion Nautikos. Birthday close: climb the Roloi clock tower or walk the mole to St Nicholas fort for the harbour view where all of this began.

## Open questions
- Exact wording of Cicero, Brutus 316 (the 'came back after two years not only better trained but almost transformed' line): could not fetch a primary text online this session; paraphrase used - verify against a Loeb/Perseus edition before scripting a direct quote.
- A 'thorn of the Crown of Thorns' among the Knights' relics: the sources consulted (Wikipedia Our Lady of Philermos, Cetinje Monastery, John the Baptist relic pages) list the hand, the icon and a True Cross fragment as the relics that went to Paul I in 1799; no source found for a thorn making that journey. Confirm before including.
- Which gate the 'dragon's head' hung on: Hasluck lists St Anthony, d'Amboise and Koskinou as candidates; modern local sources say d'Amboise. Present as tradition, not fact.
- Exact footprint of the Church of St John of the Collachio (destroyed 1856): pin is an estimate in the square between the Palace and the top of the Street of the Knights; an on-site plaque or the Ephorate's plan would fix it.
- Precise location of Malpasso and the 'dragon's cave': the Brill (Frankokratia 2024) abstract places it about 2 km SW of the Old Town on the coast road; popular sites say a cave off the 'Mal Paso' path on Monte Smith. Pin is approximate.
- Gozon's tomb slab at the Musée de Cluny: confirmed there by the Museum of the Order of St John and Hasluck (1877 transfer; 'Catalogue du Musée des Thermes No. 422'); the current inventory number and whether it is on display were not verified.
- Autumn opening hours on 30 Sep 2026 for the Palace, Archaeological Museum and Filerimos: official schedule says summer hours run to 31 Oct (08:00-20:00), but a 2025/26 guide says hours shorten fortnightly through Sept-Oct; confirm the week before. Roloi clock tower hours/price unverified.
- Whether the Acropolis/Monte Smith site is fenced with fixed hours in 2026 (currently described as an open-air park with no fee).
- Tiberius's actual house/villa location on Rhodes is unknown; the Acropolis gymnasium is used as the story spot on the strength of Suetonius' 'gymnasium' reference only.
- Aeschines' school at Rodini is local tradition (aegeanislands.gr), not attested in ancient sources; Cicero's and Caesar's attendance 'at Rodini' is likewise tradition.
- Popular pages give the Grand Master who forbade the dragon hunt as Hélion de Villeneuve; Vertot dates the episode to 1342. Not independently verified against Vertot's text.

# Jamie topic 7: The Jewish community of Rhodes as a social organism

Builds on `research/11-later-layers-of-rhodes-town-ottoman-1522.md` (La Juderia section: the 1523 resettlement, Kahal Shalom, the museum, the 1944 deportation timeline, Ülkümen, the 2002 memorial, opening hours). Nothing there is repeated at length here; this file goes underneath it, into how the community was organised, married, named, fed, schooled, taxed, judged and scattered.

## Frame for the scriptwriter

For four centuries the eastern third of the walled town was a self-governing Ladino-speaking organism with its own court, its own charities, its own school system, its own calendar and a web of cousin marriages that reached every household. It was not a ghetto in the Italian sense (no locked gate; Greeks and Turks came in to trade), but it was a *kehillah*: a corporation of households that taxed itself, buried itself, educated itself and settled its own disputes, under Ottoman rulers who were happy for it to do so and under Italian rulers who at first subsidised it and then, in 1938, turned on it. Everything Jamie cares about (households, kinship, work, calendars, welfare, law, who lives where and why) is legible in a quarter you can cross in four minutes: the wide street where brides processed, the fountain where girls drew water and were looked at, the plaque at 4 Vizantiou where the dead were prayed over, the one synagogue that survived the bombs, the square where 1,673 people stood in July 1944. The arc to tell is: a Greek-speaking Romaniote community from antiquity, overwritten by Spanish exiles after 1523; peak (4,000-5,000) around 1900; emigration of the young men to Africa and Seattle from 1896; Italian modernisation and then Italian racial law; the deportation; and the odd afterlife of a community that now exists mostly as a diaspora which comes back every July.

## Tell at stops 1 and 2 (berth and Gate of the Virgin): the Jews' Wall and the community before the Sephardim

- Jamie walks off the ship straight into the old Jewish quarter. The stretch of rampart she passes on the way to the Gate of the Virgin is what the Knights called the "Jews' Wall": the 1906 Jewish Encyclopedia records that the Hospitallers "gave the name 'Jews' Wall' to that part which encircled the Jewish quarter", and the 1480 siege narrative (research/05) has the Ottoman guns on exactly this south-east angle. The Jewish Encyclopedia adds that d'Aubusson "ordered the Jews' houses to be razed" for building material during the siege works, which matches Porter's account in research/05 of houses levelled behind the breach.
- The community is old. Rhodes appears in the Roman decree of c. 142 BCE notifying allied cities of the renewal of friendship between the Senate and the Jewish nation (1 Maccabees 15, cited by Jewish Virtual Library and the Jewish Encyclopedia). Wikipedia's article on the Jews of Rhodes notes the 1st-century BCE anti-Jewish polemic of Apollonius Molon, the Rhodian rhetorician who taught Cicero and Caesar (research/15 covers Molon's school): the earliest evidence of Jewish-Greek friction on the island is a Rhodian professor's pamphlet.
- Benjamin of Tudela, the Navarrese traveller of the 1160s-70s, in the Adler translation: "From Samos it is three days to Rhodes, where there are about 400 Jews, at their head being R. Abba, R. Hannanel, and R. Elijah." (Samos had 300 under R. Shemaria, R. Obadiah and R. Joel.) These were Romaniote Jews: Greek-speaking, with their own liturgy, present "since the Roman era" (Leon Taranto, Avotaynu). Note: jewishrhodes.org gives Benjamin's visit as "1116", which is wrong; his journey is dated c. 1165-73.
- Under the Knights the community had scholars (Rabbi Ezra of Rhodes, Rabbi Yedidiah son of Yosef Rak, per Wikipedia), and a 1480 traveller praised the silk-working of the Jewish women. Then came the reckoning. After the 1480 victory, according to the chronicler Elijah Capsali as summarised in the Jewish Encyclopedia, d'Aubusson "ordered the Jews to embrace Christianity"; some accepted baptism, some chose death, some were sold. On 9 January 1502 he "decreed the expulsion of the Jews from Rhodes, under the pretext that they were corrupting the morals of the young"; those leaving "could leave with their belongings but had to sail for Nice" (Jewish Virtual Library). Wikipedia's d'Aubusson article says he "expelled all adult Jews and forcibly baptized their children." Taranto and jewishrhodes.org say the decree was not fully executed before d'Aubusson's death in July 1503, and jewishrhodes.org says only "22 Jewish families" survived the 1480 fighting. The exact scale is contested; the shape is not.
- The Knights also held 2,000-3,000 Jewish captives (taken by piracy) as slave labour on the fortifications, and Jewish Virtual Library says "in 1522, these captives helped the Turks conquer the fortress of Rhodes." So the walls Jamie is walking beside were partly built by enslaved Jews, and partly betrayed by them.

## Tell at stop 13 (Kahal Shalom): the Sephardic refoundation and the institutions of a self-governing quarter

**The refoundation.** Research/11 has the 1523 resettlement of 150 families from Salonika. The 1906 Jewish Encyclopedia adds the terms, which read like a colonial charter: Suleiman "granted them a firman guaranteeing freedom from taxation for twenty years, and decreeing that each family be provided with a house free of expense." (Treat the twenty-years-and-a-house detail as tradition reported by a 1906 encyclopedia, not as a document anyone has shown.) Jewish Virtual Library: under Suleiman the community "numbered 144 Jewish household heads"; it later called itself "the small Jerusalem". Taranto: the Sephardic language and customs "replaced that of Romaniote Jews who had lived there since the Roman era"; the older stratum was absorbed, not expelled. (Taranto and jewishrhodes.org also claim Spanish Jews from Aragon/Tarragona arrived as early as 1280; this is asserted without a source and should be spoken as "some say".)

**The building.** Research/11 has the 1577 date, central bimah, two arks. Add: the courtyard plaque is dated "the month of Kislev, 5338 (1577)" (jewishrhodes.org); the *tevah* faces south-east toward Jerusalem; the floor is the black-and-white pebble mosaic (*chochlaki*) used across Rhodes, so a synagogue floor and a Turkish courtyard and a Knights' hospital floor are the same craft; there is "a fountain in the courtyard used for handwashing by the Kohanim before reciting the priestly blessings" (Wikipedia); the women's balcony was only added "during the 1930s" (jewishrhodes.org), i.e. before that women prayed in the side rooms that are now the six rooms of the museum; wall plaques are in Ladino and Hebrew, and one in French names the murdered (Wikipedia). The Jewish Encyclopedia actually says the Shalom synagogue was "built in 1593 by Raphael Margola"; Jewish Virtual Library reconciles the two dates as "founded later, in 1577, and renovated in 1593". The 15th-century Iberian Torah scroll that was used here for centuries is now in the National Library of Israel; its rescue belongs at stop 19 (below).

**The other five houses of prayer** (Rhodes Jewish Museum, "The Jews of Rhodes" page; jguideeurope): Kahal Grande / Kahal Kadosh Gadol, "believed to have been built in the late 1400's after the Turkish siege of 1480", i.e. rebuilt with the Knights' permission, on Kisthiniou Street near the ramparts, destroyed by wartime bombardment; Kahal Midrash, "built in 1865 by the wealthy Camondo family" (the Constantinople banking dynasty); Kahal Tikkun Hazot, "built in the 1870's"; plus two oratories. In the 1930s: "four synagogues, two oratories, and a Rabbinical College."

**Law and welfare.** The museum's customs page is explicit that "under Ottoman and Italian rule, Jews operated autonomous judicial systems. The Bet Din (Rabbinical Court) handled ritual, business, family law, inheritance, and criminal cases." Divorce (*get*) was rare but "carried no taint of sinfulness". Inheritance is where Rhodes bent the rule: Jewish law "did not normally allow daughters to inherit with brothers, yet early custom among the Jews of Rhodes did permit a married woman [to] inherit a 'nice portion' of her father's estate", and when brothers had no objection sisters routinely shared. Welfare ran through three bodies: **Bikur Holim** (the sick and the dead; a 1924 plaque survives at the *Kay de la Talmud Torah*, now 4 Vizantiou Street), **Ozer Dalim** (*Kupat Ozer Dalim*, alms), and the **Fundo Secreto**, a secret fund for the poor who would be shamed by being seen to receive. Plus a Refectory serving meals, a Parents' Association sponsoring poor scholars, a Ladies' Society. Rich men's names are still on the walls: Salomon Alhadeff's family gave the town land in 1933 for Alhadeff Park (museum: "In 1933 the land was donated to the city of Rhodes by the prominent Jewish family of Salomon Alhadeff"). The further benefactor details (Moshe Menashe "Kodja Mussani" as customs treasurer and his 1935 endowment of the yeshiva *hamam*; "the lungs of the quarter"; Isaac Alhadeff's 1935 gift for poor students at *Kay de los Ricos*, now 5 Gavala Street; the Joseph Notrica Foundation's 1920s community centre on *Kay de la Eskola*, now Kisthiniou) could not be located on any of the cited pages during fact-checking and should be treated as unverified plaque-readings, not scripted as fact. The Alhadeffs were the bankers: "In 1819, Behor Alhadeff founded a bank which later became Solomon Alhadeff and Sons" (Jewish Virtual Library).

**Schools: who controls the children.** This is the community's internal politics made visible. Boys were taught by private teachers hired by groups of families. In 1864 a Talmud Torah, "Bet Yakov", was built with money from Baron James de Rothschild. In 1882 Moses Franco, also the French vice-consul, opened the "Progress" school teaching Hebrew, Turkish and French; "it closed after five years due to fierce objection from the local rabbis. Similar attempts had the same fate" (jewishrhodes.org Alliance PDF). Abraham Galante opened Tifereth Israel in 1895 and a Talmud Torah in 1899 (JVL). "In the early 1900s the Alliance Israélite Universelle opened separate boys' and girls' schools" (JVL; the precise years 1888/1901/1902 sometimes quoted could not be sourced and should not be spoken); in August 1903 Baron Edmond de Rothschild and his wife, visiting, gave 15,000 francs, and the purpose-built school on Kisthiniou Street was finished at the end of 1904, "attended not only by boys but girls as well", unlike earlier Jewish schools on Rhodes, teaching in French, with the declared aim "to free the children from the prison of their home language (Judeo-Spanish)". It drew some non-Jewish pupils. Then the Italians: June 1917, all schools (Turkish, Greek, Jewish) put under an Italian inspector-general; after Lausanne (1923-24) the Alliance directors were dismissed and the schools renamed *Scuole Israelitiche Italiane*. The building was wrecked in the war: the museum says it "was damaged during the bombings of World War II and eventually came down during the 1960's"; jguideeurope says "destroyed by bombardments during the war"; no source gives a year, so do not say "1943". The claim that Chief Rabbi Reuben Eliyahu Israel (1921-33), last of a rabbinic dynasty that supplied two Sephardic chief rabbis of Jerusalem, translated Psalms and Proverbs into Judeo-Spanish and wrote "tenemos un dover sacro de studiar profundamente nuestra istoria para saver quien somos nosotros" (we have a sacred duty to study our history deeply to know who we are) could not be traced to any cited page in fact-checking; treat the quotation as unverified until a source is found.

**The Collegio Rabbinico (1928).** Set up with Italian government subsidy under Governor Mario Lago (Wikipedia gives 1927, the museum and Encyclopedia.com 1928), it drew "professors and students from several countries and earned an international reputation" (museum; JVL says it was founded "to serve the Italian Levant (the Balkans, Ereẓ Israel, Egypt, and Ethiopia)"; the specific list Italy/Istanbul/Jerusalem/Alexandria and the 1935 *concerto vocale strumentale* are unsourced and should be dropped). It "closed in 1938 before the Fascist racial laws were promulgated" (JVL); Encyclopedia.com says instead it "was closed down at the outbreak of World War II", so say "closed in 1938-39". Its most consequential graduate was Moïse Lévy (born Antalya 1915, raised on Rhodes), who at 22 went to Elisabethville in the Belgian Congo in 1937 and led that community, mostly Rhodeslis, for 53 years. An Italian-funded seminary on a Greek island trained the rabbi of a Ladino congregation in Katanga: that is the diaspora in one sentence.

**Visiting** (hours are in research/11; additions from jewishrhodes.org plan-your-visit, 2026): also "closed for visits on Jewish festivals"; large bags not allowed; step-free access only in the synagogue; fully renovated museum reopened 9 November 2025. 30 September 2026 is an intermediate day of Sukkot; whether that counts as a festival closure is not stated, so email jcrhodes@otenet.gr.

## Tell at stop 14 (Square of the Jewish Martyrs): the wide street, the fountain, and the household

The square is the former *La Kaye Ancha* ("the wide street"), "the central gathering place through which weddings and other joyous processions passed" (jewishrhodes.org). The museum: the plaza had a "large fountain where young women drew water", and the old fountain "was later replaced by the seahorse design that still exists today", built under the Italians. So the seahorses mark the exact place where the quarter's marriage market operated in plain sight: girls fetching water, boys watching. The quarter was not uniform: "tall, stone, palatial homes" beside "more humble dwellings built with mud-bricks"; a *Calle de los Ricos* (street of the rich; Gavala Street is given as *Kay de los Ricos*); courtyards (*cortijos*) shared by several families, floored in pebble mosaic, where Ladino *romanzas* were sung. Population: c. 500 at the end of the 16th century and 4,500-5,000 at the end of the 19th are attributed to jewishrhodes.org but were not found on its historic-background page in fact-checking (that page gives "about 1,800 Jews" in 1941, which is far too low and contradicts every other source); the Jewish Encyclopedia c. 1905 says about 4,000 in the town; Taranto says the peak was about 5,000 and the wartime community "fewer than 3,000". (Wikipedia's table gives "11,000" for the late 19th century; nothing else supports that and it should not be used.)

**The household.** The museum's customs page: "Multi-generational households were common, with the oldest male holding authority." Men answered for "family welfare and maintenance, religious devotion and children's education, and caring for the elderly"; women's work "was endless", but they "found time to socialize, embroider, knit, crochet, and sew deep into night." The trousseau (*ashuar*) was begun at fifteen and kept in a *baul*, a chest of wood, velvet and metal. The engagement display (*vijita*) began at 3 pm with sweets on trays and a silver spoon-holder (*kuchariera*); the bridal bath (*baño de novia*) the day before the wedding, the bride in mother-of-pearl clogs (*takos de boda*), water poured from a brass *tas*, face under an embroidered *yasmak*. Weddings were on Friday: the bride's father led her, with musicians, through the Juderia; before leaving the house she said "with your permission, I want to go with my husband", kissed the mezuzah, and rice was thrown; in the synagogue the couple stood under a prayer-shawl canopy while the rabbi read the *ketubah* (the museum shows one dated 1873). Gifts were lace embroidered "Dio Ti Guardi" and "la Tesoro". Ottoman-era dress (women's *tokado* headdress with a jewelled *rozeta*, men's fez and *salvar*) gave way under the Italians to European clothes and a bride in white.

**Kinship and names.** Taranto, working from the cemetery: fifteen surnames account for two-thirds of graves ("Alhadeff, Amato, Benatar, Capelluto, Codron, Cohen, Franco, Hanan, Hasson, Israel, Levy, Menascé, Scemaria, Soriano, Tarica"), and "marriages between cousins and among the same families were especially common"; he found he was related to most of his Hebrew-school classmates. Given names follow the general Sephardic pattern (sources: Jewish naming guides at heymannfamily.com, jewishgen.org): the first son is named for the paternal grandfather, the second for the maternal, daughters likewise for grandmothers, and, unlike Ashkenazim, Sephardim name after **living** relatives, considered an honour and even a lengthener of the honouree's life. Result: the same names cycle every second generation (the Capelouto letters have a Bohora, an Estreya, a Rachel). Rhodes-specific naming rules are not documented in the sources reached; state it as Sephardic custom. A birth ritual for girls survives that is specifically Sephardic: girls were not taken to the synagogue but named at home, in the godmother's (*quitada*) arms, at the *las fadas* ceremony (from *hadas*, the fairies); boys had a sword hung in the room and a two-person night watch (*shemirah*) before the circumcision; babies wore *bulsikas* with garlic or salt, blue beads (*bunjukas mavis*), a gold *dukado*, against *malu oju*. The Seattle letters (UW Stroum Center, Capelouto/Shemarya family, 1922-33, written in *soletreo* cursive) show the same anxiety in ink: a sister in Rhodes begs her married sister in Seattle to "hide your dear Savator of life" from the evil eye, and a mother worries that stress will give the baby "afflicted milk".

**On the "reshiko".** The brief asks for the *reshiko* wedding custom. No source reached (the museum's customs page, jewishrhodes.org, Taranto, the Seattle and Los Angeles Sephardic sites, JVL, Wikipedia) uses the word in any spelling (reshiko, reshico, reshikos). It may be a family term, a mishearing, or a Ladino word from Marc D. Angel's *The Jews of Rhodes* (1978), which was not available online. Do not put it in the script unless Jamie or a Rhodesli descendant confirms what it means; the documented sequence is *vijita* (trousseau viewing), *baño de novia*, Friday procession, *huppah* under a *talet*.

## Tell at stops 13-15 (any of the Juderia stops, and lunch): the calendar as lived practice

The museum's customs page gives the week and year as they were kept, and these are the details to hand Jamie as "myth as lived practice":

- **The week.** Thursday night, women start cooking for Shabbat; late Friday, the family goes to the *banyo turko* (a Turkish hamam, shared with Muslim neighbours) to wash before sunset. Saturday night after Havdalah: stories of *Djoha* (the Ottoman trickster Nasreddin Hoja, shared with every Turk on the island) told for their morals.
- **Purim.** Children carry *platikos di Purim* (plates of sweets) house to house, reciting "Bivas komo l'agua freska, ke tengas la kaza yena komo il guevo" (may you live like fresh water, may your house be as full as an egg). The saying "Purim lanu, Pesah en la mano" launched the spring cleaning, of which women said "De Purim a Pesah, mejor que mi madre no me pariera" (from Purim to Passover, better my mother had never borne me).
- **Passover.** Girls ground salt, pepper, cinnamon, cloves, matzah and nuts in the *mortero*. At the seder the Ladino "Todo el ken tiene ambre venga i koma" (all who are hungry, come and eat) was sung to the open door; the Song of Songs was chanted first in Hebrew then in Ladino; the last day was a picnic at Rodini Park, south of the town, with banjos.
- **Rosh Hashana** with sugar on the table for the *yehi ratsones*; **Yom Kippur** with handkerchiefs dipped in rose water before Neilah, so that "the entire synagogue was redolent with this sweet fragrance"; **Sukkot** (the walker's own week) with huts of palm and myrtle hung with fruit and paper chains, and the mother's *biscochos*; **Hanuka** at picnics called *el dia di la Miranda*, with *burmuelos* (fried dough in honey) and tin *hanukiás* burning olive oil with pine-needle wicks, most of them made by the tinsmith Nissim Benveniste around 1900.
- **Death.** The funeral went from the house out through the Koskinou / St John's Gate (stop 12) to the old cemetery near *La Fasana*. A caller cried "Pasa la misva!" (the commandment is passing); people in the street turned away and those at home shut the windows and stepped back three feet. Only men followed; women and Kohanim (priests, who must avoid the dead) stayed. Then seven days of *sentar en siete*, the men cross-legged on the floor round a white cloth, professional *endechadoras* singing laments "at the top of their voices". Prayer-books were kept at the *Kalijika de la Havra*, 4 Vizantiou Street; jguideeurope notes a Hebrew plaque there "that blesses all those who pass beneath its arch", which it dates "Nisan 5637 (1837)"; note that 5637 is actually 1877 and 1837 would be 5597, so give the date as "nineteenth-century" unless the stone is read on site. Walk under it.
- **Folk medicine and omens** (from Abraham Galante's records, quoted by the museum): "traimi un punado de sal!" (bring me a fistful of salt) is the healer's opening line; rue (*aruda*) gilded with gold leaf as a birth gift; never give fire to a neighbour without keeping an ember; when moving house, take the sweepings and a black knife; a dog urinating on your door foretells a guest or a letter. This is the domestic religion that ran underneath the rabbinic one, and every item has a Greek and Turkish twin.

**Food (stop 15, lunch).** The museum lists *boyos* (spinach-and-cheese pastries), *burmuelos*, *kapama* (a bake of tomato, onion, green pepper, parsley), preserved green tomatoes (*tomat*), *palamida* (bonito), *biscochos*, *masapan*, *bouloucounou* (sesame candy), *dulces* (candied fruit made by women working together in the kitchen, "La kaza estava siempre yena para alegrias"), and Turkish coffee (*kave turko*) in *finjanes* in the courtyard. The Conversation piece notes the community was in the sea-sponge trade and that one survivor's "father spoke Turkish with his Muslim neighbors". Nireas is a fish taverna, so *palamida* (bonito) is the honest link.

## Tell at stops 4, 5 and 16 (Hippocrates Square, Marine Gate, Sokratous): work, the market, and the neighbours

- The bazaar street, Sokratous, was in Ladino *Arriva del Kadi* ("up by the judge's", the kadi's court being there); it was the Jews' business district, outside the residential quarter but ten minutes from it (Rhodes Jewish Museum). The Jewish Encyclopedia c. 1905: "The commerce of the island is controlled by the Jews, among whom there are also many boatmen and porters", i.e. the community spanned the whole economy from Alhadeff's bank to the men rowing lighters at the Marine Gate. JVL's Ottoman-period list: "merchants, manufacturers of arms, moneychangers, tax farmers, and physicians; the middle class were craftsmen: carpenters, tanners, goldsmiths, and bookbinders", plus wine, silk and weaving. Cityofrhodes.com's summary of Ottoman life: a marketplace "shared by all, where merchants sold goods in Greek, Turkish, and Ladino", water-carriers, masons and tailors "of different faiths worked side by side".
- Residence pattern (research/11 has it): after 1522 Greeks were pushed to the *marasia* outside the walls; Jews and Turks lived inside. So for four centuries the Jews' next-door neighbours were Muslims, and the Greeks were the people you met at market. The memoir of the merchant Vittorio Alhadeff, quoted by K. la revue: there were "sincere and solid friendships" between Turks and Jews and Turks and Greeks, "though families did not mingle". The Conversation: relations with Christians were "decidedly thornier".
- The 1840 blood libel is the proof (Wikipedia, "Rhodes blood libel"; Jewish Encyclopedia). A Greek Orthodox boy vanished on 17 February 1840; the Greek clergy and the European consuls (British, French, Austrian, Swedish and Greek) pressed the Ottoman governor Yusuf Pasha, who blockaded the Jewish quarter for twelve days on the eve of Purim; "eight Jews were arrested, including the chief rabbi" Jacob (Yaakov) Israel (Wikipedia; the fuller name "Michael Jacob Israel" appears in older Jewish-encyclopedia usage but is not in the Wikipedia article). Eliakim Stamboli was given 500 blows of the bastinado and had red-hot wires run through his nose; the rabbi was tortured by suspension. An Austrian official told him: "What do you complain about? So you are not dead yet." Verdict of acquittal 21 July 1840; Yusuf Pasha dismissed; and after Montefiore's audience with Sultan Abdülmecid on 28 October the firman of 7 November 1840 declared blood accusations "pure calumny" and Jews entitled to "the same privileges as are granted to the numerous other nations." The Sultan was the Jews' protector against their Greek neighbours and the Western consuls; that is the Ottoman bargain in miniature, and it explains why the community's later emigrants still sang Turkish songs in Seattle.
- Under the Italians the quarter got newspapers (*El Boletin*, 1934-36, in Judeo-Spanish, edited by Hizkia Franco; *Selam*, 1926-36, "for Turkish and Jewish readers", director Mehmet Kadri, editor Franco: a joint Turkish-Jewish paper), three Jewish football teams (Maccabi, G.E.R. Gioventù Ebraica Rodi, and Città Murata), a ballroom (*La Ronda*, 1930s, at the end of Mandraki, foxtrot and waltz to a Victrola), and membership of the Circolo d'Italia until 1939, "when Jews were excluded" (museum customs page).

## Tell at stops 9 and 12, and the Kallithea B-side: Italian citizenship, the cemetery, and 1938

- Citizenship is the hinge. Under the 1923 Lausanne settlement "local Jewish Rhodians received Italian citizenship including those who migrated from nearby Turkish Asia Minor" (Kerem, Times of Israel blog); this was the *piccola cittadinanza* (Aegean "small citizenship"), which "did not include military service" (Quest journal review). The 1938 racial laws (RDL 1728 of 17 November 1938) revoked Italian citizenship granted to foreign Jews after 1 January 1919, and the decree of 7 September 1938 (RDL 1381) ordered foreign Jews to leave "the territory of the Kingdom, of Libya and of the Aegean Possessions" within six months, i.e. by 12 March 1939 (Italian Wikipedia, *Leggi razziali fasciste*); Kerem puts "those 2,000 Asia Minor Jewish immigrants" in that category and says they "had a half-a-year to leave the island". That is the mechanism behind research/11's "more than 2,000 left": not a choice, an expulsion by paperwork. Where they went: Tangier's international zone, Elisabethville, Argentina, Belgium (Conversation; Kerem). Sami Modiano, expelled from school at eight: "That day I lost my innocence."
- At the Palace (stop 9): research/11 has De Vecchi's requisition of tombstones. jewishrhodes.org: "The Italians removed about 100 of the most impressive to decorate the governor's palace"; graves whose families could not pay to move them were "destroyed by the Italians and are buried in two cenotaphs"; about 18 rabbis' graves were moved at the community's cost. The cemetery today is on Rodou-Kallitheas Avenue between the Catholic and Muslim cemeteries (the road to the Kallithea Springs B-side), open Monday-Friday 08:00-13:00, oldest stones mid-17th century, a 1949 Holocaust memorial listing the deported families. The Rhodes Jewish Historical Foundation (Los Angeles) worked with the community "between 1997 and 2008" on restoring "the ancient tombstones of the Jewish cemetery" (museum FAQ), and jewishrhodes.org notes recent excavations by American-Jewish descendants without dating them; do not give a start year for the digging.
- At the Koskinou / St John's Gate (stop 12): this is the gate the funerals used, "outside la puerta de la ciudad (Koskinou Gate, Gate of Saint John) near the La Fasana area" (museum), before the 1938 removal.

## Tell at stops 10, 14 and 1 (D'Amboise Gate, the square, the harbour): July 1944

Research/11 has the timeline and Ülkümen. Add what the walker can place:

- **Where they were held.** The "Air Force Command Center" to which men over 16 were summoned on 18 July, and women and children on 19 July, was "outside the Old City near the Gate of Amboise" (Rhodes Jewish Museum, Holocaust page); they were kept there three days. So at stop 10, the dragon's-head gate, Jamie is a few hundred metres from the building where 1,673 people spent 20-22 July 1944.
- **The consul.** On 19 July Ülkümen, aged 30 (born 14 January 1914 per Wikipedia; JVL and Kerem say "29-year-old"), told General Kleemann that "under Turkish law all citizens were equal" and that Turkey "did not distinguish between citizens who were Jewish, Christian or Muslim"; Kleemann had said Jews were Jews "regardless of nationality" (Wikipedia). Numbers vary by source: 42 (museum, Wikipedia's main figure), 46 (JVL, Kerem), c. 50 (Wikipedia, counting expired papers and spouses). His consulate was bombed, his wife Mihrinisa gravely hurt; Wikipedia dates the bombing 18 February 1944 (before the deportation) and notes the attribution (German or Allied aircraft) is disputed; she died of complications after their son Mehmet's birth on 21 July 1945. Yad Vashem named him Righteous Among the Nations on 13 December 1989 (Wikipedia; the museum says 1990); he died in Istanbul on 7 June 2003.
- **The numbers, honestly.** 1,673 marched to the harbour on 23 July; "about a hundred" from Kos joined at Leros (museum) or 85 (Conversation); the museum's later tally is 22 dead on the eight-day crossing (research/11's "seven" is the older figure), 1,145 killed at Auschwitz, 437 dead in labour camps, "approximately 151 survivors"; the museum's memorial inscription counts "1604 Jewish Martyrs of Rhodes and Cos". Kerem: "100 Rhodian women transferred from Auschwitz to Bergen Belsen comprised most of the Rhodian Holocaust survivors. Only several dozen males survived." Piraeus 1 August, Haidari, train 3 August, Auschwitz 16 August: "the longest journey of any Jewish community sent to Auschwitz" (Conversation).
- **The scroll and the mufti (stop 19 or 21, looking toward Murat Reis).** Days before the deportation a Torah scroll, believed written in Iberia in the 15th century and carried by the exiles, was smuggled out of Kahal Shalom to the Mufti of Rhodes, Sheikh Suleyman Kasiloglou, who "is said to have hidden the Torah under the pulpit of a local mosque" (Times of Israel; the article does not name the mosque, so the Murad Reis identification is unsourced and should not be spoken), and the scroll "subsequently survived the war"; it is now in the National Library of Israel (Times of Israel; the NLI blog could not be reached to confirm how it got there). Four centuries of living next door to Muslims produced, at the end, one act of custody.

## Tell at stops 19 and 21 (Mandraki): the diaspora, and courtship by the water

- Mandraki was the courtship ground: "young men and women walking from one end of the square to the other, backwards and forwards, crossing and crisscrossing each other exchanging silent looks" (museum). It was also where the young men left from. The museum's emigration page: "At first, most were young men who traveled with the intention of making some money and then return to pursue their family life on Rhodes." Joseph Capeloto left in 1907 aged 14, via Ellis Island to New York and then Seattle, earned $8 a week and sent enough home to bring the family in 1909.
- **Africa.** JVL: "young poorly educated teenage boys from Rhodes began migrating to Africa" at the end of the 19th century and "founded synagogues in Elizabethville and Salisbury". Wikipedia names the brothers Musa and Salmon Benatar as the pioneers from 1895; Ladjente: first Sephardim in Mashonaland in 1896, larger numbers from 1905, Congo from about 1911, the Hebrew Sephardic Society of Rhodesia in 1931 and a Salisbury synagogue in 1932 (Zimbabwe Jewish Community); the Katanga picnic of 1933 is in the museum's photographs. The academic study of this migration is Dario Miccoli, "Tierra prometida: Jews from Rhodes in the Belgian Congo and Southern Rhodesia, 1910s-1960s", *Jewish Historical Studies* 55 (2024), DOI 10.14324/111.444.jhs.2024v55.08 (not "Cosseto/Sertoli"); the figure "almost 2,000 Jews from Rhodes emigrated or were born in the Belgian Congo and Southern Rhodesia" is attributed to its abstract but the article was unreachable during fact-checking, so quote it as "roughly two thousand, by one recent estimate". After Congolese independence in 1960 they scattered to Rhodesia, Brussels and Cape Town. Bella Angel Restis, president of the Rhodes community from 2000, was born in the Belgian Congo (JTA).
- **America.** Seattle: Rhodes Jews, in Seattle's Sephardic shorthand "Rhodeslis", arrived from 1904 ("In 1904, Nessim Alhadeff made the journey from Rhodes to Seattle. Within three years, all of his brothers and many others followed him"); in 1909 they formed the benevolent society *Koupa Ozer Dalim Anshe Rhodes*, "the fund for aid to Rhodes's poor", the same *Ozer Dalim* as the Rhodes charity, later renamed *Koupa Ezra Bessaroth of Rhodes*, with articles of incorporation filed on 19 June 1914 (congregation's own history page); Ezra Bessaroth still keeps "the liturgy and customs of the Mediterranean Island of Rhodes" in Seward Park (Wikipedia). Los Angeles: the Avat Shalom society of 1912 united Ottoman Sephardim briefly and "soon dissolved"; "in 1917 the Peace and Progress Society was formed with members mostly from the Island of Rhodes"; in 1935 it was renamed the Sephardic Hebrew Center, later Sephardic Beth Shalom, and in 1993 merged into Sephardic Temple Tifereth Israel (Wikipedia; sephardictemple.org). The claims that Rhodeslis were "half" of Avat Shalom's founders and that the society paid sick benefits, buried by Rhodes rite and sent relief money home could not be sourced; use the Ladino name *Paz i Progreso* only as a gloss. New York's Brotherhood League of Rhodes was organised in 1910; there were Rhodesli colonies in Atlanta, Montgomery and Portland by 1912-29, and family photographs from Buenos Aires (1922), Rio (1923), Cairo (1935), Montevideo. Stella Levi, Auschwitz survivor, aged 91 in 2015: "What is it about Rhodes that is so attractive that we were driven to create the same community wherever we went, to Congo, in Rhodesia, in Seattle and California? ... I think it was because we were all one family."

## Tell at stops 13, 14 and 22: the afterlife

- After the war "twenty Jewish families" tried to settle; c. 50 Jews in 1947, 32 in 1969 (JVL). "An attempt in 1956 to restart the community brought 25 Jewish families from mainland Greece" (JTA), "from cities such as Volos and Thessalonika" answering an appeal by the Central Board (K. la revue, which says "late 1950s" and "a handful of families"); by 2015 "only five of those families remain on the island" (JTA). Wikipedia gives about 40 Jews in 2011; nobody with pre-war Rhodes roots lives on the island (Conversation). Current officers: Claudia Restis president, Yomtov Robissa vice-president, Daniel Benardout secretary (jewishrhodes.org). A Chabad house opened nearby in 2017 (K. la revue).
- The 2002 memorial (research/11) is a "black granite column", "dedicated on June 23, 2002", six-sided with Greek, Hebrew, English, French, Italian and Ladino, and counts "1604 Jewish Martyrs of Rhodes and Cos" (museum; note jewishrhodes.org's La Juderia page gives the unveiling as 23 July 2002, the deportation anniversary, so say "summer 2002" if precision matters); the museum "was established in October 1997" by Aron Hasson, "a third generation Rhodian from Los Angeles", in the former women's prayer rooms, gained a permanent panel exhibition in June 2002, reopened after expansion in 2006 (jewishrhodes.org; the museum FAQ frames the work as 1997-2008) and was fully renovated in 2025. Resti at the reopening: "This museum exists so that we remember who they were, not only how they were lost. It restores presence where there was absence."
- The return: "Dozens of Rhodeslis families visit each year for cultural events and memorial services" (JTA); the annual commemoration is pegged to 23 July; in 2015 more than 500 came to a Ladino concert in the Palace courtyard; Giuseppe Giannotti, 61, son of a survivor: "Last year I did my bar mitzvah here with all these people... I was 60 years old and everyone was crying." For the 80th anniversary, 16-22 July 2024, "over 300 people" took part, descendants from abroad, Greek Jewish organisations and local officials; six candles were lit at Kahal Shalom by Rabbi Gabriel Negrin of Athens, Auschwitz survivor Sami Modiano, community president Claudia Resti, David Saltiel of the Central Board (KISE), Rabbi Owen, and, "on behalf of the younger generation of Rhodeslis, Idan and Yaniv Pressman" (jewishrhodes.org). The Rhodes/Cos Memorial Committee of Seattle's "International Rhodes/Cos Holocaust Memorial" on 26 July 2026 is a Zoom event (registration link on jewishrhodes.org). ## Continuities and contrasts

- **An ancient Rhodian** would recognise the phratry-like web of cousin marriage and the naming of grandsons for grandfathers (Greek practice too: the first son took the paternal grandfather's name); the *cortijo* as the old Greek courtyard house; the pebble mosaic floor; the amulet against the evil eye (*baskania* in Greek, *malu oju* in Ladino, *nazar* in Turkish; the same blue bead); the seder's open-door invitation as a *xenia* rule; and a city whose commerce was run by a resident foreign-language community with its own law, exactly as Rhodes' own metics had been. He would not recognise a single God, a Sabbath, or a community that buried its own dead outside the walls without a family tomb.
- **A Hospitaller** would recognise a corporation with its own statutes, court, hospital and charity chest (the Order's Hospital at stop 6 and the Juderia's Bikur Holim do the same job), the French-language school as a civilising mission, and the wall he named for the Jews. He would recognise the Italian governors' habit of deciding who may hold what citizenship. He would not recognise a Muslim sultan as the Jews' protector, nor the conversion decree as anything but policy.
- **An Ottoman-era householder**, Turkish or Jewish, would recognise almost everything: the hamam on Friday, the fez and *entari*, Djoha's stories, Turkish coffee in *finjanes*, the shared market in three languages, the *millet* bargain (pay the tax, keep your court), the funeral cry in the street. Jewish and Turkish householders in 1900 lived more like each other than either lived like the Greeks outside the walls; the mufti's pulpit in 1944 is the last proof.
- **A resident today** recognises the food (a *boyo* is a cousin of the Greek *tyropita*, a claim of resemblance, not documented descent), the cemetery road, the July return, and the seahorse fountain, but lives in a town where the quarter's houses are mostly holiday lets and the synagogue is a museum. What has gone is the thing Jamie is asking about: a household economy in which a fifteen-year-old's chest of embroidery, a grandfather's name, a charity fund with no donor list, and a rabbi's court were all one machine for staying a people. The machine now runs in Seattle and Cape Town, and comes home once a year.

# Reading the stone: coats of arms, the owl, the rose, the deer and the marks on the walls as a registry of who ruled

**Frame for the scriptwriter.** Every regime that has held Rhodes town has signed it. The Hellenistic republic stamped a facing sun-god and a rose on its coins and re-cut the names on its bronze statues; the Knights carved a shield on every gate, tower, inn and hospital they paid for, so that the walls read today as a ledger of grand masters, dated bastion by bastion; the Ottomans, by the best witnesses, left those shields where they were and added their own plaques and fountains; the Italians restored, re-carved and in a few cases invented Hospitaller stone while stamping the new town with the fasces; and the Greek state took the fasces down in one afternoon in March nineteen forty-eight and left everything else standing. For Jamie the point is not the heraldry as decoration but as administration: a stone shield in Rhodes is a receipt, a date-stamp and a claim of jurisdiction rolled into one, and a walker who can read three shapes (the plain cross of the Order, a grand master's family arms, a king's or pope's arms) can date most of what she is looking at to within a decade without a guidebook. Jamie's stops in this file follow her numbering: four is Hippocrates Square, five the Marine Gate, seven the Street of the Knights, nine the Palace, ten the d'Amboise Gate, eleven the walls walk, twelve the Koskinou (St John) Gate, eighteen Liberty Gate, nineteen the deer columns, twenty Fort St Nicholas, twenty-two the Naillac stump and St Paul's Gate, plus the dry moat B-side.

## 1. The Order's heraldic rule as an administrative system

**The practice, and what it lets a walker do.** I found no statute text ordering that the arms of the reigning grand master be carved on every work, and the file should not claim one. What is documented is the practice itself, on the stones and in the travellers' notes. William Turner, walking the town in eighteen fifteen, wrote that "on the bas-reliefs in the city which contained the arms of the Knights were generally dates, all of which that I saw were from 1492 to 1519" (quoted at romeartlover, Rodi3). Henry Fanshawe Tozer, who landed in April eighteen eighty-six, describes "the escutcheons over the dwellings or the gateways, denoting either the possessor or the person by whom they were erected, but all of them containing the cross of the Order; all these bear witness to the religious character of the institution, and to the principle according to which the independent position of each people was recognised" (Tozer, The Islands of the Aegean, page two hundred and three, verified in the archive.org text; romeartlover Rodi1 quotes the same sentence and names Tozer). That sentence is the whole system: one shield says who paid or who lived there, the other says under whose sovereignty. Because grand masters reigned for known spans, the shield dates the wall. The catalogue that lets a scriptwriter do this is François Velde's heraldica.org page on Rhodes, which photographs and identifies the surviving escutcheons.

**The catalogue, work by work** (heraldica.org unless noted; the research files confirm the walk's own stops):

- **Roger des Pins** (grand master thirteen fifty-five to thirteen sixty-five): arms on the facade of the *first* hospital, built about thirteen sixty, the building on the Street of the Knights that is now the Decorative Arts collection beside Argyrokastrou Square (stop 7); heraldica photographs Fabrizio del Carretto's arms on the same facade, so the building was re-signed a century and a half later.
- **Juan Fernández de Heredia** (thirteen seventy-six to thirteen ninety-six): arms over the Gate of the Arsenal on the harbour side (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia; kastra.eu).
- **Antoni de Fluvià** (fourteen twenty-one to fourteen thirty-seven): arms carried by two angels on the facade of the Great Hospital, built fourteen forty to fourteen eighty-nine with money he left in his will, and again at the St Anthony Gate. Romeartlover reads the Hospital relief as Lastic's; heraldica and research/06 say Fluvian. Use Fluvian, the man who paid, and say the building was begun under Lastic (stop 6, mentioned from stop 5 and 7).
- **Pope Pius II**: arms on St Peter's Tower, built by Zacosta fourteen sixty-one to sixty-four (romeartlover, Rodi2). **Pope Sixtus IV**: arms with a relief of St Paul over St Paul's Gate (romeartlover, Rodi1; stop 22). Popes appear because the Order was a papal foundation and the popes were paying for the walls after fourteen eighty.
- **Pierre d'Aubusson** (fourteen seventy-six to fifteen oh three): arms at the St Athanasios Gate dated fourteen eighty-six; on the wall facing the port, once plain and once with a cardinal's hat, because Innocent VIII made him a cardinal in fourteen eighty-nine "which explains why the Grand Master's coats of arms after 1489 have the insignia of a cardinal" (romeartlover, Rodi4); on St John's Gate (stop 12); on the Inn of France with the cardinal's hat (stop 7). He also set the arms of the King of France on the Windmills Tower on the eastern mole, which is why it is called the Tower of France (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia; research/16). At St Catherine's Gate "the coats of arms below the relief showed the heraldic symbols of the King of France (centre), in addition to those of the Order (left) and of the Grand Master (right)" (romeartlover, Rodi1). That three-shield arrangement, king in the middle, Order on the dexter, master on the sinister, is the standard Rhodes gate formula and the one Jamie should learn.
- **Emery d'Amboise** (fifteen oh three to fifteen twelve): the outer face of the d'Amboise Gate carries an angel relief with the arms of the Order and of d'Amboise and the inscription DAMBOYSE MDXII, that is fifteen twelve, the year he died (heraldica.org; it.wikipedia Fortificazioni di Rodi; verify-deltas.md item confirmed). The same man signed the Castellania in Hippocrates Square three times (stop 4): the Renaissance lintel at the top of the outside stair, where an angel holds "the arms of the Order in his left hand and those of Grand Master d'Amboise in his right" (lecrac, in jamie-05; the lintel itself carries no date); a separate Gothic frame with "the arms of Grand Master d'Amboise and the date 1507" (lecrac, the only source read for the date); and a portal photographed by heraldica where d'Amboise's shield sits in the centre flanked by the arms of Villiers de l'Isle-Adam as bailiff and an unidentified shield. His arms also appear on a great culverin of the Order, French work of fifteen hundred to fifteen ten (Wikipedia, Emery d'Amboise, photograph caption), so the stamp went on the guns as well as the gates.
- **Fabrizio del Carretto** (fifteen thirteen to fifteen twenty-one): shield at the centre of the Inn of Italy, fifteen nineteen (research/06), and the great round bastion at the south-east corner that bears his name.
- **Philippe Villiers de l'Isle-Adam**: as bailiff, not yet master, his arms on the Inn of France with "POUR L'ORATOIRE 1511" and the companion plaques "Pour la Maison" and "Pour Philerme" (heraldica.org; research/06); as the last grand master, his arms on the pentagonal Bastion of St George (Fortifications of Rhodes, Wikipedia; kastra.eu; stop 11 and the moat B-side).
- **Guy de Blanchefort**, grand master for a year in fifteen twelve to thirteen who died at sea on his way out and never reached Rhodes as master (Wikipedia), on the Inn of Auvergne with the Gothic inscription "D'Auvargne, G. Prior, F. Guy de Blanchefort, 1507", carved while he was still prior (research/06).

**The Order's cross: plain, not eight-pointed.** On the Rhodes stones the Order's shield is a plain cross, the "gules a cross argent" that Pope Alexander IV's bull of twelve fifty-nine ordered worn on the mantle. The forked cross, "at first a cross fourchée or cross ancrée", "developed into the fully articulated Maltese cross only around or after 1500", and a fourteen eighty-three image shows the Order using both the plain cross and a quartered version with a cross ancrée (Wikipedia, Flag and coat of arms of the Sovereign Military Order of Malta). So if Jamie sees an eight-pointed cross carved crisply on a Rhodes wall, the carving is Italian or later; the medieval Order signed with a straight-armed cross. On the surviving stones the master's family arms and the Order's cross appear either as two shields side by side or with the cross quartered into the master's own shield; the later Maltese rule that every grand master quarter the Order's arms with his own is documented for Malta and should not be asserted as a Rhodes statute [unverified for Rhodes].

**Which inn shows which langue.** Inn of France: the arms and cardinal's hat of d'Aubusson, the fourteen ninety-two inscription to Emery d'Amboise as Grand Prior of France, three l'Isle-Adam plaques, and the fleur-de-lis of the French crown (research/06; romeartlover Rodi3). Inn of Spain: Castile, Aragon, Granada, Navarre and Portugal. Inn of Italy: Carretto. Inn of Auvergne: Blanchefort. Inn of Provence: four shields set in a cross above the door, identities unverified (research/06 open question). Inn of England: the arms of England on a front rebuilt in nineteen nineteen to the old plans, so a twentieth-century carving of a medieval claim (research/06). The tiny Holy Trinity chapel on the street bears the papal arms framed by the royal arms of England, and Tozer in eighteen eighty-six saw exactly that: "a little chapel... called the English chapel, and has the arms of England sculptured on its walls. It is now a mosque" (Tozer, Islands of the Aegean, page two hundred and nine, verified in the archive.org text; jamie-06 gives page two hundred and six, which is wrong). The chaplain's house lintel next door carries ten shields (England, Villeneuve, the Order, Gozon, Corneillan, Bérenger, de Pins) and is the closest thing on the street to a family tree in stone (research/06).

**Burgundy on the fort (stop 20).** Whitworth Porter's history records that Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, sent "12,000 gold crowns for the strengthening of the defences of the island", that Zacosta used the gift to build the tower of St Nicholas at the end of the mole, that the Duke's arms were placed "over the principal façade" in gratitude, and that Zacosta kept his old castellany of Emposta and "devote[d] its revenues entirely to the completion of the new fort", which "at the time gave great dissatisfaction" (Porter, A History of the Knights of Malta, ch. 9, Wikisource). Charles Newton in the eighteen fifties found inside "many brass guns of the time of the knights, some of which bear the date 1482... others 1507, with the arms of France and England" (quoted by Porter). Whether the Burgundian shield is still legible on the fort today I could not verify [unverified]; the fort is often closed (research/10). Research/16 dates the tower fourteen sixty-four to sixty-seven and its conversion to a sea-fort to after fourteen eighty.

**Who was entitled to a shield.** A shield on a Rhodes wall belongs to an institution or a sovereign: the Order, a grand master, a king, a pope, a langue. No serjeant-at-arms and almost no ordinary knight is on the walls. That matches the Order's admission rule: the knightly class was "confined... to sons born in lawful wedlock of knights or members of knightly families", while for serving brothers "it sufficed that they should not be serfs" (1911 Britannica, in jamie-05). The famous "sixteen quarters" of nobility were not a Rhodes-era requirement; even around eighteen hundred four quarters sufficed for a Frenchman (verify-deltas.md, correction to research/06). A proof of nobility was a documentary exercise, a bundle of parish and notarial papers showing that a candidate's grandparents bore arms; the shield he then carried on his mantle was the summary of that file. On the walls the only individual whose file mattered was the master's, and his shield sits beside the Order's as a reminder that he ruled for life but as an elected officer of a corporation.

## 2. What each regime did with the last one's symbols

**Ottomans: toleration, not plaster.** Two of Jamie's files disagree. jamie-03 says the Knights "carved their coats of arms into every gate and inn, and the Ottomans plastered over them"; jamie-05 quotes Ettore Rossi that the Turks "did not touch the fortress, the auberges of the langues, the buildings of the castellany, or the magnificent hospital... they left almost intact the coats of arms, the gateways, and the inscriptions" (Rossi in Setton, A History of the Crusades III, confirmed in jamie-05's fact-check; Rossi's own monograph is Assedio e conquista di Rodi nel 1522 secondo le relazioni edite e inedite dei Turchi, Rome, nineteen twenty-seven, cited in Setton's bibliography). The witnesses side with Rossi. Turner in eighteen fifteen read the dates on the shields; Tozer in eighteen eighty-six saw the arms of England on a chapel that was then a mosque and the escutcheons still on the dwellings and gateways; English Wikipedia's summary of the Ottoman town is that "most of the architectural features of the existing buildings were preserved" (Rhodes (city)). The Castellania kept its angel and its arms while its upper floor was a mosque and its ground floor a fish market (jamie-05). **Verdict: drop the plastering claim from jamie-03 as a blanket statement.** What the Ottomans did was add: wooden balconies on the inns, which the Italians tore off in nineteen sixteen (research/06), Arabic inscription plaques and fountains, and the Suleymaniye and its dependencies on the site of the Apostles' church. Individual shields may have been whitewashed with the wall around them; there is no source for a policy of defacement [unverified either way].

**Italians: restoration, re-carving and a little invention.** The Italian archaeological mission under Amedeo Maiuri ran from nineteen sixteen to nineteen twenty-four; Giuseppe Gerola surveyed the Dodecanese monuments from nineteen twelve (jamie-08). The Street of the Knights was stripped of its balconies and restored in nineteen thirteen to sixteen; the Inn of Italy in nineteen fifteen, the Inn of England rebuilt in nineteen nineteen with its arms, the chaplain's house in nineteen eighteen with a new Virgin and Child in the niche (research/06). English Wikipedia is blunt that the Italians "preserved what was left from the Knights' period, and destroyed all Ottoman buildings" in the plan area (Rhodes (city)). The Palace, blown up in eighteen fifty-six, was rebuilt in nineteen thirty-seven to forty by Vittorio Mesturino "based on medieval castles in Northern Italy, rather than on the Byzantine/Gothic features of the original palace" (romeartlover Rodi3), with "inherent lack of historical accuracy and unnecessary alterations to the authentic remains" and, in one scholar's phrase, "horrendous fascist taste" (Palace of the Grand Master, Wikipedia). I found no published list of specific arms Mesturino re-cut or invented; the honest rule for the script is that any crisp escutcheon inside the Palace courtyard or on its upper storeys should be assumed Italian work unless the guide labels it otherwise [unverified in detail]. Mussolini's name is on a large plaque near the Palace entrance (Wikipedia, single-sourced in jamie-08).

**The fasces of nineteen forty-eight.** The fasces were not on the Palace but on the new town: the Casa Littoria on Mandraki, built nineteen thirty-seven to thirty-nine by Bernabiti as the fascist club, with Mussolini's gift statues of Julius Caesar and Octavian in the plaza. On seven March nineteen forty-eight the Greek flag was raised there with the King present and "the symbols of fascism and the statues extracted" (imrtreasures.gr, confirmed in jamie-08). The building is the Town Hall. Jamie can see the blank where a symbol was on the Mandraki facades (stop 18 to 21) [which facades exactly: unverified]. Today the Greek and European Union flags fly on the Palace [unverified observation; treat as what Jamie sees, not as a documented fact], while the Order's flag does not.

## 3. The town's non-heraldic emblems

**Helios and the rose.** From the synoecism of four oh eight onward the federal coins carry the facing head of Helios on one side and the rose (rhodon, for the nymph Rhodos) on the other; the exact start date of the type was unverifiable (jamie-03). The marble Helios head in the Archaeological Museum is the object Jamie can point at (stop 6, mentioned at 5 and 7). Whether the rose survives formally as a municipal emblem I could not establish; the modern municipality's emblem is displayed on Greek Wikipedia's page for the Municipality of Rhodes without a text description, and the municipal website gives no history of it [unverified]. Say "the rose is the island's name and its oldest badge" and no more.

**The deer.** The two bronze deer on the columns at the Mandraki mouth (Elafos and Elafina, OSM nodes in verify-deltas-round2) are the modern civic emblem in practice. Their date is disputed in the files: jamie-03 says nineteen thirty-nine and Italian; publicartaroundtheworld says "unveiled" in nineteen forty-seven with the sculptor "unknown"; jamie-06's fact-check lists the date and sculptor as unverified. Script it as "put up in the last years of the Italians or the first of the Greeks", not with a year. "Elafoussa, the deer island" is not in any ancient author; Pliny's old names for the island mean snake island (jamie-06). The legal basis of the deer as municipal emblem was not found [unverified]; the Kallikratis municipality dates from the Government Gazette of seven June twenty ten (el.wikipedia, Δήμος Ρόδου).

**The owl on the fountain, Hippocrates Square (stop 4).** Status: **nobody has explained it, and no fetched source dates the fountain.** Wikidata Q134543663 ("Castellania fountain, Rhodes") gives coordinates and no inception or creator; the Commons category holds nineteen photographs from nineteen seventy-seven to August twenty twenty-five and one file titled "Castellania-Brunnen · Eule" (owl); jamie-14's fact-check confirms the owls come from Commons and visitors, not from a dated record. The Metropolis's fountains page says only that under the Ottomans "numerous public fountains are created" and that "during the Italian occupation, fountains are built in public spaces for decorative purposes, in a variety of style" (imrtreasures.gr). Candidate readings, in order of my confidence: (a) an Italian decorative fountain of the nineteen twenties or thirties, when the square was remodelled as the tourist heart of the Old Town and Italian classicising taste favoured the owl of Athena as a badge of learning and of Greece itself, which would suit a fountain beside a building the Italians read as a medieval court; (b) an Ottoman fountain rebuilt by the Italians with a new crown; (c) a post-war Greek municipal fountain. Reading (a) is the likeliest but unproven. For the script: "a stone owl that nobody has written down; ask three shopkeepers and you will get three dates."

## 4. Marks that are not heraldry

**Mason's marks.** Hospitaller masonry elsewhere carries cut marks, but I found no published survey of mason's marks on the Rhodes walls this session [unverified]. Do not assert them; invite Jamie to look for small incised letters on the ashlar of the d'Amboise Gate passage.

**Ship graffiti.** The only scholarly ship-graffiti study surfaced by the search was Joseph Muscat's "Ship Graffiti: a Comparative Study" (Journal of Mediterranean Studies nine, nineteen ninety-nine), cited in Wikipedia's article on the Castellania of Valletta, not for Rhodes. **No study of incised ship graffiti on the Rhodes fortifications or Palace was found; drop the claim.**

**Stone cannonballs.** Research/06 puts stone balls and a stone lion in the Hospital courtyard; research/10 puts "26-inch" stone balls in the moat "lying where they fell" in fourteen eighty and fifteen twenty-two, a guidebook phrase. The balls were Ottoman siege shot and the Order's own; their present tidy stacks are curatorial arrangement of the Italian and Greek periods [who stacked them: unverified]. They are the clearest object on the walk that has changed meaning without moving far: ammunition, then rubble, then ornament, now a bench for a tired walker.

**The current layer.** Stickers, spray tags and shop signs are governed by the Medieval City Appearance Regulation, whose chapters run "signs, display windows, shutters, colour schemes, shading, external lighting..." (jamie-18). A sticker on a Hospitaller gate is illegal and ephemeral; a shield on it was legal and permanent; both say "I was here and I had the right".

## Five-item field guide for any gate

1. **Look above the arch, then at the flanking towers.** Gate shields sit over the outer arch; tower shields sit high on the seaward or landward face. On a gate the standard set is three: a king or pope in the middle, the Order on the left as you face it, the grand master on the right (St Catherine's Gate formula, romeartlover Rodi1).
2. **Find the plain cross.** A shield bearing only a straight-armed cross is the Order, the sovereign. If the cross has eight points or forked ends, suspect a carving made after fifteen hundred or by the Italians.
3. **Find the family shield beside it.** Fleur-de-lis with a cardinal's hat above: d'Aubusson after fourteen eighty-nine. A chevron-and-pale shield with DAMBOYSE: d'Amboise, fifteen oh three to twelve. Bendy shield: Carretto, fifteen thirteen to twenty-one. Cross with figures at the corners, on the newest bastion: l'Isle-Adam, fifteen twenty-one to twenty-two. [Blazons here are from the heraldica.org photographs and standard armorials; verify the drawing before scripting a shape.]
4. **Read the date.** Roman numerals in the frame (MDXII), or Gothic letters naming a prior and year (the Inn of Auvergne, fifteen oh seven); if there is no date, the master's reign is the date.
5. **Ask what else is on the wall.** An Arabic plaque, a wooden balcony scar, a bronze plaque with an Italian name, a municipal sign, a sticker: each is the same act in a later hand.

## Continuities and contrasts

- **The re-inscribed base.** Dio Chrysostom's Rhodian magistrate who "merely points to whichever statue meets his eyes, then, after removing the former inscription and engraving another name" (Or. 31.10, jamie-03) and the nineteen nineteen mason cutting the arms of England on a rebuilt inn are doing the same civic work: keeping an honour system current by editing stone.
- **The Hospitaller shield and the Ottoman plaque.** Both are notices of jurisdiction. The Knights put a French king in the middle of a gate because his money and men held that stretch; the Ottomans put an inscription over a fountain because the vakf that paid for it was a legal person that outlived its founder (jamie-06, jamie-14).
- **Finta pietra.** The Italians built the Foro Italico in "a red-orange porous stone called finta pietra (fake stone) to replicate the masonry of the Knights" (McGuire, in jamie-08), and Governor Lago posed in a knight's robe: a regime forging the last one's signature instead of erasing it.
- **The modern shop sign.** The Appearance Regulation's approved sign is today's escutcheon: proof that the occupant holds a permit from the authority that owns the wall, which since two thousand twenty-two is, for some three hundred and sixty-five properties, the Ministry of Culture (jamie-09).
- **What every Rhodian would recognise.** A Hellenistic citizen, a Hospitaller serjeant, an Ottoman kadi and a municipal clerk would all agree that the mark on the wall must be authorised, dated and legible to strangers; they would disagree only about whether an owl needs an explanation.

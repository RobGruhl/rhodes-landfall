# The Knights Hospitaller on Rhodes (1309-1522) as a faction system: langues, auberges, piliers, Grand Masters, Collachium vs Burgh, the Hospital (Archaeological Museum), the Palace of the Grand Master and the Church of St John site

## The Order as a faction system

Think of the Hospitallers on Rhodes as a sovereign guild of warrior-monks run by eight national chapters. The Order had reorganised itself into seven *langues* ("tongues") by 1301-1327 — Provence, Auvergne, France, Aragon, Italy, England (with Scotland and Ireland) and Germany (the whole Holy Roman Empire plus Scandinavia, Hungary and Poland); in 1462 Aragon was split and an eighth tongue, Castile-León-Portugal, was born. Each tongue lived in its own *auberge* (inn) in the walled inner quarter, ate together there, and defended its own assigned stretch of city wall. The head of each tongue, the *pilier*, held one of the Order's great offices by hereditary right of his nation: Provence gave the **Grand Commander** (deputy to the Grand Master, treasury); Auvergne the **Marshal** (commander of the army); France the **Hospitaller** (master of the great infirmary); Italy the **Admiral** (the fleet); Aragon the **Drapier / Grand Conservator** (standard-bearer, quartermaster); England the **Turcopolier** (light cavalry and coastal watch); Germany the **Grand Bailiff** (fortifications); Castile the **Grand Chancellor** (seals and documents). The piliers, the Grand Crosses, the bishop and the prior of the conventual church sat together on the Council under an elected Grand Master who served for life.

Three classes made up the Order: **knights** (nobles — "proofs of noble descent being more severely scrutinized than religious dispositions," in the Catholic Encyclopedia's dry phrase; French sources speak of sixteen quarters of nobility), **sergeants-at-arms** (commoners who fought and nursed) and **chaplains**. All took the monastic vows of poverty, chastity and obedience, plus the Order's own vow to serve "our lords the sick." A young knight had to sail three "caravans" (patrols on the Order's galleys) before he could hope for a commandery back home. They wore the black mantle with the white eight-pointed cross; in war, a red surcoat with the white cross.

## Collachium and Burgh

The walled town had two zones. The northern **Collachium** (Kollakio, "the Castello") belonged to the Order alone — palace, conventual church, hospital and the auberges — and "only members of the Order were allowed to remain in the Collachium overnight." An inner wall, now vanished, ran roughly along today's Sokratous street and separated it from the **Burgh** (Bourgo, Chora), where Greeks, Latins and Jews lived, traded and were ruled but not admitted. In D&D terms: the Collachium is the inner keep of a faction stronghold; the Burgh is the town that grew up around it. The tongues also partitioned the outer walls into "posts": from the Palace westward and round, France (St Paul's Gate to the d'Amboise Gate), Germany (to the St George bastion), Auvergne (to the Tower of Spain), Aragon (to the Tower of St Mary), England (to St John's Gate), Provence (to the Tower of Italy) and Italy (round to the harbour Gate of the Mills). England and Aragon swapped posts after 1480 "to reflect the respective weights of these nations."

## Walking the Street of the Knights, uphill

You enter Museum Square from the harbour side. Behind you on the square's south-east corner stands the **Inn of England** (built 1483; the arms of England on its front are on a building rebuilt in 1919 to the old plans — the English tongue was the smallest and, after Henry VIII's dissolution, effectively extinct). On the north side of the square is **Our Lady of the Castle**, the 11th-century Byzantine cathedral that the Knights gothicised into their Latin cathedral (now the Byzantine Museum). The **Hospital** fills the south side, its long flank forming the whole left-hand wall of the street as you climb.

The street is 200 m long, about 6 m wide, cobbled, and rises gently westward. The Italians in 1916 tore down the Ottoman wooden balconies to return it to its 15th-century look — so what you see is a restoration, but the stone, the arches and the coats of arms are largely original. Right-hand (north) side, in order:

1. **Inn of Italy** (1519): the shield in the centre of the facade is that of Grand Master Fabrizio del Carretto, the Ligurian who died in Rhodes in January 1521 and was buried in St John's — the last Grand Master to die here. Restored by the Italians in 1915.
2. **Inn of France** (restored c. 1492 after the 1480 siege): "by far the handsomest inn," with a corner turret, crocodile-shaped gargoyles, the arms and cardinal's hat of Pierre d'Aubusson, a 1492 inscription naming Emery d'Amboise as Grand Prior of France (he became Grand Master 1503-1512), and three plaques of Villiers de l'Isle-Adam's arms marked "Pour la Maison", "Pour l'Oratoire", "Pour Philerme." Chateaubriand admired "the lilies of France, as fresh as if they came from the hand of the sculptor"; Lamartine called the street "the heraldic monument of all the nobility of Europe." Bought in 1911 by the French ambassador Maurice Bompard, it now houses the French consulate.
3. **Chapel of the French tongue (Holy Trinity)** (1365-1374): tiny Gothic chapel bearing the arms of the Pope framed by the royal arms of England — it was apparently the English tongue's chapel first. Under the Ottomans it was the Khan Zade mescid.
4. **Chaplain's house**: Gothic door with a Virgin and Child in a niche (a 20th-century statue) and a lintel with ten shields — England, Villeneuve, the Order, Gozon, Corneillan, Bérenger, de Pins.

Left-hand (south) side: after the Hospital, the **Inn of Spain** (Aragon and Castile), one of the largest, with a 150 m² hall and shields of Castile, Aragon, Granada, Navarre and Portugal; recently restored by the Ministry of Culture. Opposite it, back on the right, the **Inn of Provence** (dated 1518), joined to Spain by the arch that bridges the street; the tongue of Provence was the oldest and gave the Grand Commander. Only its facade, portal and ground floor survived Ottoman use as a bath-house.

The street ends in the **Loggia of St John**, a vaulted arcade opening onto Kleovoulou Square. Right is the Palace; left, where a neoclassical school and the Ottoman clock tower now stand, was the **Church of St John of the Collachium**, begun 1310: burial place of at least six Grand Masters (Gozon, Corneillan, Juilly, Milly, Orsini, Carretto), treasury of the Order's relics — a piece of the True Cross, one of the thirty pieces of silver, and the right hand of John the Baptist. After 1522 it became the town's main mosque. On 6 November 1856 lightning struck gunpowder stored in the cellars beneath its bell-tower; the blast levelled the church, wrecked the upper floors of the Palace and killed at least 200 people. Alfred Biliotti, the British vice-consul and amateur archaeologist, dug for survivors. Only foundation fragments remain; the Italians built a replica church at Mandraki in 1924-29.

## The Palace of the Grand Master

A Byzantine citadel of the 7th century, set on the site of a temple of Helios (some argue the Colossus stood here), became the Order's headquarters after 1309: a rectangular fortress c. 75 × 80 m with a courtyard 50 × 40 m, grain silos under its paving for sieges, and a gate flanked by two horseshoe towers. What you enter today is mostly the reconstruction of 1937-40 by architect Vittorio Mesturino for Governor Cesare Maria De Vecchi, meant as a holiday residence for King Victor Emmanuel III and Mussolini — grand, gloomy, "horrible fascist taste" to its critics. Upstairs, the floors are paved with Late Hellenistic, Roman and Early Christian mosaics ripped out of Kos in the 1930s: the Nine Muses (the most valuable), the Medusa in the colonnaded hall, a nymph on a sea-horse, fish and dolphins, and a full-size cast of the Laocoön, the Rhodian masterpiece now in the Vatican. The courtyard holds eight Hellenistic statues from the theatre of Kos. Downstairs are two permanent exhibitions: **"Ancient Rhodes — 2400 years"** (12 rooms, north wing, opened 1993) and **"Rhodes from the Early Christian period to the Turkish conquest (1522)"** — the medieval one with the Knights' arms, weapons, icons and ceramics. On a Wednesday the ancient exhibition is open 09:00-17:00 but the medieval one is closed.

## The Hospital

Begun in 1440 under Jean de Lastic on the ruins of a Roman building and finished by d'Aubusson in 1489, the Great Hospital was the Order's reason for existing. Above the door, two angels carry the arms of Grand Master Fluvian, who left the money. A monumental stair leads to the first-floor infirmary: a vast hall divided by seven octagonal pillars, with a projecting chapel apse, isolation cells and a cedar ceiling; it could hold about a hundred patients, tended by the knights themselves. Look for the stone lion in the courtyard and the heap of stone cannonballs. Inside: the **Aphrodite of Rhodes** (a 49 cm crouching Venus wringing out her hair, 2nd-1st c. BC, found 1923 in the Governor's garden), the sea-worn **Marine Venus** (fished up in 1929 near the harbour; Durrell watched her hauled from her wartime crypt: "I can still see the faces of my friends as they surrounded the dark trap door out of which she rose so gravely into the sunlight"), the **stele of Krito and Timarista** (c. 420-410 BC, Kamiros, a dead mother embracing her daughter), the head of Helios, the Kamiros kouros and, in the garden, mosaics and Knights' tombstones.

## Legends to flag as legend

Grand Master Dieudonné de Gozon (1346-53), *Extinctor Draconis*, is said to have killed the dragon of Malpasso and hung its head on a gate; the crocodile gargoyles of the Inn of France commemorate the tale — but no writer before the 17th century mentions the head. The story that Suleiman told the defeated l'Isle-Adam he was sorry to force "this brave old man from his home" is later tradition. And whether Chancellor Andrea d'Amaral, beheaded for treason in the 1522 siege, was guilty or simply the loser of the 1521 election is still disputed.

## Open questions
- Exact date of the 1856 explosion: English Wikipedia and viagallica say 6 November 1856; grandmasterpalacerhodes.gr says 3 April 1856; Greek Wikipedia says 1 August 1856; History Hit says 1865. The academic paper by A.-M. Kasdagli on academia.edu could settle it but was not accessible.
- Whether Palace and Museum hours on Wed 30 Sep 2026 are really 08:00-20:00: the official archaeologicalmuseums.gr page (updated 28/07/2026) says 08:00-20:00 through 31 October, but greece-moments.com claims September-October hours shorten every two weeks. Last-admission time not stated officially.
- Byzantine Museum (Our Lady of the Castle) 2026 hours and ticket, and whether a combined ticket still exists after the April 2025 ticket reform (older sources cite a 10 EUR combined ticket).
- Whether the Inn of Spain now opens to the public as a museum/cultural venue (imrtreasures.gr says restored for that purpose) and whether the Inn of France garden/first floor can still be visited mornings.
- Which berth Scarlet Lady will use (Tourist Port berths vs Akandia).
- The hand of John the Baptist: Wikipedia says attested in St John's from 1413, while French sources say it was a gift of Bayezid II to d'Aubusson (1484) - reconcile.
- Capacity of the hospital's great ward: viagallica says '32 beds and could receive a hundred patients'; other sources give different figures.
- Was the Inn of England really destroyed by the 1856 explosion (gpsmycity) given its distance from St John's, or simply ruinous by the 19th century?
- Authenticity of Suleiman's remark pitying l'Isle-Adam, and the exact execution date of Chancellor Andrea d'Amaral (secondary works give 8 November 1522).
- Whose four coats of arms form the cross on the Inn of Provence facade (Britannica Kids caption could not be fetched).
- Reduced-ticket eligibility for a non-EU adult under 65 at the Palace (likely full 20 EUR).

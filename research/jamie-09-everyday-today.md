# Topic 9. How people organise their lives in Rhodes town now, especially inside the walls

*Research notes for Jamie's track. Builds on research/07 (food), 09 (cafés), 11 (Ottoman, Jewish, Italian layers) and 17 (Byzantine churches, Agios Fanourios). Corrections in verify-deltas.md and verify-deltas-round2.md override those files. Written 8 September 2026 for a walk on Wednesday 30 September 2026.*

## Frame for the scriptwriter

The Medieval City is not a museum; the Greek state's own description is "not a museum complex but a living, inhabited settlement" (Greek Wikipedia, quoting the Ephorate's framing). But nobody can tell you exactly how many people live inside the 4 km of wall. The figure "6,000" is repeated by every guidebook and by the Greek castles gazetteer, which calls it "the largest active medieval city in Europe"; a Rhodian travel firm's page says "2,500 residents share a wonderful palette of multi-ethnic influences"; no census figure for the walled area was published anywhere I could reach. Treat the trajectory as a contested downward slope, not a number. What can be documented is the machinery around the residents: roughly 365 buildings inside the walls belong to the Ministry of Culture and are let by a state landlord (ODAP, formerly TAP); 79 more were confirmed in June 2026 as the heirless property of the deported Jews, now vested in a Jewish welfare organisation; since 14 April 2026 residents need a special permit to drive in, and delivery vans are allowed only 06:00 to 09:00; rents in the wider town are the highest in the Dodecanese and substitute teachers are evicted each May to make room for tourists. Around that walled core the year is still shaped by the Orthodox calendar (Easter fires 20 to 30 m high, the barefoot climb to Tsambika on 8 September, the fanouropita cut for the town's own saint on 27 August), by name days rather than birthdays, by a police regulation that makes 15:00 to 17:30 legally quiet, and by a tourist season that on 30 September is exactly at its hinge: it is the last day of summer quiet hours, the last day of the high-season cruise levy, and the week the souma stills are lit in the mountain villages. Jamie is walking through a town at the moment it exhales.

## Tell at stop 1 (the berth) and stop 21 (the birthday drink): the cruise-ship economy and how the island counts itself

- Rhodes took 547,549 cruise passengers in 2025, sixth among Greek ports after Piraeus, Mykonos, Santorini, Corfu and Heraklion; Greece as a whole had 6,129 ship calls and 8,415,713 passengers, up from 5,490 and 7,927,709 in 2024 (Dimokratiki, 18 Aug 2026, relaying ERT). August 2026 had 55 cruise calls scheduled, with four ships in port at once on 10 August; the airport passed 1,093,044 passengers in June 2026 alone, and the island expects "over 3 million" visitors for the year, after just over 3 million in 2025 (Ta Nea via inewsgr, 8 Aug 2026). The headline verb in the Athens press was "βουλιάζει", "sinking", under tourists.
- Since 21 July 2025 every cruise passenger stepping ashore in Greece pays a "sustainable tourism" levy: €20 at Mykonos and Santorini in high season, €5 at other ports including Rhodes from 1 June to 30 September, lower in shoulder and winter months (Euronews, 21 Jul 2025). Scarlet Lady's call on 30 September is the last day of the high-season rate. Euronews gives the other tiers as €12 (Mykonos/Santorini) and €3 (other ports) in the shoulder months of April, May and October, and €4 and €1 from 1 November to 31 March, paid quarterly by the operators through a digital platform; the number of the enabling law is not verified here.
- The labour behind the season is increasingly imported. The hotel-sector institute ITEP found 26.6 per cent of Greek hotels hired non-EU workers in 2025 and 36,000 of 263,000 positions went unfilled; it names "the difficulty of finding affordable housing in popular tourist destinations, where... a significant portion is directed to short-term rentals" as a primary cause, with the South Aegean, Crete, the Ionian and Macedonia-Thrace holding about 70 per cent of the shortage (Dimokratiki, 20 Aug 2026). The local paper Rodiaki headlined that some 9,000 "metaklitoi", workers brought in under the third-country recruitment scheme, are on Rhodes this year (headline only; article behind a block). The tourism workers' federation president Giorgos Chotzoglou says only 10 per cent of hotels and 5 per cent of catering businesses apply the collective agreement, that "the worker is not a machine", and that seasonal unemployment benefit requires 100 insurance stamps a season (Dimokratiki, 20 Aug 2026). That last rule is why a waiter's autumn mood is arithmetic: did the season give me my hundred stamps.
- The cruise passenger who does not sleep on the island is a specific kind of visitor in local talk, and the Old Town shopkeeper's day is organised around ship timetables (research/07 and 09 record the 09:00 to 20:00 shop pattern on Sokratous). Nothing I could reach quotes a named Old Town resident on cruise crowds; the sourced residents' complaints are about noise, cleanliness, occupation of public space, access to homes, shop hours and touting (Rodiaki report of a public evening on protecting the Old Town, from the search index; the article itself was blocked). Do not put words in a resident's mouth.

## Tell at stop 2 (Gate of the Virgin) and stop 3 (Our Lady of the Burgh): who lives inside the walls, and who owns the houses

- The walled area is 65.85 ha, inscribed by UNESCO in 1988 under criteria ii, iv and v; the Hellenic Ministry of Culture's Ephorate of Antiquities of the Dodecanese, headquartered on Ippoton street inside the walls, manages it (Wikipedia; efadod.gr). In 1522 the city held about 5,000 people (Greek News Agenda). Under the Ottomans the Greeks were pushed out to the marasia, nine suburbs around the castle that are now ordinary neighbourhoods of the modern town (Agios Nikolaos, Ano and Kato Rodini and others: Greek Wikipedia), while Turks and Jews stayed inside (research/11). So the people who live inside today are, in the long view, the third or fourth population to hold the grid.
- Who is there now: Greeks (including families who moved in after 1948 and people who rent from the state); a Muslim community of Turkish origin estimated at 2,500 (its association president) to 3,500 (its association), Greek citizens since 1947 and never subject to the 1923 exchange because the island was Italian; the Ibrahim Pasha mosque on Platonos square (1540/41) is "the only one open to worship today", and was granted an official operating licence only in 2019 (Wikipedia, Ibrahim Pasha Mosque, Rhodes; the Turks of the Dodecanese article gives the population figures but does not mention the mosque; research/11); a Jewish community of about 40 (research/11); foreign and Athenian owners of restored houses (a documented presence, but I could not find a citable quotation: the elxis.com line previously quoted here is not on that page); and University of the Aegean students competing for rooms (Dimokratiki, 19 Aug 2026). I could not find any demographic breakdown by nationality for the walled area; the above is a list of documented presences, not proportions.
- The landlord is the state. The Ministry of Culture owns "approximately 365 properties" inside the walls, 50 of them on the Street of the Knights, and in March 2022 commissioned, with the European Commission's DG REFORM and consultants AARC, a management strategy that divides them into a monumental zone, a commercial zone and a residential zone; the minister, Lina Mendoni, listed among its aims "ensuring housing within appropriate modern standards" alongside "gentle tourism exploitation" (Days of Art, 2 Mar 2022). On 7 April 2023 she signed a ten-year programme contract worth €63,576,244.30 with the region and the municipality covering underground utilities, four squares, the Ottoman baths, the medieval gardens and a property-utilisation programme for ODAP's holdings, with the line "we must socialise our monuments" (ERT, 7 Apr 2023). The first seven properties went out to lease-tender with a deadline of 16 September 2024 (ERT, 14 Aug 2024). Whether any of the 2022 to 2026 programme is a formal UNESCO "management plan" I could not verify: the UNESCO site refused every request. Say "state property strategy", not "UNESCO plan".
- The Jewish houses. In three decisions published on 23 June 2026 (on suits filed 8 October 2024) the multi-member Rhodes court of first instance ruled that 79 properties in the Old Town, in the registry areas named "Evraic", "Mercato", "Ibrahim Pasha" and "Synagogue Shalom", belong to OPAIE, the Organisation for the Care and Rehabilitation of Greek Jews, and struck out land-registry entries from 1950 to 1989 that had recorded them as Greek state property. They were freehold (mulk) homes and shops of people "arrested during the racial persecutions of the Second World War" (the article gives no date; the July 1944 deportation is documented in research/11) who never returned and for whom no heir ever appeared; Law 846/1946 diverted such heirless estates from the state to OPAIE, which the court found had possessed them in good faith for over thirty years (Dimokratiki, 3 Jul 2026). Walking from the Gate of the Virgin into the Juderia you are walking through the largest block of that inheritance.
- Traffic. Since Tuesday 14 April 2026, under municipal regulatory decision 140/2025, the Symi Square entrance is closed to vehicles, "free vehicle entry is prohibited, while permanent residents may enter only with a special permit", supply vehicles are admitted "exclusively between 06:00 and 09:00", and daily police checks and a reporting app (MyStreet) enforce it (ERT, 9 Apr 2026; 12vima, 11 Apr 2026). The resident's permit is now the physical badge of belonging inside the walls.
- Housing pressure in the wider town: average asking rent in Rhodes municipality was €10.27 per m² in July 2026, up 4.1 per cent on the year, the highest in the Dodecanese; a 50 m² flat about €514, a 70 m² about €719, furnished studios from €590; substitute teachers "face eviction before the school year ends to accommodate tourists"; flats "convert to short-term rentals May to September" (Dimokratiki, 19 Aug 2026). Athens' and Thessaloniki's freeze on new short-term-rental registrations was extended to 31 December 2027, but Rhodes is not in a freeze zone; the article's AirDNA figure of €200.35 average daily rate in July 2026, up 12.8 per cent, is for Greece as a whole, and no Rhodes-specific rate is given (Dimokratiki, 8 Sep 2026). Depopulation inside the walls therefore has a mechanism you can describe without a census: a state landlord re-letting, a private market converting, and a permit system that defines who is "permanent".

## Tell at stop 4 (Hippocrates Square) and stop 16 (Roloi, Suleymaniye, the library): the kafeneio, the volta and the legally quiet afternoon

- The kafeneio was "a place where women were not welcome but now kafenia are frequented by girls and women"; it serves Greek coffee, frappé, beer, retsina and ouzo with meze, "rarely full meals", and is "the social centre of the villages and islands" where people "play a game of cards" after work (Wikipedia, Kafenio). The unwritten rules a walker can watch for: the regulars sit facing the square, not the wall; a single coffee buys the chair for the afternoon; the man who is celebrating pays for the table (see name days below); a stranger who sits inside rather than at the edge has made a statement. A travel-writer's lament, "these days kafeneia have started to fade away... a kafenio is what Greece is made of" (Stema Journeys), is the tone locals use too. On Hippocrates Square the Cellar of Knights under the Castellania stairs (research/09) is the nearest thing to a kafeneio the tourist zone allows.
- Cold coffee is the modern kafeneio's currency: freddo espresso, sketo, metrio, glyko (research/09). Order it the local way and the transaction changes register.
- The volta is "a leisurely stroll along the main promenade of your town during the hours of sundown... the main square or beside the sea", shared "with friends and neighbours", the Greek cognate of the Italian passeggiata and the Balkan korzo (Mahabis guide to volta). In Rhodes town the volta runs along Mandraki and up to Elli, outside the walls; the Old Town's evening is for visitors. Sunset on 30 September is about 18:55 (verify-deltas-round2 overrules research/09's 19:00), after the 18:00 all-aboard, so Jamie will see the town before its volta; say so.
- The quiet hours (koini isychia) are law, not custom: Police Regulation 3/1996, Government Gazette B 15 of 12 January 1996, amended 1998 and stiffened by Law 4637/2019. From 1 April to 30 September noise is prohibited 15:00 to 17:30 and 23:00 to 07:00; from 1 October to 31 March 15:30 to 17:30 and 22:00 to 07:30. Banned: works producing noise, "operation of musical instruments, radio, television or amplifiers", "noisy conversations, shouting, disputes"; 10deco classes the offence as a petty offence (πταίσμα) and says Law 4637/2019 stiffened the sanctions without stating them; the figure "up to five months' imprisonment under the Penal Code" comes from Protothema alone (23 Mar 2026) and I have not read the Penal Code article itself, so say "a few months" rather than a number (10deco.gr; Protothema). 30 September 2026 is the last day of the summer schedule; on 1 October the night curfew moves an hour earlier. The siesta is therefore enforceable: a resident inside the walls can call the police on a bar at 15:10.
- The Muslim library and mosque at stop 16 mark the community whose family life still runs on a second calendar (Ramadan, the two Bayrams) inside the Orthodox one; the wedding office of the Muslim community is in the Mustafa Pasha mosque on Arionos square (research/11).

## Tell at stop 6 (the Hospital) and stop 8 (the exploded church): the year as the Church keeps it, and what 30 September is

- The Orthodox year that frames Rhodian life: Great Lent seven weeks before Easter, Holy Week, Easter, then the summer fast for the Dormition (1 to 14 August) leading to 15 August, then the autumn feasts (Nativity of the Virgin 8 September, the Cross 14 September) and the winter run to Christmas. In Rhodes the fixed points are local: Easter in the Old Town means the Epitaphios carried from Agios Fanourios through the cobbled lanes, "one of the most emblematic processions on the island" (rhodesguide.com, Easter in Rhodes, which confirms the Agios Fanourios procession but says nothing about the Evangelismos cathedral; that the New Town's Epitaphios and Resurrection centre on Evangelismos by the town hall is unverified). On Good Friday, during the Epitaphios procession, Rhodians light the kalafounos, a bonfire kept burning until Easter Sunday, when Judas is burned on it after the Resurrection; villages and town neighbourhoods hold an informal competition for the tallest, which "in many cases reaches 20 to 30 metres" according to Panagiotis Engglezos, president of the island's federation of cultural associations (Ethnos; iefimerida has the Agios Isidoros fire lit on Holy Saturday). In Agios Isidoros the unmarried, the "lefteroi", elect a council on Holy Saturday which on Easter Monday fines anyone who came late to church or missed a service, and dunks non-payers in the vourna, the water trough; the fines go to charity; the village of about 100 permanent residents swells to 700 for it (iefimerida; Ethnos). This is Jamie's brief in one custom: a village organising its unmarried young by mock law, at Easter, in public.
- 15 August: Panagia Kremasti, west of the airport, "a huge market takes over the streets of the village, while lively celebrations with loud music and careless dancing take place till the early morning hours" (Greeka); the regional tourism board dates the feast 13 to 15 August in Ialysos and Kremasti (aegeanislands.gr). 8 September: Tsambika, the Nativity of the Virgin, where women climb the roughly 300 steps barefoot to ask for a child (greece-moments counts 300, Greeka 350), some sleeping the night in the pilgrims' room, and the names Tsambikos and Tsambika, "exist only on Rhodes, given in gratitude for this wish being granted" (greece-moments.com). 28 to 30 July: Agios Soulas at Soroni, with horse and donkey races in the pine grove (aegeanislands.gr; mygreecetours.org). 27 August: Agios Fanourios in the Old Town, vespers with the bishop, the icon carried through the lanes, liturgy next morning, and "of course fanouropita is distributed, the saint's trademark" (Verena); the recipe and the forty pieces are in research/17. The panigyri itself is the direct descendant of the ancient panegyris, "gathering", the assembly at a sanctuary with prayers, sacrifice, procession and fair (Wikipedia, Panegyris): the Kremasti street market is the fair half of that pair still running.
- Name days. "Traditionally, name days are celebrated as an open house where close friends and relatives drop by to give their wishes, offer a small gift, and receive the traditional kerasma (food, sweets, coffee and drinks)"; "in recent years the open house tradition has changed and name days are celebrated by invitation"; the wish is "Chronia Polla, many years"; a name day is "more important than a birthday, particularly as people get older"; and "the eldest grandson in each family will be named after the grandfather, and the eldest granddaughter after the grandmother" (XpatAthens). The celebrant "would provide drinks and treats for the guests. That is a common practice for all celebrations in Greece even now"; from the Church's side birthdays mark only the earthly arrival and the Church "actively discourage[s] celebrations, parties or festivities to mark the day" (Definitely Greece). So the rule Jamie should know at the birthday lunch: the person celebrating pays, and everyone says chronia polla, not happy birthday. (Greeks whose names have no saint celebrate on All Saints' Sunday, the Sunday after Pentecost: Wikipedia's Name days in Greece lists "everyone with no name day" on that movable feast, and the All Saints' Day article fixes the Orthodox date.)
- What is 30 September in the calendar. The Orthodox commemorations are Gregory the Illuminator, "Bishop and Enlightener of Greater Armenia" (d. c. 335), with the martyrs Rhipsime and Gaiane and their companions, Stratonicus and Mardonius (Wikipedia, September 30 liturgics); the Greek page adds the new-martyr Metropolitan Emilianos of Grevena (d. 1911) (Greek Wikipedia, 30 Σεπτεμβρίου). It is a quiet day: Greeks named Grigoris keep their name day on 25 January, for Gregory the Theologian, not today (Wikipedia, Name days in Greece). No Rhodian custom attaches to 30 September that I could find. The day's local meaning is secular: end of summer quiet hours, end of the high-season cruise levy, and (per Visit Greece, in research/07) the start of souma distillation in Embonas and Siana "in late September, immediately after the harvest". A birthday here falls in the week the island turns from serving to making.
- Death, briefly. Memorials "may be held on the 3rd, 9th and 40th day, and on the completion of a year from the day of death", commonly also at three years; kollyva, boiled wheat, is blessed on a table before the icon and the priest may pour wine, oil and kollyva on the grave (OrthodoxWiki). Loring Danforth's *The Death Rituals of Rural Greece* (Potamia, Thessaly) describes the laments, daily grave visits and the exhumation years later that let a village "sustain relationships with the dead and at the same time bring them to an end" (loringdanforth.com). Widows in black at the laiki and the kollyva trays in the church porch on a Saturday of Souls are the visible ends of that system in Rhodes town.

## Tell at stop 13 (Kahal Shalom) and stop 14 (Square of the Martyrs): household, kinship, koumbaros, the wedding and the melekouni

- Rhodian wedding custom, as summarised on a local site: "the processions and the carrying of the dowry goods of the bride and also of the groom form a large part of the Rhodian tradition"; days before the wedding the bride's relatives and friends "lay out the proikia", stacking blankets, sheets, woven cloths, tablecloths, robes and nightdresses into the giouko, the display pile; "another notable custom of the old days was the cleaning of the new house in which the newlyweds would live" (rodosreport.gr, from the search index). Melekouni, the honey-and-sesame diamond, is the wedding announcement itself: made by the guests under an expert's eye, sent to those who cannot attend "like sugared almonds in the towns", carried house to house by men with musicians as the invitation in Maritsa, with the wish "may you live as many years as there are sesame seeds in the piece" (EU PGI dossier via research/07). The bride carried moschopougia to her mother-in-law at engagement (research/07). For the birthday, the melekouni wish converts cleanly.
- Koumbaros. The wedding sponsor who exchanges the crowns and normally baptises the first child; the tie he creates, koumbaria, is a spiritual kinship that Greeks treat as binding as blood and that bars marriage between the two families. This is general anthropological knowledge (Campbell's *Honour, Family and Patronage* on the Sarakatsani is the classic account); I could not fetch a clean reference page this session, so cite it as such and keep it short.
- The eldest daughter's house. On Karpathos the custom of primogeniture gave the firstborn son, the kanakaris, his father's property and the firstborn daughter, the kanakara, her mother's: "the father's property went to the first son and the mother's to the first daughter" (anamniseis.net); its provisions "had been approved by the mayors of the island by vote on 8 February 1864", it was "a defence mechanism aiming at assembling and preserving the economic power of the family", the Church opposed it as ruinous to younger siblings, it declined after 1920 and was abolished "after the liberation of the Dodecanese in 1947 and the application of the Civil Code provisions" (aegeanislands.gr, Karpathos; the 1864 vote and the Civil Code abolition rest on that tourism-board page alone: neither anamniseis.net nor TRC Leiden mentions them, TRC saying only that the custom lasted "until c. 1948", so give the date as "the 1860s, by local vote" rather than the day). The kanakara also inherited the kolaina, the chain of gold coins, Byzantine, Venetian and English, worn at the neck (anamniseis.net), and "everything from their mothers, including clothing, textiles and embroideries", keeping "white hands" while younger sisters did the field work (TRC Leiden). Renee Hirschon's gloss: "If you live on an island the land is limited, so if the families would continue to divide their land it would end up having no value"; Olympos had 1,200 people in 1947 and 60 to 65 permanent residents now (New Lines Magazine; Greek Wikipedia has 1,202 in 1947 and 200 in the 2021 census, so treat 60 to 65 as the magazine's count of year-round residents). The claim for Rhodes is weaker but real: the regional tourism board's page on Rhodian architecture says of the village house that "the first born daughter inherited her mother's property. This fact resulted in the fragmentation of the building into small, single-room houses" (aegeanislands.gr, Rhodes architecture). So: Karpathos codified it and abolished it by statute; Rhodes practised a version of it and its houses show the marks. Do not say Kalymnos or Symi; the only link I found is Karpathian surnames turning up there through emigration of disinherited younger children (anamniseis.net).
- The Rhodian house itself, for stop 13's lanes: a single long room "with an entrance on one of the two long sides"; interiors of "embroideries, woven cloths, plates and pitchers on shelves along the walls", so dense that "on many occasions the plates occupy the entire surface of the long wall, which is thus called piatelotichos (plate wall)"; courtyards "filled with flowers" and paved with chochlakia, "mosaics of black and white pebbles", whose designs in Lindos "show how many ships the owner of the house had" (aegeanislands.gr). Greek Reporter ran "Reviving the timeless art of pebble mosaic in Rhodes" on 6 September 2026, two days before these notes; the page was blocked, so use only the fact that the craft is being revived. Inside the walls the pebble courtyards are behind doors; Jamie will see them through open gates in the Juderia and at Christos Garden in the New Town (research/09).
- Naming closes the loop: the eldest granddaughter takes the grandmother's name (XpatAthens), so a family's house and its name travel together down the female line in the very neighbourhoods where the Jewish houses have just been re-titled to a welfare fund because no heir survived.

## Tell at stop 15 (lunch at Nireas) and stop 18 (Liberty Gate): the market, the season's end, the young, the university

- The market. The Nea Agora is a closed construction site until at least October 2026 (research/07). The town's producers' market, the laiki of Agios Dimitrios by the Taxiarches cemetery, was listed by the municipality in 2020 as Wednesday and Saturday; the municipality's own August 2026 notice treats Saturday as the normal day (moved to Friday 14 August for the Dormition), and the Vironos street (Thursday) and Ptolemaion/Analipsi (Friday) markets also run (rhodes.gr, 2020 and 12 Aug 2026). The Wednesday session is confirmed for 2026: the municipality's notice of 20 March 2026 names Wednesday as the normal day (moved to Tuesday 24 March that week because 25 March fell on a Wednesday), so the Wednesday-and-Saturday schedule is live (verify-deltas.md, laiki). Wednesday 30 September 2026 is therefore a market morning at Agios Dimitrios; hours (08:00 to 14:00) rest on a guide site only.
- The season. Rhodes "ends the season the final week of October"; tourist hotels close on 1 November; "most hotels, restaurants and tour operators shut their doors from November to March", and those with enough stamps draw seasonal benefit while others "go back to the mainland and work in their hometowns over the winter" (Tripadvisor threads from the search index; a forum source, treat as colour). On 30 September the town is a month from that, in the good weeks: cooler, fewer ships, staff counting stamps, the New Town's year-round population visible again in the evening. The Dimokratiki housing report is the flip side: the flats let to tourists May to September come back onto the winter market in October, which is why the teacher who was evicted in May finds a place now.
- The university. The University of the Aegean (founded 1984) keeps its School of Humanities in Rhodes town, in "the former School of Constabulary and the former Kazarma Reggina", two Italian-era barracks just outside the walls, with departments of Primary Education, Pre-School Education and Educational Design, and Mediterranean Studies (confirmed by the School's own site, sae.aegean.gr, which gives the address as Dimokratias 47-49) and "over 3,000 students" (Greeka; the student figure and the barracks names appear only on Greeka, the studyingreece page is dead, and Wikipedia gives only a university-wide total of about 10,000, so say "some three thousand"). Its students are the one young, non-tourist population the town imports, and the housing report says they lose the summer bidding war for rooms.
- The young. I found no Rhodes-specific study of youth emigration this session (search budget exhausted before that query ran). What is documented: an ageing profile for the region (about 18 per cent over 65, from a secondary source); a municipal-unit population of 56,440 in the 2021 census (Wikipedia); a tourism labour force that is 26.6 per cent third-country and a union president saying "workers are leaving tourism" (Dimokratiki, 20 Aug 2026). Script it as the union's claim, not as a statistic.

## Tell at stop 11 and 12 (the walls walk): one line

- From the rampart: state-owned Ippoton inside; the Juderia's OPAIE houses south-east; the Turkish quarter round Platonos and Arionos; the nine marasia outside; Mandraki north for the volta. ## Continuities and contrasts

- An ancient Rhodian would recognise the panigyri: the panegyris was exactly a gathering at a shrine with sacrifice, procession and a fair, and Kremasti on 15 August still has all three. He would recognise the volta as the evening in the agora, and the habit of treating the company on your own feast. He would not recognise a state that owns a third of the houses.
- A Hospitaller would recognise the ownership pattern immediately: the Order held the Collachium and let the burgh; today the Ministry holds 365 buildings and lets by tender. He would recognise the fast before 15 August and the Dormition itself (his Church kept it too), the fires at Easter, and the idea that the population inside the walls is chosen rather than born, by permit then as now. He would not recognise women at the kafeneio table or a court giving Jewish houses back.
- An Ottoman-era householder inside the walls would recognise the two calendars running side by side, the wedding office at the mosque, the marasia outside as the Greek town, and the coffee-house as the men's parliament. He would recognise the siesta and the noise rules, if not the Gazette number. He would be baffled that the Greeks now live inside and the Turks are a few thousand, and that the Jews of the quarter are commemorated in six languages in a square.
- A resident today recognises all of it as inheritance in the Karpathos sense: the house came down the female line, the name came from the grandmother, the feast is the saint's not the birthday, the fine for noise is the same as for missing church in Agios Isidoros. What she does not share with any of the three is the arithmetic of stamps, permits and per-night rates that now decides whether the next generation lives inside the walls at all.

# Rhodes rules the waves: the ancient harbours, shipsheds and navy of Rhodes, the Rhodian Sea Law, pirates, and the Knights' fleet (the ground under the gangway)

## The ground under the gangway

Scarlet Lady ties up on the outer face of the Tourist Port mole. Walk down the gangway and you are standing on the eastern arm of the ancient **Great Harbour** of Rhodes, the basin the Greeks called the *Emporion* and modern Rhodians call **Kolona** (the inner, northern part) and the **Emborio/Tourist Port** (the outer part). Two moles enclose it: the Naillac mole to the north and the Windmills mole to the east; the cruise berth is bolted onto the Windmills mole. Behind the mole is Mandraki, the small closed harbour; ahead, the sea-lane to Alexandria that made the city rich.

**Foundation (408 BC).** Strabo, who came here in the age of Augustus, could not contain himself: the city "is so far superior to all others in harbours and roads and walls and improvements in general that I am unable to speak of any other city as equal to it, or even as almost equal to it, much less superior to it." He adds the origin story: "The present city was founded at the time of the Peloponnesian War by the same architect, as they say, who founded the Peiraeus" (Strabo 14.2.5, 14.2.9, Loeb via LacusCurtius). Whether Hippodamus himself drew the grid is "as they say" even in Strabo; what is certain is the synoecism of 408/7 BC, when Lindos, Ialysos and Kameiros "all came together at Rhodes" (14.2.11). The archaeologists count **five harbours** for the ancient city: Mandraki (the small, military harbour), the Great Harbour (Kolona/Emporio), Akandia to the south, plus the West and South harbours (Stefanakis, Spantidaki & Mpardanis 2022, citing Blackman et al.). Today three of them still work: Kolona (inter-island ferries), the Central/Tourist Port (cruise, Turkey ferries) and Akandia (cargo).

**The secret dockyards.** This was a naval base first. Strabo: the Rhodians "held the mastery of the sea for a long time and overthrew the business of piracy"; their poor were fed by liturgies "so that ... the city does not run short of useful men, and in particular for the manning of the fleets. As for the roadsteads, some of them were kept hidden and forbidden to the people in general; and death was the penalty for any person who spied on them or passed inside them." The Greek is exact: *τῶν δὲ ναυστάθμων τινὰ καὶ κρυπτὰ ἦν καὶ ἀπόρρητα τοῖς πολλοῖς, τῷ δὲ κατοπτεύσαντι ἢ παρελθόντι εἴσω θάνατος ὥριστο ἡ ζημία* (Strabo 14.2.5, Meineke text on Perseus): peer in or step inside a *naustathmon* and the penalty was death. Those forbidden sheds have been found. Rescue excavations published by Blackman, Knoblauch and Yiannikouri ("Die Schiffshäuser am Mandrakihafen in Rhodos", *Archäologischer Anzeiger* 1996, 371-476) uncovered Hellenistic shipsheds (*neosoikoi*) at the south end of Mandraki; the 2022 HISTIA paper summarises "a few shipsheds ... with the main volume located by the small/military (mod. Mandraki) harbour. A rough estimate speaks of a max. of 100 shipsheds allocated on the east and south shoreline of the harbour" for a fleet "estimated to consist of less than 100 ships of various types, kataphracts such as trireme, tetreres, penteres and aphracts such as hemiolia, trihemiolia, and smaller vessels known as 'phylakides nēes'". On the ground the findspot is the fenced sunken area on the seaward side of the moat, labelled on OpenStreetMap "Ancient shipyards - Roman tetrapylon" (36.44576, 28.22607), between the Nea Agora and the Liberty Gate. Stand on the pavement of Eleftherias street and look down: the ramps once ran into water that is now the taxi rank and the Italian market.

**Monster-hunters of the sea.** The pirates' ship was the *hemiolia*, "one-and-a-half" files of oars, fast and light. Rhodes answered with the **trihemiolia**, first attested in 304 BC when a squadron went out as commerce raiders during Demetrius's siege, and "very likely ... also invented there, as a counter to the pirates' swift hemioliai" (Wikipedia, citing Morrison and Casson); the Lindos relief gives a crew of 144, 120 of them rowers. Diodorus (20.81) says Rhodes "by herself undertook her war against the pirates and purged the seas of these evil-doers," and during the great siege "Damophilus, who had ships of the kind called by the Rhodians 'guard-ships,' sailed to Carpathos ... he sank some, shattering them with his rams" (20.93). The saboteur story is pure D&D: Philip V's agent Heracleides of Tarentum arrived posing as a defector, won the trust of the Rhodian prytaneis (Polybius 13.4-5), then, "taking advantage of a rough and windy night, he set fire to all their docks. Thirteen of them were entirely destroyed, with all the ships that were in them" (Polyaenus 5.17.2, attalus.org). The reckoning came at **Chios in 201 BC**: against Philip's fifty-three decked ships and "a hundred and fifty galleys and beaked ships", the allies had "sixty-five decked warships, including those of the Byzantines, nine trihemioliae, and three triremes"; the Rhodians "dipping their prows themselves ... received the enemy's blow above water, but piercing him below water produced breaches which could not be repaired." Philip's ten-banked flagship was lost; he "lost about three thousand Macedonian soldiers and six thousand sailors"; the Rhodian admiral **Theophiliscus** "survived for one day, and after writing a dispatch to his country about the battle and appointing Cleonaeus to replace him in command, died of his wounds" (Polybius 16.2-9, LacusCurtius). Behind the fleet stood the **Peraia**, Rhodian demes on the Carian shore opposite, which after Apamea (188 BC) swelled into "the entirety of Caria and Lycia south of the Maeander" until Rome took it back in 167 BC (Wikipedia, Rhodian Peraia). After the earthquake of c. 227 BC the kings rebuilt this navy for free: Ptolemy sent "timber for the construction of ten quinqueremes and ten triremes ... three thousand pieces of sail-cloth" and "twenty thousand artabae to feed the crews of ten triremes" (Polybius 5.89).

**The law that outlived the fleet.** Cassius abolished the Rhodian navy in 46 BC, but "Rhodian law" sailed on. Justinian's *Digest* 14.2.1 (Paulus): *Lege Rhodia cavetur, ut si levandae navis gratia iactus mercium factus est, omnium contributione sarciatur quod pro omnibus datum est* ("By the Rhodian law it is provided that if goods are jettisoned to lighten a ship, what was given for all is made good by the contribution of all"), the root of general average in marine insurance. *Digest* 14.2.9 (Volusius Maecianus) preserves a Greek petition: shipwrecked Eudaemon of Nicomedia complains of being plundered by the *demosioi* of the Cyclades, and the emperor answers, *ἐγὼ μὲν τοῦ κόσμου κύριος, ὁ δὲ νόμος τῆς θαλάσσης· τῷ νόμῳ τῶν Ῥοδίων κρινέσθω τῷ ναυτικῷ* ("I am indeed lord of the world, but the law is lord of the sea; let it be judged by the sea-law of the Rhodians, wherever none of our own laws opposes it"). The Byzantine *Nomos Rhodion Nautikos*, edited by Ashburner (1909), is another thing: its language "cannot well be earlier than the seventh century", it is "clearly later than the Novels of Justinian", its title lists emperors (Hadrian, Antoninus, Tiberius, Septimius Severus, Pertinax) as guarantors, and Ashburner is blunt: "any learned person who was making a collection of rules of maritime law would be strongly inclined to call his book the Rhodian law, in order to add to its authority." Hellenistic Rhodes had customary sea-law; the book bearing its name is a medieval heirloom with a forged pedigree.

**The quay of the orators.** Two Romans stepped ashore here to learn to speak. In 75 BC a young Julius Caesar, sailing for Rhodes "to study under Apollonius Molo, the most eminent teacher of oratory of that time", was "taken by pirates near the island of Pharmacussa" (Suetonius, *Julius* 4). Plutarch adds that when they asked twenty talents he "laughed at them for not knowing who their captive was, and of his own accord agreed to give them fifty"; for thirty-eight days he read them his poems, called the unimpressed "illiterate Barbarians", and after the Milesian ransom came, he hunted them down and "crucified them all, just as he had often warned them" (Plutarch, *Caesar* 1-3). Then he "sailed to Rhodes to study under Apollonius the son of Molon ... of whom Cicero also was a pupil." Cicero's own memory of this harbour: *Rhodum veni meque ad eundem quem Romae audiveram Molonem adplicavi* ... *ita recepi me biennio post non modo exercitatior sed prope mutatus* ("I came to Rhodes and attached myself to the same Molon ... I came back two years later not only better trained but almost transformed": *Brutus* 316, Latin Library).

**The Knights' chain.** Stand at the ruined stump of the **Tower of Naillac** (OSM way 266509202, 36.44633, 28.22955). Grand Master Philibert de Naillac built it 1396-1421 "on the mole east of the Gate of Saint Paul that closes the commercial harbour"; it was 46 m tall, square, and fell to 19th-century earthquakes before the Ottomans demolished it. Across the harbour mouth stands its partner, the **Tower of the Windmills** ("Tower of France or Tower of the Angel", built by d'Aubusson, carrying the arms of France; "originally 13 windmills. Today only one is left"). Between them ran the chain: in 1522, per the Italian Wikipedia entry citing Marin Sanudo's *Diarii*, "la flotta degli Ospedalieri fu ancorata nel porto commerciale che venne poi chiuso da due pesanti catene, l'una tra la Torre di Naillac e la Torre dei Mulini, l'altra tra questa e Forte San Nicola"; the English article agrees that "the harbour entrance was blocked by a heavy iron chain, behind which the order's fleet was anchored." Pilgrims felt the wariness. Nicolas de Martoni (1394-95) could not enter "portum Rodi" at night because the patron feared "naviliis cursorum" (corsair ships), and a Catalan corsair's pinnace was inside. Felix Fabri in 1480, sailing into "the port of the Colossus" by moonlight weeks after the siege: "the people ashore lighted beacons on their towers ... they alarmed us much by firing a big cannon at us"; they "permitted us to anchor our ship outside the harbour, but would not let us come into the harbour for fear of treachery," and next morning the pilgrims rowed in "passing through the bodies of dead Turks cast up by the sea." Pietro Casola (1494) counted "sixteen of the said mills on the walls of the port" and called it "the largest and strongest between Venice and Jaffa." Martoni is also the earliest witness to the legend that the Colossus stood "with one foot on the head of the mole where the church of St Nicholas is and the other on the head of the other mole where the windmills are ... ships passed with masts and sails between its legs" (Revue de l'Orient latin 3, 1895, 585-6): legend, not fact.

**Departure, 1 January 1523.** The Order's flagship was the Great Carrack: first the *San Juan Baptista* (1478), then the Mamluk *Mogarbina* captured in 1507 and renamed *Santa Maria*; on the *Santa Maria*'s mainmast the icon of Our Lady of Philermos was fixed as the fleet left. The relic-hand of John the Baptist, sent by Bayezid II to the Knights in 1484, went with them. "The remaining knights and soldiers marched out of the town, with banners flying, drums beating, and in battle armour. They boarded the 50 ships ... accompanied by several thousand civilians" (Wikipedia, Siege of Rhodes 1522; the Italian article gives 4,000). They anchored at Malta in 1530 on the *Santa Anna*, the lead-sheathed carrack laid down at Nice the month Rhodes fell.

**Underwater afterlife.** Finds "lifted mainly from the port of Rhodes" fill the free exhibition *Rodos ... Stories from the seabed* on Ippoton street; the Ministry page (updated 7 May 2026) warns "the exhibition will remain closed from 06.05.2026", so check before walking up.

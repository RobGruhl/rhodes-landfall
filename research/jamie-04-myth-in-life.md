# Jamie 04. Myth as lived practice on Rhodes: from Helios's lottery to the fanouropita

## Frame for the scriptwriter

Rhodes is a place where myth was never "stories": it was the calendar, the constitution, the passport and the insurance policy. The island's charter myth (Pindar, 464 BC) explains why the year is named after the priest of the Sun, why the three old cities each got a founder-grandson, why the great sanctuary at Lindos burned its offerings without fire, and why a Rhodian embassy could walk into Argos and be greeted as "kinsmen." Hero cult gave the dead a civic job (Tlepolemus got games, Phorbas got a constellation, Althaemenes got an oracle-ordered shrine). When Christianity arrived the machinery kept running with new names on the doors: the Sun's priest became the bishop's calendar of saints, the anatomical votive became the tin tama, the panegyris became the panigyri, the hero's annual games became the saint's three-night festival behind the church. Jamie's thread through the walk is this: watch for the same social needs (naming a year, naming a child, finding what is lost, getting rain, getting pregnant, getting home safe from the sea) and notice how each regime answered them. Everything below is fact-checked against primary texts where possible; research/04 already covers the Telchines, the dragon of Malpasso, and the relics, and research/17 the church of Agios Fanourios and the cake, so those are only cross-referenced here.

## Tell at stop 1 (cruise berth) or stop 19 (deer columns): the Sun's chariot driven into the sea

The Halieia ("Sun-games") were the island's principal festival, held in summer in honour of Helios with a great procession, gymnastic and musical contests, horse and chariot races, and a wreath of white poplar for the victors, the poplar being sacred to the god for its shining leaves. Athenaeus, Xenophon of Ephesus and the lexicographers attest it, and after the Colossus went up the games drew envoys and competitors from Pergamon and the other kingdoms; Pythian, Isthmian and Nemean victors thought Rhodes worth the trip. The ritual to tell at the water's edge is recorded by Festus (De verborum significatu, s.v. October equus): "the Rhodians, who every year throw four-horse chariots consecrated to the Sun into the sea, because he is said to be carried round the world in such a chariot." Festus gives no date and no explanation beyond that one; the widely repeated "24th of Gorpiaeus" is not his but the Pindar scholiast's end-date for the Halieia games (the 24th of the Rhodian month Panamos, which the scholiast equated with Macedonian Gorpiaios), and Iversen doubts the chariot rite belonged to the Halieia at all, reading it as a solstice-or-equinox ceremony marking the completion of the Sun's circuit. Whether the horses drowned or the vehicle alone went in is not settled by the sources, and Festus is a Roman lexicographer reporting at second hand, so present it as "the Rhodians were said to". Paul Iversen (Eulimene 2018-19) has shown the Halieia are named on the Antikythera Mechanism's games dial, one of only a handful of festivals the device tracks, which is the best evidence of their standing in the Hellenistic Greek world.

Jamie's angle: a maritime society making its wealth from ships choosing to throw a chariot, the most expensive land vehicle it owned, into the harbour once a year. Compare the fishing-boat blessings and the Epiphany cross-diving (6 January) of modern Greek harbours, where the priest throws a cross into the sea and young men dive for it; the modern rite is Christian in content but identical in grammar (the community's most valuable object into the water, retrieved as a blessing).

## Tell at stop 8 (St John's site) and stop 9 (Palace): the Sun's house, the Sun's priest, and the Sun's calendar

Badoud (2024) now puts the sanctuary of Helios on this small hill under the Palace (research/01 and 02 have the argument; Vedder and Hoepfner disagree, and Zachhuber's 2024 book, following Hoepfner, places the sanctuary on the acropolis, identifying it with the so-called temenos of Apollo Pythios, so say "the current leading view"). The point for Jamie is what the sanctuary did, not where it was.

**The year was named after the Sun's priest.** The priest of Helios was the eponymous official: every Rhodian document, contract and tombstone was dated "in the priesthood of X." Rhodes thus ran on a religiously grounded chronology in the way Athens ran on its archon list. A surviving inscription of the priests of Helios (SEG 12.360, published by Morricone 1949-51) is thought to begin with the year of the synoecism, 408/7 BC. Stéphanie Paul calls the priesthood "the culmination of the Rhodian priestly career" and says the priest "was appointed in turns from the tribes of Ialysos, Cameiros, and Lindos"; the further claim in secondary summaries of Badoud that he was chosen by lot is unsourced. Treat the rotation as Paul's and Badoud's argument rather than settled fact; I could not reach Badoud's chapter to confirm the wording.

**Two calendars ran at once.** Iversen argues Rhodes kept an eponymous year and a separate bouleutic (council) year with different starting points, plus a leap month, Panamos deuteros, inserted to keep the lunar months in step with the seasons. The twelve Rhodian months, several named after festivals, were Agrianios, Badromios, Theudaisios, Dalios, Artamitios, Panamos, Pedageitnyos, Hyakinthios, Karneios, Thesmophorios, Sminthios and Diosthyos (the exact order is still argued; Iversen has the council year running from 1 Karneios to 30 Thesmophorios and the priest of Helios taking office on 1 Dalios, so do not call any one month "the first"); the Lindian Chronicle is dated "on the twelfth of the month Artamitios." Sminthios points to the Sminthia, the festival of Apollo Smintheus, the mouse-god, worshipped at Lindos; Karneios and Hyakinthios are Dorian festivals shared with Sparta; Thesmophorios is the women's festival of Demeter. A Rhodian year was a string of religious obligations, and Jamie can read a modern Greek wall calendar the same way: every day carries a saint, and the year is still organised by feasts (Dekapentavgoustos, the Dormition on 15 August, is called "the Easter of summer").

**The synoecism as a religious settlement.** In 408/7 BC the three old poleis, Lindos, Ialysos and Kamiros, founded the single city of Rhodes here. Diodorus implies the old cities were abandoned; they were not. They kept their assemblies, their demes and their cults, and the new state's tribes simply reproduced the old three-way split. What the new city needed was a god who belonged to all three and to none, and Helios, who in the myth had fathered the whole island, was the answer; his facing head went on the first tetradrachms (after 404 BC) with the rose (rhodon, for the nymph Rhodos) on the reverse. Paul's phrase is that the cult of Helios "did not overshadow the preexisting cults of Athena, but rather, they functioned on different levels": Athena Lindia stayed Lindian, Zeus Atabyrios stayed on his mountain, and a joint cult of Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus appeared in each of the three old towns as the "civic" pair, while Helios sat above them all as the island's god. Jamie's frame: a federation that solved its identity problem by inventing a shared ancestor.

**The Sun's house became St John's.** The Knights built their conventual church of St John on this hill and stored the Baptist's hand in it (research/04); after the 1856 explosion the Italians rebuilt it as the Evangelismos at Mandraki. Whether or not the Colossus stood here, the site went from sun-temple to Byzantine citadel to Latin conventual church to Mussolini's palace, and no regime chose a different hill.

## Tell at stop 6 (Hospital / Archaeological Museum): the fireless altar, the goddess who sends rain, and the temple as archive

Pindar's Olympian 7, written for the Rhodian boxer Diagoras's Olympic win of 464 BC, is the island's charter. Three myths, told in reverse order: Tlepolemus the manslayer sent to Rhodes by Apollo's oracle; the birth of Athena from Zeus's head, when the Sun told his sons to be first to sacrifice to her; and the island rising from the sea for the absent Helios, who refused a re-draw of the lots and had Lachesis swear the great oath of the gods that it was his. Diodorus (5.57) says the Heliadae surpassed all men "especially in astrology" and "established the division of the day into hours," a lovely detail for a walk on the Sun's island.

The fireless sacrifice is the lived-practice hook. Pindar, lines 45-52: "they climbed the hill without bringing the seed of burning flame; and they established the sacred precinct on the acropolis with fireless sacrifices. Zeus brought to them a yellow cloud and rained on them abundant gold. And the gray-eyed goddess herself bestowed on them every art." Diodorus adds that the Athenians under Cecrops "performed the sacrifice over fire, but later than the Heliadae," and then the key sentence: "This is the reason, men say, why the peculiar practice as regards the manner of sacrificing persists in Rhodes to this day, and why the goddess has her seat on the island." Philostratus (Imagines 2.27, 3rd c. AD) still describes a painting in which Rhodians offer "fireless sacrifices that are incomplete" while gold rains on their houses and narrow streets. So a ritual quirk at Lindos, no fire on Athena's altar, was explained for at least 700 years by an origin story about a forgetful family, and the story in turn justified Rhodes's claim to be Athena's first and favourite worshippers (over Athens, which is the polemical point). The scholia say a copy of Olympian 7 was kept in gold letters in the temple of Athena at Lindos, on the authority of the Rhodian historian Gorgon; probably gold ink on a scroll rather than gilt stone, but it shows the poem was treated as a sacred text.

**The temple as archive: the Lindian Chronicle (99 BC).** A marble stele about 8 by 3 feet, now in two pieces (in Copenhagen, National Museum of Denmark; the Danish expedition found it), set up "during the priesthood of Teisylos son of Sosikrates, on the twelfth of Artamitios," when the mastroi and the Lindians resolved that because "most of the offerings together with their inscriptions have been destroyed on account of time" (the temple burned in 392/1 BC), Tharsagoras son of Stratos and Timachidas son of Hagesitimos should compile a list of them from books and letters. The list is a genealogy of the island in objects: Lindos himself gave a phiale "to Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus"; the Telchines "a vessel... a tenth of their labours"; Cadmus (per Diodorus 5.58) "a striking bronze cauldron worked after the ancient manner... which carried an inscription in Phoenician letters"; Heracles two wicker shields; Helen "a pair of bracelets" inscribed "Helen to Athena"; Tlepolemus, Menelaus, Minos, Rhesus; and then real kings, Alexander's caltrops "having overcome in battle Darius and becoming lord of Asia," Ptolemy, Pyrrhus, Hieron, Philip V. Danaus and his fifty daughters founded the temple on their flight from Egypt; Diodorus says three of the daughters died at Lindos. Jamie will see immediately what this is: a community writing its own provenance, in which the legendary donors are listed in exactly the same format as the historical ones because, to the compilers, they were the same kind of fact.

**Epiphanies: the goddess as a working institution.** The stele's second half records Athena's interventions. In about 490 BC, when Datis's Persian fleet besieged the Lindian acropolis and the water ran out, the goddess appeared to a magistrate in his sleep and told him to be bold, "since she was about to ask her father for water." The Lindians asked Datis for a five-day truce, saying Athena had sent to her father for help and they would surrender if nothing came. "When on the next day a great dark storm cloud settled over the acropolis and a big storm rained down," the cisterns filled while "the Persian force was in need"; Datis "sent for dedication the mantle and torque and armlets," "made a treaty of friendship with the besieged people," and "declared in addition that the gods protect these people." During Demetrius's siege of 305/4 BC Kallikles, a former priest of Athena Lindia still living in Lindos, dreamed six nights running that Athena wanted the prytanis Anaxipolis to write to King Ptolemy for help; on the sixth night he told the council, and the council sent Anaxipolis to Egypt. Notice the procedure: a dream, reported to the council, minuted, acted on, and a century later inscribed as precedent. This is the ancient form of what Jamie will meet at Tsambika (stop 16): a miracle that becomes a pilgrimage that becomes a naming custom.

**The Museum on a Wednesday.** The Epigraphical exhibition is closed on Wednesdays (research/04 hours), so the inscriptions cannot be seen; the Head of Helios (2nd c. BC, drilled for a bronze ray-crown) and the crouching Aphrodite can. Point out that the Head is a cult image, the face of the god whose priest named the year.

## Tell at stop 10 (D'Amboise Gate): the first monster-hunter, and how a hero gets a job

Rhodes was Ophiussa, "snake island," before it was anything else (Pliny NH 5.36 lists it among the old names). Diodorus 5.58: when the island was infested with serpents, Apollo's oracle told the Rhodians to summon Phorbas the Thessalian; "Phorbas destroyed the serpents, and after he had freed the island of its fear he made his home in Rhodes... after his death he was accorded honours like those offered to heroes." Hyginus (Astronomica 2.14.5), citing the Rhodian writer Polyzelus, puts him in the sky: the constellation Ophiuchus, the Serpent-holder, "is Phorbas, who was of great assistance to the Rhodians"; because Apollo favoured him "he was put among the constellations, shown killing the snake for the sake of praise and commemoration." Hyginus also says "the citizens called their island, overrun by a great number of snakes, Ophiussa." Research/04 tells the 14th-century dragon of Malpasso here; Jamie's version is the institution behind both stories. Hero cult is the mechanism by which a Greek community converts a benefactor's death into an annual civic obligation (a tomb, a sacrifice, sometimes games), and the D'Amboise "dragon's head" is the same social act performed by the Knights: a trophy over the gate that tells newcomers who cleared the land.

The second hero here is Althaemenes of Crete (Diodorus 5.59), son of King Catreus, who fled Crete because an oracle said he would kill his father, settled at Kamiros, and "founded a temple of Zeus who is called Zeus Atabyrius" on the island's highest mountain (Attavyros, 1,215 m, visible from the ramparts on a clear day looking south-west). His father came looking for him and "disembarked by night upon the land of Rhodes with a few followers, and when there arose a hand-to-hand conflict between them and the natives, Althaemenes, rushing out to aid them, hurled his spear, and struck in ignorance his father and killed him" (Diodorus 5.59.3, Oldfather). The pirates and the beach are not in Diodorus: they belong to Apollodorus 3.2.2, where the cowherds chase the landing party "who imagined that they were pirates on a raid" and Althaemenes kills Catreus "with the cast of a javelin, not knowing him to be Catreus". Cite the pirates to Apollodorus or leave them out. Althaemenes then "shunned all meetings and association with mankind... until the grief put an end to his life; and at a later time he received at the hands of the Rhodians, as a certain oracle had commanded, the honours which are accorded to heroes." The scholiast on Pindar says the sanctuary of Zeus Atabyrios held bronze bulls that bellowed when disaster approached, an alarm system in the form of a cult object. Note for the scriptwriter: the Cretan founder at Kamiros, the Argive-Heraclid founder Tlepolemus, the Egyptian Danaus at Lindos, the Phoenician Cadmus, the Thessalian Phorbas. Every foundation story on Rhodes is an immigrant story, which is the anthropological fact under the mythology: a port island writing its cosmopolitanism into its origins.

## Tell at stop 11 (walls walk) and at the B-side stadium on Monte Smith: Tlepolemus, the games, and Helen on a tree

Homer's Catalogue (Iliad 2.653-670): Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, "famed for his spear," brought nine ships from Rhodes, whose people lived "ordered in three parts," Lindos, Ialysos and Kamiros, and on whom Zeus "poured wondrous wealth." The three-city structure that the synoecism later federated is thus already in Homer, which is why Rhodians could treat the Iliad as their founding constitution. Pindar gives the crime and the punishment: Tlepolemus killed Licymnius, his father's aged uncle, "with a staff of hard olive-wood," and Apollo's oracle at Delphi sent him to "the pasture land with sea all around it." Then, lines 76-80: "a sweet recompense for his pitiful misfortune is established for Tlepolemus, the first leader of the Tirynthians, as for a god: a procession of flocks for burnt sacrifice and the trial of contests." That is a hero-cult in one sentence: procession, sacrifice, athletic games (the Tlepolemeia; Pindar adds that Diagoras himself was twice crowned there, and the scholia say Tlepolemus's comrades brought his bones back from Troy and that he had a shrine and tomb in Rhodes, with a funeral contest in which boys' age-classes competed for crowns of white poplar). The 210 m stadium on Monte Smith is 3rd century BC and cannot be tied to the Tlepolemeia by any source (verify-deltas), so say "games like the ones Pindar describes."

Pausanias (3.19.9-10) gives the cult's dark twin. Tlepolemus's widow Polyxo, "an Argive by descent," was queen of the island after his death at Troy, "having been left with an orphan boy." When Helen, driven out of Sparta after Menelaus died, came to Rhodes, Polyxo sent against her as she bathed "handmaidens dressed up as Furies, who seized Helen and hanged her on a tree, and for this reason the Rhodians have a sanctuary of Helen of the Tree" (Helene Dendritis). (The funeral games with poplar wreaths that popular summaries credit to Polyxo are not in Pausanias; they are the Pindar scholia's funeral contest for Tlepolemus, above.) Scholars read Polyxo as a story invented to explain how a Spartan tree-cult of Helen arrived on Rhodes; for Jamie it is a case study in how cult precedes myth, not the other way round: the sanctuary existed, and the story was built to justify it.

## Tell at stop 5 (Marine Gate) or stop 4 (Castellania): kinship as foreign policy

Greek cities did diplomacy through syngeneia, claimed blood kinship traced through myth. Rhodes had three usable lines.

**Argos.** Tlepolemus was born in Argos/Tiryns; his widow Polyxo was Argive. An Argive decree of the late 4th or early 3rd century BC (Moretti ISE 1.40; attalus.org inscription 225) honours the Rhodian people with a gold crown of 100 gold pieces because the Rhodians, "as kinsmen of the Argives," had remained friends and had lent Argos 100 talents for wall repairs and cavalry; the envoys Philias, Eirenidas and Athanodoros are named, and the crown was to be proclaimed at the Hekatomboia and the Nemean games and at the Rhodian Dionysia. A myth about a Bronze Age manslaughter underwrote a Hellenistic loan.

**Gela.** In about 688 BC Antiphemus of Lindos and Entimus of Crete founded Gela in Sicily (Thucydides 6.4 gives the founders and dates it 45 years after Syracuse; Diodorus and the Wikipedia summary agree); the colony's acropolis temple was Athena Lindia, and Gela in turn founded Akragas (Agrigento) "near one hundred and eight years" later, c. 582-580 BC. The Athena Lindia cult was exported as the colonists' identity card.

**Rome.** Here the interesting fact is the absence of a treaty. In 167 BC, after Rhodes had tried to mediate the war against Perseus and the Senate was considering war on Rhodes, the ambassador Astymedes made a speech that Polybius thought servile and Livy thought contrite. Livy 45.25: the Rhodians "had for all these years maintained their friendship in such a way as not to bind themselves" by formal alliance, and the Senate's reply was "couched in such form that they were neither declared enemies nor continued to be allies"; the Perseus edition's note says Polybius put the friendship at 140 years. Rhodes had been the Greek world's neutral broker on a basis of friendship, not kinship; when it finally asked Rome for a formal alliance it was because friendship no longer protected it. The Argive and Roman cases together are a compact lesson in how a small sovereign state used, and could not use, myth.

Also here, for the "how people organised themselves" thread: below the citizen body Hellenistic Rhodes had demes and tribes (the legacy of the synoecism), older land-units called ktoinai, and about 200 known private associations (koina), an unusually large number for any Greek city. Thomsen's 2020 study shows they were named after gods (the inscriptions give Haliastai for Helios and Paniastai for Pan), after founders (the "Matioi ktoinetai eranistai Philokrateioi," after the wealthy Philokrates of Ilion; the Timapoleion koinon), or after clans (the Euthalidai, with branches in Netteia and in town). They gave foreigners, who could not own land or be buried as citizens, access to property and burial plots; one foreigners' association "acted as a thirteenth deme of Lindos"; and Rhodian grandees competed to found them, one man giving his name to twenty-three. The BMCR review of Zachhuber (2024) sums up her view of them as "extensions of public cult, offering inclusive spaces for foreigners and marginal groups." Jamie will recognise the modern Greek syllogos (village association, parish committee, the "Ippites Palaias Poleos" who co-run the Fanourios festival) as the same social form.

## Tell at stop 12 (Koskinou Gate), with a five-minute detour to Agios Fanourios (36.44153, 28.22597): the saint of lost things

Research/17 has the church, the icon-finding legend (14th-century Metropolitan Neilos version versus the popular c. 1500 version) and the cake. What to add for Jamie is the practice as anthropology.

**The vow.** Fanourios's name is heard as phanerono, "I reveal"; he is "the Newly Revealed" and is asked to reveal what is lost, an object, a job, a spouse, a diagnosis. The vow is overwhelmingly a women's practice: on and around 27 August "mostly women are seen entering the place of worship with trays of cakes, sometimes with candles, and holding small pieces of paper... bearing lists of names of beloved ones" (To Vima). The cake is lenten (oil, no eggs or butter), made with an odd number of ingredients (seven "as many as the sacraments," nine, or eleven, by region), taken to church, blessed by the priest, and shared with at least nine people; the wish said over it is that God forgive the saint's sinful mother, a folk element the Church does not formally hold. The whole transaction, promise to the saint, offering brought to the sanctuary, food shared out, is a Greek votive sacrifice with the fire removed, which is a nice echo of Lindos.

**The panigyri.** The Old Town church runs a three-night festival, 25-27 August 2026, from 21:00 in the square behind the church (Agiou Fanouriou 84), co-organised with the "Ippites Palaias Poleos" association: vespers and liturgy inside, music and dancing outside. The word panigyri is the ancient panegyris, the assembly of a whole community at a sanctuary for procession, sacrifice, market and games; the Halieia and the Tlepolemeia were panegyreis. The scriptwriter can say without exaggeration that the festival form survived the change of gods.

**Tamata.** Look on the icon stands inside for small stamped metal plaques hung on ribbons: an eye, a leg, a heart, a baby, a ship, a house, a pair of wedding crowns. These are tamata (from tazo, "I vow"): you buy the image of the thing you need at the church shop, hang it on the saint, and redeem the promise when the request is granted. The ancient temples had exactly this, terracotta and marble anatomical votives (eyes, breasts, feet, wombs), and the Lindian Chronicle's list of gifts is a tama list on a royal scale: Datis's torque and armlets after the rain.

**The evil eye.** Vaskania or mati is not a folk survival the Church ignores; the Orthodox prayer book contains a priest's prayer against vaskania, and the fathers (Chrysostom, Basil) redefined the eye as envy rather than a ray. The home cure, xematiasma, is done by a lay healer (usually a woman) who drops olive oil into water: if it sinks, the eye is on you, and a secret prayer, passed only between relatives of opposite sex, lifts it. Plutarch already discussed the eye's "deadly rays." Ovid's Telchines (research/04) had the evil eye too. Blue glass eyes on sale in Sokratous street are the same technology.

**Name days.** In Greece the name day, the feast of the saint you were named for, historically outranked the birthday; it is an open house at which the celebrant feeds the visitors rather than being fussed over, and children are named for grandparents so the same saints recur through a family every second generation. Someone named for a pagan hero (Achilleas, Odysseas) "does not have" a name day and may borrow All Saints'. Rhodes has names that exist nowhere else because they are vows: Fanourios, and Tsambikos/Tsambika (see stop 16). Rob's 30 September birthday can be framed against this: a birthday is the Western, individual calendar; the name day is the communal one.

## Tell at stop 16 (Roloi, mosque, library): the Panagia's year, and a Pasha's wife at a Christian shrine

Under the Ottomans the Old Town's churches became mesjids (Fanourios's was "Peial el Din"; Panagia tou Kastrou was the "Enderun" mosque), Greeks were moved outside the walls to the marasia suburbs, and the Orthodox calendar carried on there. The Marian feasts became the spine of the rural year: the Annunciation (25 March, also Independence Day), the Dormition (15 August, "the Easter of summer," when emigrants come home to the village panigyri), the Nativity of the Virgin (8 September), the Presentation (21 November). The Panagia, "All-Holy," is the most-invoked figure in Greek religion and the most-dedicated church name; on Rhodes almost every village has one.

Tsambika, on the east coast 25 km south, is the island's Marian pilgrimage and a textbook of vow-practice. The icon was found on the hilltop; the little single-room 14th-century church up 292 steps (the aegeanislands.gr figure; others say about 350) is the goal; the icon itself is kept in the lower monastery by the road for security. Childless women climb the steps barefoot (traditionally on the eve of 8 September, but any time), pray, and if a child follows it is named Tsambikos or Tsambika, "names typical of Rhodes and not found anywhere else." The tradition's founding miracle is that an Ottoman Pasha's wife, unable to conceive, prayed to the Panagia and swallowed the wick of the vigil lamp, and the child was born holding the wick (Greek City Times; a popular account, unverifiable, but it is what pilgrims tell). Two things for Jamie: first, that the story's exemplary pilgrim is a Muslim, which is how a mixed island explained shared shrines; second, that naming a child for the shrine converts a private miracle into a public genealogy, the same operation as the Heliadae naming the three cities.

## Tell at stop 17 (Temple of Aphrodite, Symi Square): the goddess of the harbour and the goddess who was refused

The fenced podium and column drums are a 3rd-century-BC temple, dug by the Italians; the Ministry page (odysseus obj_id 9521) identifies it as Aphrodite's, and its position between the commercial and war harbours fits her aspect as Pontia/Euploia, protector of ships and "goddess of ports." The crouching Aphrodite in the Museum is the island's best-known statue. Diodorus's story of the Telchines' sons who drove Aphrodite off when she tried to land and were struck mad (research/04) reads, at this spot, as a warning about hospitality in a port: the goddess of safe landing is the one you do not refuse. Note the sequence of sea-guardians on this shore: Aphrodite here, Helios on the mole, then St Nicholas of the sailors in the fort at the mole's end (stop 20), then the Evangelismos cathedral of the Virgin on the Mandraki waterfront. Four regimes, one job.

## Tell at stop 18 (Liberty Gate), 19 (deer columns) and 20 (Fort St Nicholas): the modern civic myth

The Italians cut Liberty Gate in 1924 and in 1939 put two columns at the harbour mouth with bronze deer by Renato Brozzi (research/02, from a 2026 Dimokratiki article; the same article denies the guidebook claim that a Roman she-wolf once stood on one column). Three myths are stacked here, all modern in their present form.

**The Colossus straddling the harbour.** A 1394 pilgrim (Niccolò de Martoni, research/02) is the first to write down the local claim that the statue stood with a foot on each mole; no ancient source says so, and modern scholars are unanimous it did not. The deer columns were placed precisely to give that medieval picture two feet to stand on, and every guide still says "the Colossus stood here." That is a civic myth in the strict sense: a story a community tells about a place to give it meaning, tolerated by everyone who knows better.

**The deer and the snakes.** The stag and doe are "Elafos and Elafina," and guides say deer were brought to Rhodes to rid it of snakes, either in antiquity on the advice of an oracle or by the Knights to protect their camps, on the belief that the smell of deer antlers repels snakes. No ancient text says this; what the ancient texts say is that the island was Ophiussa, that Phorbas cleared the snakes (stop 10), and (per a 6th-century-BC tradition cited on rodiaki.gr) that Rhodes was once called Elafousa, "deer island." The Rhodian fallow deer (Dama dama) are real, genetically distinct, and the island's official emblem, so a Hellenistic hero cult (Phorbas) has been quietly transferred to an animal. Present it as "the story locals tell" and let Jamie enjoy the transfer.

**St Nicholas.** The fort at the mole's end (1464-67) is named for the sailors' saint whose chapel stood there before it, the Christian replacement for Aphrodite and Helios as the last thing a departing ship saw. In 1480 the Ottoman assault opened here.

## Tell at stop 22 (Naillac stump, St Paul's Gate) on the walk back: Paul, and the last word

Acts 21:1 has Paul's ship call at Rhodes on the way from Miletus to Patara; tradition (not Acts) lands him at Lindos, where a bay is named for him under the acropolis of Athena Lindia. The scriptwriter can close on that geography: the Christian founder-hero of the island is planted, by tradition, directly below the fireless altar of the Heliadae.

## B-sides

- **Monte Smith (acropolis).** Vedder's candidate for the Helios sanctuary; the stadium and odeion. If the walk ever gets there, tell the Halieia contests and Diagoras's family: Pindar's boxer (Olympic victor 464, plus four Isthmian, two Nemean, one Pythian), whose three sons and two grandsons were all Olympic champions; when two sons carried him round the stadium at Olympia a spectator is said to have shouted "Die, Diagoras, you will not also climb Olympus"; and his daughter Kallipateira (or Pherenike) who entered Olympia disguised as a trainer to watch her son and, when discovered, was acquitted for the family's record (Pausanias 5.6.7-8), after which Olympia ordered trainers to strip too. The Diagorids were a political clan as well as an athletic one (Dorieus, driven out by his political enemies to Thurii, "fought against the Athenians with his own ships" on Sparta's side until the Athenians captured him, Pausanias 6.7.4; Rhodes itself revolted from Athens in the winter of 412/411 "upon the invitation of some of the principal men there," Thucydides 8.44), and the modern airport and the football club are named Diagoras: the hero cult continues by other means.
- **Kallithea Springs.** Nothing mythological is securely attested; the Italian spa (1929) invented its own "ancient healing waters" pedigree. Skip for this track.
- **Dry moat.** No myth material.

## Continuities and contrasts

What an ancient Rhodian, a Hospitaller, an Ottoman-era householder and a resident today would recognise in each other:

- **Naming the year.** Ancient: "in the priesthood of X" (priest of Helios). Knights: regnal year of the Grand Master and the Church's liturgical year. Ottoman: the Hijri year for the state, the Orthodox calendar for the Greek household. Today: the civil year, but the household still runs on the saints' calendar (name days, Dekapentavgoustos). All four would understand a year organised by festivals; only the modern resident would be surprised to be asked who the priest was.
- **Naming a child.** Ancient: grandfathers' names, and vow-names after gods (Heliodoros, "gift of Helios"). Today: grandparents' names, and vow-names after shrines (Tsambika, Fanourios). The Hospitaller, celibate, is the odd one out; the Ottoman householder named for the Prophet's family on the same logic.
- **Getting what you need from the sacred.** Ancient: a vow, an anatomical votive or a gold torque on the goddess's stand, a share of the sacrificed meat. Today: a vow, a tin tama on the icon, a share of the blessed cake. The Knights hung captured banners and ex-votos in St John's; Ottoman Rhodes had its own shrine-visiting and its Pasha's wife at Tsambika. The grammar (promise, gift, communal meal) is identical across all four.
- **Protection from envy.** The Telchines' eye (Ovid), Plutarch's rays, the Church's prayer against vaskania, the blue bead in the shop. Nobody in this line-up would find the evil eye strange; the Ottoman householder's nazar boncugu is the same blue bead.
- **Assembly.** Panegyris at the sanctuary with games and a market; the Order's chapter-general and feast of St John; the Ottoman mahalle's mosque festival; the parish panigyri behind Agios Fanourios with a band at 21:00. Same shape, same function: the community counting itself.
- **Sea-safety.** Aphrodite Pontia, then Helios on the mole, then St Nicholas in the fort, then the Evangelismos on the quay. The prayer changes; the location does not.
- **The founding story as passport.** The ancient Rhodian used Tlepolemus to get a loan from Argos; the Knights used St John and the Pope; Ottoman Rhodes used the Sultan's firman; the modern resident uses the EU flag beside the Greek one on the Palace. Each is a kinship claim to a larger body.
- **The contrasts.** The ancient state ran the cult (the priest was the magistrate); today the state and the Church are separate bodies and vow-practice is private and largely female. Ancient hero cult was for the exceptional dead; the Christian saint is a stranger from elsewhere (Fanourios is not even certainly a Rhodian). And the ancient Rhodian sacrificed animals and drove a chariot into the sea; the modern Rhodian bakes an eggless cake. The offering got cheaper; the promise did not.

## Uncertain or contested (say so in the script)

- Location of the Helios sanctuary and Colossus: Badoud (Palace hill) vs Vedder (Monte Smith) vs Hoepfner and Zachhuber (acropolis, the "temenos of Apollo Pythios"). See research/01-02 for the Colossus placements.
- Priest of Helios rotating Ialysos, Kamiros, Lindos: stated by Paul (following Badoud); the "chosen by lot" detail is unsourced. Not verified against Badoud's text.
- The Halieia chariot: Festus is a late Roman lexicographer, gives no date, and Iversen doubts the rite was part of the Halieia; the "24th of Gorpiaeus" belongs to the Pindar scholiast's dating of the games. Whether horses were drowned is unclear.
- Order of the Rhodian months and which month opened the year: Wikipedia calls Thesmophorios the first month; Iversen argues the council year ended with Thesmophorios and began with Karneios, and that the priest of Helios's year began with Dalios.
- The Tlepolemeia and the Monte Smith stadium cannot be linked by any source.
- The Tsambika Pasha's-wife miracle and the step count (292 vs 350) are popular tradition.
- The deer-versus-snakes story and "Elafousa" have no ancient literary source I could find; Ophiussa and Phorbas do.
- Fanourios icon date: 14th-century (Neilos) vs c. 1500 versions; see research/17.
- Fanouropita: "nine days of suffering" (To Vima) is one explanation of the nine ingredients; Greek Wikipedia gives seven "as the sacraments."
- The gold-letter copy of Olympian 7 at Lindos rests on scholia citing Gorgon.

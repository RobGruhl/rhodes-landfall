## Frame for the scriptwriter

Between the monuments Jamie will walk perhaps two kilometres of residential lane, and everything in those lanes is governed by somebody. The cat asleep on the step is, since 2021, the legal responsibility of the Municipality of Rhodes (Law 4830/2021, article 10), was very likely neutered on a German charity's money at a clinic in the suburbs, and is fed by a neighbour the municipality calls a *philozoos* and asks to register the animal's location by telephone. The basil in the oil tin was blessed in church on 14 September, sixteen days before the walk, and is the plant said to have grown where Helena found the Cross. The shop sign, the awning, the air-conditioner and the satellite dish on the wall above it are all chapters of a 2015 "Appearance Regulation" enforced by a municipal Directorate of the Medieval City that sits at Ippoton 4, and any change to the fabric itself needs the Ministry of Culture, because the whole 65.85 hectares has been a designated monument since 1960 (a date that rests on one source; see section 3). The lane was swept this morning by one man with a hand-cart who works seven days a week, and the bins were emptied four times in the last twenty-four hours. The chairs outside the door belong to the oldest institution in the street, the *geitonia*, which anthropologists treat as a social unit in its own right. Jamie's brief, how people organise their lives, is answered here at the smallest scale: a lane is a commons with a written constitution (Ministry, municipality, police regulation, animal law) and an unwritten one (who feeds the cats, who keeps the lamp lit, who may sit on whose chair), and the two constitutions are visibly negotiating on every wall. Observation and sourced fact are kept apart below; the ten-item checklist at the end is observation unless a source is named.

## 1. Cats (tell at stops 2 and 3; return to it at 13 to 15 and 17)

**The law.** Greek Law 4830/2021 ("Νέο πλαίσιο για την ευζωία των ζώων συντροφιάς", the "Argos" law) makes the municipality the responsible party for stray companion animals: article 10 obliges it to collect, sterilise, microchip and register strays in the National Companion Animal Registry (ΕΜΖΣ, article 4), to set up feeding stations, and to return sterilised animals to the place they were found; article 9 obliges owners to sterilise their dogs and cats or lodge a DNA sample; articles 34 and 35 carry the criminal and administrative penalties (taxheaven.gr summary of the law; the Gazette text itself, ΦΕΚ Α' 169/18.8.2021, was on a host that refused connections, so the article numbers rest on the taxheaven digest). The municipality's own notices define a stray, following the law, as "an animal without a proprietor", and stress that the free sterilisation "concerns exclusively stray companion animals; domestic pets are never included" (rhodes.gr, 18 Sep 2023 and 2 Apr 2026). The €30,000 administrative fine for killing an animal is real: in September 2024 a sixty-year-old man who shot a kitten with a rifle on Rhodes faced a criminal charge plus that fine, the case reported by the island's animal-welfare society, which said "we were confronted with yet another homicide" and that this "was not an isolated incident" (Dimokratiki, 25 Sep 2024).

**The Rhodes programme.** The machinery is run by the municipality's Primary Sector Directorate (Διεύθυνση Πρωτογενούς Τομέα). In September 2023 it announced an island-wide sterilisation and microchipping contract under the Interior Ministry's "Philodemos II" programme, split into three zones (north: Rhodes town and Ialysos; east; west), with this procedure: a volunteer telephones a named municipal employee (Anna Chrysanthaki, 2241361639), registers the stray and its location, a municipal vet books the surgery, and the volunteer carries the animal to the contracted clinic and back "to its natural environment" ("πίσω στο φυσικό περιβάλλον τους"; the notice does not say "to the point where it was found", and it gives a second number, 2241361653) (rhodes.gr, 18 Sep 2023). That is the neighbourhood "cat woman" written into an administrative circular: the state supplies the surgery, the *philozoos* supplies the cat, the transport and the follow-up feeding. The municipality has also installed feeders and water bowls "at specific points of the island" (rhodes.gr, 13 Nov 2020, which names the municipal vet Aikaterini Veloni and Georgia Dimeli, then president of the Rhodes Philozoic Society, but gives no count and no locations); bought two equipped vans; contracted private vets for injured strays; and renovated the municipal shelter (Kynokomeio) (rhodes.gr, 4 Oct 2021 and 5 Apr 2023). None of the municipal notices gives a figure for the stray population of the town or of the Old Town; say "hundreds" from observation, not a number.

**The German charity.** The cats Jamie photographs were, statistically, neutered by **Flying Cats e.V.** of Hannover, which "since 2012" has run an annual free sterilisation campaign on Rhodes; at the October 2021 press conference with the municipality it reported "15,000 cats" sterilised since the programme began, working out of "Ms Tomazou's clinic" (Dimokratiki, 30 Oct 2021). The same month it agreed to donate "30 small houses" for the town's street cats and offered coupons of "€50 for females and €30 for males" so that poorer residents could sterilise their own animals (Dimokratiki, 21 Oct 2021). By May 2023 the municipality was thanking it for its "eleventh consecutive year" and naming the vet Antonia-Michaelitsa Chatzidiakou (Dimokratiki, 18 May 2023). The 2026 campaign ran from 16 April to 3 May: residents "caring for stray cats" were told to book by Facebook message to the charity's representative Claudia Losse and bring the animals to the reception point in the Agioi Apostoloi district beside the KEP citizens' office; the notice is signed by Nikolaos Chatzikalymeris, the councillor delegated for "stray animal welfare and libraries", which is itself a small fact about municipal priorities (rhodes.gr, 2 Apr 2026). The charity describes its Rhodes work as "regularly organizing, financing and implementing castration campaigns together with the veterinarians' pool e.V." (flying-cats.de). So the cat on the doorstep at stop 3 is a joint product of Greek law, a Hannover bank account, a volunteer with a carrier and a neighbour with a tin of food.

**The societies.** Three names are documented. The **Philozoic Organisation of Rhodes (Φιλοζωική Οργάνωση Ρόδου)**, founded 1990, president Stefania Papakonstantinou, which rescues, sterilises, adopts out, teaches in schools and gives legal support to volunteers; she told Dimokratiki in September 2024 that "our goal is animal welfare in all its forms" ("Σκοπός μας είναι η ευζωία των ζώων σε όλες τις μορφές") and that although Law 4830/2021 makes feeding the municipality's duty, the municipality has not funded it, so the society pays for the food itself; she also estimated that about ninety per cent of Greece's strays (she was speaking of the country, not of Rhodes in particular) are hunting and shepherd dogs abandoned when no longer useful, a reminder that the cat problem and the dog problem are different problems (Dimokratiki, 29 Sep 2024). Her other line is worth Jamie's ear: "Violence escalates, and when it is directed at animals it should worry everyone" ("Η βία είναι κλιμακούμενη και όταν ασκείται ενάντια στα ζώα θα έπρεπε να ανησυχεί όλους"). Second, the **Rescue and Care Society for Stray Animals of Rhodes "United for Animals"**, international name "Animal Rescue Rhodes Paws & Claws", whose founding by twenty-three people was reported on 8 August 2013 with a statute admitting members "regardless of sex and nationality" ("ανεξαρτήτως φύλου και υπηκοότητας"; the article lists the founders' names but does not describe their nationalities, so the notion that foreign residents form a large part of the informal welfare corps is my inference, not the paper's) (Dimokratiki, 8 Aug 2013). Third, Flying Cats itself. I found no quoted Old Town shopkeeper or resident giving a view on the cats, for or against; the residents' complaints on record (section 3) are about noise, parking, touts and rubbish, and cats do not appear in them. Do not invent a grumbling taverna owner. What can be said is that at the municipality's 2021 press conference the municipal vet Veloni framed cats as useful, "it is important that there are stray cats, because cities without cats have many mice" ("Είναι σημαντικό να υπάρχουν αδέσποτες γάτες, διότι πόλεις που δεν έχουν γάτες, έχουν πολλά ποντίκια"), but said "their population must grow in a controlled way" ("ο πληθυσμός τους πρέπει να αναπτύσσεται ελεγχόμενα") (Dimokratiki, 30 Oct 2021).

**Cats as tourist economy.** Observation only: cat calendars, cat postcards and cat fridge-magnets are sold on Sokratous, and cat photographs are a standard cruise-passenger output. No source quantifies it; say so or leave it out.

**Long views.** An ancient Rhodian would hardly have known the house cat: "housecats seem to have been extremely rare among the ancient Greeks and Romans", "weasels, not cats, were seen as the ideal rodent-killers", the Greek word *ailouros* means "thing with the waving tail", and domestic cats "were probably first introduced to Greece and southern Italy in the fifth century BC by the Phoenicians" (Wikipedia, Cultural depictions of cats). The Latin Church the Knights belonged to is often accused of persecuting cats after Gregory IX's decretal *Vox in Rama* (1233), which describes heretics kissing a black cat; but "the text of Vox in Rama does not claim that all cats are Satanic or contain any orders to kill cats", and contemporary rules "permitted consecrated women and anchorites to own pet cats" (Wikipedia, Vox in Rama). The Order's own statutes cared about horses, not cats: the chapters-general of 1311 and 1314 resolved "to maintain five hundred horse and a thousand foot" (Luttrell, in jamie-05), and the brethren were forbidden animal fur on their clothes (jamie-05, cl. 8). The Ottoman householder is the one who would recognise today's lane: in Islam "the domestic cat is regarded as ritually clean and thus holds a unique status in comparison to other companion animals, such as the domestic dog"; cats are held to carry *barakah*; Sultan Baybars endowed a cat garden in thirteenth-century Cairo, and the popular story of Muhammad's cat Muezza, though "no such pet cat is mentioned as belonging to Muhammad in any verified hadith", is what every Turkish grandmother tells (Wikipedia, Islam and cats). Ottoman Istanbul, a city of wooden houses and therefore of mice, had a paid profession, the *mancacı* or cat-feeder, and today keeps an estimated 125,000 street cats with council vets offering free neutering and coin-operated food dispensers (Wikipedia, Feral cats in Istanbul). The dog, by contrast, was tolerated on the street but not indoors, and in 1910 (Turkish Wikipedia's Sivriada page; the English Sivriada page says 1911) the city prefect of Istanbul, Suphi Bey, exiled the city's strays to the island of Sivriada, where "about 80,000 dogs" died; the earthquake that followed was read by the people as "a punishment by God for abandoning the dogs" and the island was renamed Hayırsızada, "the inauspicious island" (Wikipedia, Sivriada). I found no Orthodox text on cats either way; the Church's silence is itself the finding. English Wikipedia's line that Aegean cats are "considered a national treasure" in Greece rests on a single Animal Planet "Cats 101" episode and should not be repeated.

## 2. The household on the street (tell at stops 2 and 3, then 13 to 15)

**The geitonia.** The classic Greek ethnography of the urban lane is Renée Hirschon's *Heirs of the Greek Catastrophe* (1989, fieldwork 1972) on the refugee quarter of Yerania in Kokkinia, Piraeus, which treats "the neighbourhood as a social unit", describes houses subdivided so that each daughter could be given a dwelling as dowry, and finds the street rather than the house to be the arena of women's sociability (Berghahn Books description of the 3rd edition, 2023). What Jamie sees in the Juderia, a single-room house per daughter (jamie-09's *aegeanislands.gr* line on fragmentation "into small, single-room houses"), is the Dodecanesian form of the same rule. Observation: the plastic chairs outside a door are not furniture left out; they are the household's claim on the lane, and an evening seat is assigned by custom to the woman of the house and her visitors. That claim is the thing the 2015 Appearance Regulation regulates when it lists "τραπεζοκαθίσματα" (tables-and-chairs) among its chapters, though the regulation is aimed at shops, not doorsteps (Dimokratiki, 24 Apr 2015).

**Who sweeps.** Since the "#Keep_Clean" campaign of 30 August to 31 October 2021 the municipality has kept "one street-sweeper exclusively in the Medieval City with a cleaning hand-cart, Monday to Sunday", supported by extra sweepers and a private contractor; two areas are pressure-washed daily; rubbish and bulky items are removed "at least four times in the twenty-four hours"; and "35 new green bins" were placed in the Old Town's neighbourhoods, with blue recycling bins "where appropriate". The deputy mayor Stefanos Drakos said the Medieval City "had not seen a street-sweeper's hand-cart in daily operation until now" (rhodes.gr, 14 Sep 2021). The notice does not say where the bins stand; observation is that most are pushed against walls near the gates and squares rather than in the narrowest lanes, which is consistent with the resident's own habit of carrying a bag to the gate. The whitewashed step-edge and the lime wash around the door are the older sanitation: whitewash is slaked lime, "mildly antibacterial", which is why it was used in dairies; Wikipedia says the whitewash on fruit-tree trunks is against sun scald, not germs, so the antibacterial reading of a Greek doorstep is inference (Wikipedia, Whitewash; the Greek island habit itself is observation).

**The oil-tin garden.** Observation: basil, geraniums, a lemon seedling and mint in five-litre olive-oil tins, the tin being the free container of an olive-oil economy. What is sourced is basil's meaning. The Greek name is *vasilikos*, from *basileus*, "king", the "royal plant" (Wikipedia, Basil: "βασιλικόν φυτόν"). "It is believed the herb was found growing at the original cross of Christ when it was discovered by the Empress Helena" (Greek Wikipedia, Βασιλικός: "Πιστεύεται ότι το βότανο βρέθηκε να αναπτύσσεται στον πρωτότυπο σταυρό του Χριστού, όταν ανακαλύφθηκε από την Αυτοκράτειρα Ελένη"); OrthodoxWiki's version: "On the spot where the Cross was discovered, St. Helen had found a hitherto unknown flower of rare beauty and fragrance, which has been named 'Vasiliko'... the plant Vasiliko, Basil, is tied to the Precious Cross of the King of Glory". On 14 September, the Exaltation of the Cross, "the priest, having prepared a tray with the cross placed on a bed of fresh basil leaves or flowers" brings it out for veneration (Wikipedia, Feast of the Cross), and "at the feast of the Exaltation of the Precious Cross on 14 September, basil is distributed in the churches" (Greek Wikipedia: "Στη γιορτή της Υψώσεως του Τίμιου Σταυρού στις 14 Σεπτεμβρίου, στις εκκλησίες, μοιράζεται βασιλικός"). The day is "always a fast day and the eating of meat, dairy products and fish is prohibited" (Wikipedia, Feast of the Cross). At Theophany "the priest sprinkles the faithful with holy water, dipping a sprig of basil in the agiasmos" (Greek Wikipedia), and "basil has religious significance in the Greek Orthodox Church, where it is used to sprinkle holy water"; some Greeks will not eat it for that reason (Wikipedia, Basil). Sixteen days before Jamie's walk every Orthodox household in the Old Town brought a sprig home from church; where it went is the next paragraph.

**The icon shelf and the wall shrine.** The Greek word for both the domestic icon shelf and the roadside shrine is *eikonostasi* (Greek Wikipedia disambiguation: "a small structure in houses where icons are placed" and "a small independent structure placed at the roadside"). Inside the house the icon corner faces east, ideally "visible when one first enters the house from the main entrance", with an oil lamp before it whose "careful trimming ... to keep it burning at all times is interpreted as symbolic of the attentive daily care faithful Christians should take over their souls"; it holds "incense, holy water, palms and pussywillow from Palm Sunday, candles from Pascha, and other sacred items" (Wikipedia, Icon corner). The 14 September basil goes there, beside the wedding crowns (observation; the crowns are general Greek practice, not in the fetched article). Outside, the small shrines are *kandylakia*, "commonly built in the memory of a fatal car accident" with a photograph and the victim's name-saint, or "from car accident survivors thanking the saint who protected them" (Wikipedia, Wayside shrine). A lit lamp in a wall niche in the Old Town is therefore one of three things: a vow kept, a death remembered, or the household's own icon corner turned outward; which, you can only learn by asking. Who keeps it lit is not documented anywhere I reached; observation and general Greek practice say the nearest woman of the house, on Saturday evening and on the eve of feasts, with olive oil from the kitchen. Flag it as observation.

**The door.** The hand-shaped knocker is the *hamsa*, "believed to provide defense against the evil eye", known to Muslims as the hand of Fatima, to Sephardi Jews as the hand of Miriam and to Levantine Christians as the hand of Mary; it is older than all three (a Punic stela at Carthage shows the open hand dedicated to Tanit and Baal Hammon), and it was "painted in red ... on the walls of houses for protection" or "hung on the doorways of rooms, such as those of an expectant mother or new baby" (Wikipedia, Hamsa). In a quarter that was Jewish until 1944 and Turkish before that, a hand on a door is the one object every former population would read the same way. The blue bead nailed to the jamb is jamie-04's *mati*; the blue paint on shutters is observation and the "blue against the eye" explanation is folk-etymology not confirmed by any source here. House numbers and the blue-and-white enamel street plates are the Greek state's (post-1947) layer over the Italian and Ottoman street names, but I found no source on the renaming and jamie-08 does not cover it; treat as an open question.

**Bread for the poor and food for the dead.** The Old Town has a five-century record of food left out. The Hospitaller nun Margarita of Negroponte kept an oven in the 1340s "to bake bread to be given to the poor as alms" (Coureas, via jamie-05); at the Murad Reis tomb pilgrims slaughtered animals by the grave and gave the meat to the poor (jamie-10); the kollyva of the memorial service is eaten by strangers in the church porch (jamie-10). Observation: a plate of food on a step is usually for the cats, a plastic bag hung on a door-handle is usually a neighbour's gift of fruit or bread, and a sweet left by a wall shrine on a Saturday is for the dead. Do not script the third without a source; it is common Greek practice but not documented for Rhodes here.

**The ancient courtyard, for contrast at stop 17.** Greek city houses "were inward-facing, with major openings looking onto the central courtyard, rather than the street", built "with adjoining walls and ... divided into small blocks by narrow streets", with "shops ... sometimes located in the rooms towards the street", walls of "sun-dried clay bricks ... on a base of stone" (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek architecture). At Kameiros "the main settlement was on the middle terrace, consisting of a grid of parallel streets and residential blocks", supplied from "a covered reservoir having a capacity of 600 cubic meters of water, enough for up to 400 families" (Wikipedia, Kameiros). The household's religion lived in the court: Hestia "was often the recipient of the first offering during sacrifices, and of the first and last libations made during feasts" (Wikipedia, Hestia), and in Athens a man standing for office was asked "who has a Family Apollo and Homestead Zeus, and where are these shrines?" and "whether he has family tombs and where they are", whether he "treats his parents well", "pays his taxes" and "has done his military service" (Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 55.3, Perseus). The courtyard altar was, in other words, a citizenship document. The Old Town's icon corner and wall lamp are what that altar became.

## 3. The rules (tell at stop 2 on entry; the shop rules at 15 and the Sokratous end of 16; the residents' voice at 12 and 14)

**The status.** "In 1960 the old city of Rhodes was officially designated as a monument of cultural heritage by the Greek Ministry of Culture, and restoration works began under the auspices of the Ministry"; UNESCO inscription followed in 1988, and Rhodes has belonged to the Organization of World Heritage Cities "since it was founded in 1993" (Greek News Agenda). Only 1988 and the OWHC's 1993 founding at Fez are independently confirmed (Wikipedia; the OWHC's own Rhodes page, which lists Rhodes as a member); the 1960 date and "since it was founded" were found in no other source reached, so say "listed nationally in 1960" only with Greek News Agenda as the named source. The Ephorate of Antiquities of the Dodecanese sits inside the walls at Ippoton street (efadod.gr; jamie-09). The general rule of Greek archaeological law, that any work on or near a listed monument needs the Ministry's approval, is Law 3028/2002 article 10 (codified as Law 4858/2021); I could not fetch the article text this session from any host, so cite it as the standard rule and not as a quotation. In practice a resident's new door, roof, window, plaster colour or shop conversion goes to the Ephorate, and the Ephorate's own notice on a scaffold ("Εφορεία Αρχαιοτήτων Δωδεκανήσου", with a project title and EU or Recovery Fund logos) is the visible sign of that approval; the Ministry's 2022 to 2026 strategy for its 365 buildings and the €63.6 m programme contract are in jamie-09.

**The Appearance Regulation.** The municipality's Directorate of the Medieval City and Monuments (Διεύθυνση Μεσαιωνικής Πόλης & Μνημείων, Ippoton 4) enforces a "Κανονισμός Εμφάνισης της Μεσαιωνικής Πόλης Ρόδου" whose chapters are, in the Directorate's own list, "signs, display windows, shutters, colour schemes, shading, external lighting, air-conditioners, solar water-heaters, antennas, tables-and-chairs, umbrellas, price-lists, merchandise, partition elements, noise pollution, cleanliness" ("επιγραφές, προθήκες, εξώφυλλα, χρωματισμούς, σκίαστρα, εξωτερικό φωτισμό, κλιματιστικά, ηλιακούς θερμοσίφωνες, κεραίες, τραπεζοκαθίσματα, ομπρέλες, τιμοκατάλογους, εμπορεύματα, διαχωριστικά στοιχεία, ηχορύπανση, καθαριότητα"). Its headline rules: signs limited in "number (one per street) and quality (not oversized and illuminated)" ("μία ανά οδό... όχι υπερμεγέθεις και φωτεινές"; the phrase "one per street" presumably means one per street-frontage, but that is my gloss), goods displayed outside a shop "to a maximum width of 0.70 and maximum height of 1.70" metres, and a ban on "the touting of customers which internationally defames our tourist product" ("την άγρα πελατών που δυσφημεί διεθνώς το τουριστικό μας προϊόν"). The full text "is available in printed form at the Directorate's offices", which is why nobody online can quote it; no decision number, Gazette or penalty schedule was given (Dimokratiki, 24 Apr 2015). Laundry lines are not in the chapter list; I found no rule on washing across the lane, and the washing Jamie sees is evidence that none is enforced. So: satellite dish, air-conditioner and sign are regulated; the sheet on the line is not.

**Traffic, noise, rubbish.** The resident's driving permit (decision 140/2025, in force 14 April 2026), the 06:00 to 09:00 delivery window, the Symi Square closure and the MyStreet reporting app are in jamie-09, as are the legally quiet hours (Police Regulation 3/1996: 15:00 to 17:30 and 23:00 to 07:00 until 30 September, the day of the walk). Rubbish is section 2 above. What the 2025 council debate added is the residents' side: the "Medieval City Residents' Association" (the article's name for it; I could not confirm the registered title) supported the traffic scheme in principle but asked for postponement, wanted "15 exclusive parking spaces" at Symi Square and "15 to 20" near Platanika, insisted on access for ambulances, fire engines, deliveries, rubbish collection and "elderly and mobility-impaired residents", warned that "without proper and systematic policing and protection nothing can be implemented", and said that over-restriction plus tourism growth would turn the place into "a tourist theme park rather than a living cultural monument"; the mayor Alexandros Koliadis answered that "the Medieval City has been greatly wronged in recent years" and that comparable European towns put residents first (Dimokratiki, 7 May 2025).

**Earlier voices.** In May 2013 the shopkeepers' and business bodies met under the headline "The situation in the Medieval City cannot be borne any longer": the Association of Shop Owners of the Medieval City (president Andreas Panagiotopoulos; treasurer D. Foitos) and the hoteliers, chamber and commercial associations complained of bars playing music until morning, of "over 15,000 parking citations a year" ("our monument is one big anarchic car park", "Το μνημείο μας είναι ένα μεγάλο άναρχο πάρκινγκ"), of 380 citations for illegal micro-sellers in the previous year, and of touts: "in the Square of the Jewish Martyrs we have the absolute rodeo with the touts" ("Στην πλατεία Εβραίων Μαρτύρων έχουμε το απόλυτο ροντέο με τους κράχτες"); they demanded that "not only will the touts be arrested but the shop owners will be prosecuted" and that the ten worst premises be closed (Dimokratiki, 17 May 2013). That is stop 14 seen from a shop door. In February 2019 the Environmental Protection Association of the Island of Rhodes (Σύλλογος Προστασίας Περιβάλλοντος Νήσου Ρόδου, "Σ.Π.Π.") published the conclusions of a public evening on the Old Town: the daily problems, "cleanliness, noise pollution, occupation of common spaces, access to residences, shop hours, permit violations, customer touting", could be solved "by simple application of existing legislation"; the walls need vegetation cleared; dangerous derelict buildings exist; "depopulation as permanent residents leave" is under way; and "the Medieval City will never be what it was if the permanent residents and the healthy business people are removed" ("αν απομακρυνθούν οι μόνιμοι κάτοικοι και οι υγιείς επιχειρηματίες"), so a "credible and functional control body" with all stakeholders is needed (Dimokratiki, 5 Feb 2019). Cats do not appear in any of these lists. The Ippites Palaias Poleos, the residents' cultural association that co-runs the Fanourios festival, is in jamie-04; I found no statement by it on living conditions.

## 4. The moat and the gardens as the town's open space (B-side; tell at stop 12 looking down, and at stop 11 if the walls walk is open)

The land walls "were protected by a dry moat 30 meters wide" (kastra.eu; the "70 m" and "2,300 m" figures in circulation are unverified, verify-deltas). The three masonry islands in it are the terrepleins of Spain, England and Italy, built after 1480 so that "the cannon could [not] have a free range on the walls" and reached "through underground passages" (kastra.eu; research/20 has the tunnels); the Italian terreplein was "partially demolished in the 1930s". The green belt is Italian: "during the Italian rule ... they also formed gardens and groves in the perimeter, in places where, according to historical testimonies, there were orchards during both the Knighthood and the Ottoman Empire", and the fortifications now sit inside "a wide protection zone ... which consists of the archaeological site of the moat and the well-designed perimeter green areas, in the centre of which operates the tourist spectacle 'Sound and Light'" (kastra.eu). The Ottoman cemeteries against the walls that Tozer saw in 1874 were cleared for those parks (jamie-06, research/11). The Palace gardens were reopened on 13 June 2026 (research/20), and the 2023 programme contract lists "Palace gardens", "four public squares", "underground utility and lighting networks" and a "review of the regulatory provisions for the Medieval City" among its twelve sub-projects (ERT, 7 Apr 2023), so the rulebook of section 3 is itself scheduled for rewriting.

The moat's civic use is the **Theatre of the Medieval Moat "Melina Mercouri"** (Θέατρο Μεσαιωνικής Τάφρου "Μελίνα Μερκούρη"), an open-air amphitheatre at 36.44042, 28.23033, in the moat about 250 m east of the Bastion of St John, between the Gate of St John and the Tower of Italy (verify-deltas-round2, OSM way 283080408). It hosts municipal and private programming alike. The municipality's Culture Department (Διεύθυνση Πολιτισμού) put on "The Swings of Manolis Chiotis" on 25 August 2025 at 21:00, free, and a Mitropanos tribute on 24 September 2025; DOPAR, the municipal culture-and-sport organisation, presented *The Barber of Seville* on 4 and 5 September 2022; Eleftheria Arvanitaki's concert on 30 August 2022 was a private production (Novel Vox, 12ηχος), and the "free admission" premiere of 20 June 2025 was a school play by the private Rodion Paideia schools with the Rodion Odeion, not a municipal event (Dimokratiki, dates as given). No capacity figure was found. Who maintains the moat gardens (municipality's Environment and Green Directorate, or the Ephorate as archaeological site) is not stated in any page reached; the kastra.eu wording, "archaeological site of the moat", implies the Ephorate for the ditch and the municipality for the perimeter parks, but that is inference. Dog-walkers, joggers and the evening volta in the moat are observation (the Tripadvisor page that would have supplied dated quotations returned 403). ## 5. The shop-sign layer and the tout economy (tell at stops 4, 15 and the Sokratous end of 16)

What Jamie sees on Sokratous and Ippokratous is what the 2015 regulation permits plus what it fails to stop. Sourced: one sign per frontage, not oversized, not illuminated; goods racks no wider than 0.70 m and no taller than 1.70 m; touting banned (Dimokratiki, 24 Apr 2015). Observation, to be checked on the day: the signs are far from uniform (painted board, cut metal, vinyl, and hand-lettered in Greek, English, and sometimes Russian, German or Hebrew), the racks routinely exceed 0.70 m, and the replica Knights' armour, the "Colossus" ouzo bottles and the sponges are jamie-11's list. The touts are the one item every source agrees on: the 2013 "rodeo" at the Square of the Jewish Martyrs, the 2015 regulation's "internationally defames our tourist product", the 2019 SPP list, and the Hippocrates Square reviews in verify-deltas (a €20 dropped-glass charge, cash demands). For Jamie the anthropology is that the tout is the modern *kráchtis*, the caller, and that the same street had a market judge at its head under the Knights (the Castellania) and the Ottomans (the kadi at the top of "Arriva del Kadi", jamie-06); the 2015 Directorate at Ippoton 4 is the third market judge to sit within 100 m of the same fountain.

## Ten things to spot in one residential lane (between stops 2 and 3, or 13 and 15)

Observation unless a source is named; each with one sentence of meaning.

1. **A cat with a notched or tipped ear.** Ear-tipping is the international mark of a sterilised-and-returned stray; on Rhodes that almost certainly means Flying Cats and the municipal contract (Dimokratiki 2021-26; the ear-tip convention itself is general practice, not sourced here).
2. **A plastic bowl of dry food by a door, or a wooden cat-house.** The *philozoos* at work; the 30 cat-houses were a 2021 donation (Dimokratiki, 21 Oct 2021).
3. **Basil in an olive-oil tin.** The royal plant of the Cross, blessed and distributed on 14 September (Greek Wikipedia; OrthodoxWiki); the tin is the container of an oil economy.
4. **Two or three plastic chairs against the wall.** The geitonia's seating plan; the women's evening parliament (Hirschon).
5. **A lit lamp in a wall niche.** A vow kept, a death remembered or an icon corner facing the street (Wikipedia, Icon corner and Wayside shrine); ask before you photograph.
6. **A hand-shaped knocker or a blue bead on the jamb.** The hamsa and the mati, apotropaic across all three of the quarter's former populations (Wikipedia, Hamsa; jamie-04).
7. **Whitewashed step-edge and lime around the door.** Lime is mildly antibacterial (Wikipedia, Whitewash); it is also the cheapest paint.
8. **Washing strung across the lane.** Not a chapter of the Appearance Regulation, therefore the one facade element no one polices (Dimokratiki, 24 Apr 2015, by omission).
9. **An air-conditioner boxed in wood, or a satellite dish tucked behind a parapet.** Both are chapters of the 2015 regulation; the boxing is compliance.
10. **A green municipal bin against a wall, and a man with a hand-cart.** One of 35 bins and the one dedicated sweeper, seven days a week, four collections a day (rhodes.gr, 14 Sep 2021).

## Continuities and contrasts

- **An ancient Rhodian** would recognise the inward-facing house on a narrow street with a shop in the front room, the cistern under the court, the shrine at the door and the first spoonful given to the hearth; he would recognise the citizenship question "where is your Zeus Herkeios?" in the resident's driving permit, which is also a question about where your house is. He would not recognise the cat (a Phoenician novelty, a weasel's job) nor a state that neuters it.
- **A Hospitaller** would recognise the rule that the Order, not the householder, decides what a facade may look like, and the alms-oven of Margarita of Negroponte in the plate of food on the step; he would recognise the ban on colour and fur in the Appearance Regulation's chapter on "χρωματισμούς". He would not recognise a municipality answerable to residents, or a hand of Fatima on a Christian door.
- **An Ottoman-era householder** would recognise nearly everything: the ritually clean cat fed at the door, the dog kept out, the lattice that let the women watch the lane, the neighbourhood as the unit of life, the wall lamp and the amulet on the jamb, the caller at the shop door. He would be puzzled by the sheet across the lane (his house faced inward) and by the printed rule that a sign be "one per street".
- **A resident today** holds all four in one hand: a permit from the municipality, a lease from the Ministry, a cat neutered by Hannover, basil from the priest, a chair from her mother, and a complaint about touts that her shopkeeper neighbour made in 2013 and her association repeated in 2025. What she alone knows is the arithmetic that decides whether her daughter's single-room house will be a home or a rental.

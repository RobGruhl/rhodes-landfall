# Walks and viewpoints in and around Rhodes town (walls walk, moat, Monte Smith, Roloi, Fort St Nicholas, Elli/Aquarium, Rodini, Murad Reis) with practical detail for Wed 30 Sep 2026

## The lie of the land

Rhodes town is three walks stacked on top of each other. The medieval city is a walled crescent facing the sea; the Knights' dry moat wraps its landward side; and the Hellenistic acropolis sits on the hill to the south-west, looking down on everything. From Hippocrates Square (36.44362, 28.22823) nothing inside the walls is more than ten minutes away, the harbour mole is fifteen, the beach and aquarium at the island's tip about thirty, and Rodini Park is a genuine 35–40 minute march south.

The one hard constraint of the day is the walls walk. Every source that names days and hours agrees: the gate onto the ramparts opens **Monday to Friday, 12:00–15:00 only**, and is closed at weekends. 30 September 2026 is a Wednesday, so it should be open — but it is a three‑hour window in the flattest light of the day, with almost no shade, so build the birthday around it: moat or acropolis in the cool morning, walls at noon, Roloi and a drink after.

## The Walls Walk (Palace of the Grand Master → Gate of St John)

Buy the ticket at the Palace ticket office (Palace hours in mid-September were 08:00–19:30, shortening every fortnight into October; adult entry reported at €20 — the wall walk is a separate, cheap ticket). A May 2025 reviewer: "Tickets are €5 each and are purchased at the same ticket office as the Grandmaster Palace. Once you have purchased your ticket turn right to some iron gates. These gates are open between 12 noon and 3pm, Monday to Friday." Another source lists €6. The iron gates are on the Palace forecourt by the Cannon Gate; the route runs one‑way, about 1.6–2 km, along the western and southern curtain to the south‑east corner by the Gate of St John (Koskinou), ending in steep concrete steps. The walkway is the *terreplein* top — wide enough, as guides love to say, for two carts to pass — with no railings on the inner side. Allow 45–90 minutes; one April 2026 visitor guessed the odd hours exist "to observe the privacy of those residents" whose gardens you look straight into.

What the scriptwriter should have the walker see: the *langue* system. The Order divided the circuit among its national "tongues" — Grand Master (north, at the Palace), France, then Auvergne (with the Tower of Spain/St Mary sector), Spain, England (the long south stretch between St Athanasios and St John gates), Provence, and Italy at the harbour corner. Each tongue's coat of arms is carved on its stretch. This is a faction map you walk across.

## The Moat (free, always open, ~2.5 km)

Never filled with water. Roughly 30 m wide, walls up to 12 m thick, now a landscaped park with sandy paths, lawns, an open‑air theatre and stone cannonballs lying where they fell in 1480 and 1522. Entrances: at the Mandraki end by the taxi rank near St Paul's Gate / Liberty Gate, and at the far end beside the Akandia Gate; stairways also drop in from the gates of St Anthony and St Athanasios, the latter through a short (~30 m) tunnel under the inner wall. It is lit after dark; there are no toilets or kiosks inside except at the exits.

Walking it clockwise from Mandraki you pass under the twin round towers of the **d'Amboise Gate** (early 1500s, fleur‑de‑lis of Grand Master Emery d'Amboise, drawbridge chains still visible), then reach the **Bastion of St George** — a pentagonal artillery fort built after the 1480 siege with the help of engineer Basilio della Scola, wrapped around the old gate tower of 1421–31 that Grand Master d'Aubusson sealed shut; above it the arms of Villiers de L'Isle‑Adam, the last Grand Master of Rhodes. Historians call it a prototype of the modern bastion. Behind it the "terreplein of Spain" — a huge earth‑and‑stone embankment — was, per Wikipedia's summary of the fortifications, honeycombed with underground passages "designed for defensive collapse if needed": the Knights built tunnels meant to be blown up under any enemy who got into them. (Whether any gallery is open to visitors in 2026 I could not verify — see open questions.)

Next the **Tower of Spain**, where on 24 September 1522 the Ottomans got onto the wall and Fra Jacques de Bourbon led knights back through the underground passages to retake it. The long **English** curtain follows, then the **Bastion of Italy / del Carretto** at the harbour corner: a great round drum built where, on 27 July 1480, some 2,500 Janissaries stormed the "tower of Italy" and broke into the city. Grand Master d'Aubusson, "wounded in five places," fought at the breach with a lance; after three hours "between three and four thousand Turks were slain" and the army fled, abandoning the commander's tent and standard. Grand Master Fabrizio del Carretto later rebuilt the corner as the cannon‑platform you see. The Akandia Gate beside it is a 1930s Italian cut through the old Italian terreplein.

## Monte Smith — the Acropolis

Thirty to forty minutes uphill from the Old Town (leave by the Gate of St Athanasios at the south‑west corner and follow Komninon then Diagoridon), or a 20‑minute walk from the d'Amboise Gate to the car park. It is an open, free archaeological park, "accessible all day and evening." The hill is named for Admiral Sir Sidney Smith — the man who stopped Napoleon at Acre — who, local tradition says, set an observation post here in 1802 to watch for the French fleet; Wikipedia confirms "the site of the acropolis in Rhodes town is still named 'Monte Smith' after the admiral."

The Temple of Pythian Apollo crowns the summit: four Doric columns and a scrap of architrave re‑erected by the Italians, bombed in WWII, restored in the 1960s–70s and 1996 — 2025 reviewers found them scaffolded and fenced, so treat it as a silhouette, not a walk‑in. Below it the 210 m Hellenistic stadium keeps its sphendone, VIP seats and the athletes' starting mechanism; beside it the little marble Odeon (~800 seats, almost all Italian reconstruction — "only two or three seats in the first row and a few steps are original"). Further along the ridge are the four rock‑cut Nymphaea, cave sanctuaries with cisterns, and the stump of the Temple of Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus where the city kept its treaties. The view is the reason to climb: the whole town, both harbours and the Turkish coast.

## Roloi — the clock tower

Orfeos Street, the highest point inside the walls. Built in 1852 by Fethi Pasha on a Byzantine tower base, rebuilt after the 1856 gunpowder explosion that gutted the Knights' church of St John. €5, which includes a drink (beer, wine, coffee or soft drink) in the courtyard bar; 53 steep wooden steps to a chamber with four small open windows: the Palace to the north, Monte Smith west, the harbour and cruise ships east, the minarets of Suleiman's mosque to the south‑east. Only about four people fit at the top. Hours are not reliably published (aggregators say 09:00–21:00; a reviewer notes an annual closure November–May); sunset is the famous slot but the ship sails at 18:00, so make it a 15:30 birthday drink after the walls.

## Mandraki, the windmills and Fort St Nicholas

From Liberty Gate the promenade runs out along the mole past the three surviving Genoese‑era windmills (of thirteen) to the Fort of St Nicholas, built 1464–67 by Grand Master Zacosta on the site of a chapel of St Nicholas. In 1480 Mesih Pasha's guns bombarded it continuously from 9 June and hurled assault after assault at it; it held, and d'Aubusson later wrapped it in a new outer bastion. A lighthouse has burned on its roof since the mid‑17th century. Walk the mole (uneven at the far end); the fort interior is usually closed. About 15–20 minutes from Hippocrates Square.

## Elli beach and the Aquarium

Ten minutes on past the deer columns and the casino (the Italian Grande Albergo delle Rose, 1925–27) is Elli, the spearhead‑shaped pebble beach at the island's tip, with its diving platform anchored offshore — Durrell swam here. At the very point stands the Aquarium, Armando Bernabiti's Art Deco Reale Istituto di Ricerche Biologiche (built 1934–35, opened 1937), now HCMR's Hydrobiological Station: a basement corridor built to look like an underwater cave, black‑and‑white pebble floors, a 2,000‑year‑old monk‑seal skeleton. Open 09:00–20:30 April–October; adult ticket reported at about €6 (unverified).

## Murad Reis and Villa Cleobolus

Opposite the sea at Georgiou Papanikolaou 30, behind a blue door, is the mosque of Murad Reis, corsair‑admiral of Rhodes, and its cemetery of exiles: Ottoman grandees with turban‑topped stones, a satirical poet, and in the centre the domed türbe of Canbek Giray, Khan of Crimea, deposed in 1634 and dead here "on Saturday night, 15 November 1636." In a corner sits the tiny Villa Cleobolus where Lawrence Durrell lived 1945–47 and wrote *Reflections on a Marine Venus* — "in the evenings we gather for drinks and gossip, sitting in cane chairs around the little painted table." Warning: a March 2025 visitor found the gates locked and the site "falling into disrepair"; Tripadvisor now lists it as closed. Look through the bars if so.

## Rodini and the Tomb of the Ptolemies

A 35–40 minute walk south (or bus from the market), Rodini is the Hellenistic necropolis of Korakonero, turned by the Italians in 1929–30 into a ravine park of dams, lakes, bridges and peacocks — "the world's first landscaped park" in local telling. Ten minutes up the path is the rock‑cut "Tomb of the Ptolemies," a 28.5 m square mausoleum with 21 Doric half‑columns and a pedimented door — a Hellenistic noble's tomb, not really Ptolemy's. Free; reviews of neglect are common. Skip it if the day is tight.

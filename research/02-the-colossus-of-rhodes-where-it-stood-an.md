# The Colossus of Rhodes: where it stood, and its afterlife (location theories, the 653/654 camel story, modern rebuild schemes, Mandraki deer and Fort St Nicholas)

## The wonder nobody can find

Start with what is certain. Around 292 BC the Rhodians, having outlasted Demetrius "the Besieger", sold his abandoned siege machinery (Pliny says the scrap fetched 300 talents) and commissioned Chares of Lindos, pupil of Lysippus, to raise a bronze Helios seventy cubits tall — roughly 32–34 m, the height of a modern ten-storey building. The dedication survives in the Greek Anthology (6.171): *"To thy very self, O Sun, did the people of Dorian Rhodes raise high to heaven this colossus ... Not only over the sea, but on the land, too, did they establish the lovely light of unfettered freedom."* Twelve years to build; it stood about sixty-six. Then, in 227/226 BC, an earthquake snapped it at the knees. Strabo, writing two centuries later, saw it: *"it now lies on the ground, having been thrown down by an earthquake and broken off at the knees"* — and, he adds, an oracle forbade the Rhodians to raise it again. Pliny the Elder gives the tourist's-eye view of the wreck: *"Few people can make their arms meet round the thumb ... where the limbs have been broken off enormous cavities yawn, while inside are seen great masses of rock with the weight of which the artist steadied it."* That is the single most important fact for a walker: the Colossus lay on land, in pieces, for eight or nine centuries, visited like a fallen dragon's skeleton. Nobody in antiquity thought to write down where.

## Theory one: the harbour-straddler (a medieval ghost)

The image everyone carries — legs planted on two moles, ships gliding between — is not ancient. Its earliest trace is a Campanian notary, Niccolò de Martoni, who came through in 1394/5 and wrote down what the locals told him: *"At the end of the mole there is a certain church named St Nicolas. And a great wonder was told and confirmed to me: in olden times there was a great statue ... which had one foot on the end of the mole where the church of St Nicolas stood, while the other foot rested on the end of the other mole where the mills are."* Those two moles are still there — the St Nicholas mole on your left, the windmill mole on your right — and they are about 400 m apart. A 33 m figure cannot stride 400 m; engineers add that bronze legs splayed even 12 m apart would buckle under the statue's own weight, and that a statue which fell across the harbour mouth would have closed the port, whereas Pliny describes it lying on ground you could walk around. German scholars trace the legend to the humanist court of Grand Master Juan Fernández de Heredia (1377–96), a learned misreading of the epigram's "over land and sea". The picture was fixed for ever in 1572 when Philips Galle engraved Maarten van Heemskerck's design for the *Octo Mundi Miracula* in Antwerp: Helios astride the harbour, a smoking urn in his raised hand, worshippers at his feet, and in the foreground workmen scrubbing the giant's severed head. Shakespeare's Cassius ("he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus") and Emma Lazarus's "brazen giant ... with conquering limbs astride" both descend from this print. Fischer von Erlach (1721) and the Comte de Caylus (1752) already called it impossible; it never mattered.

## Theory two: the St Nicholas mole (Hoepfner)

Local tradition — and most postcards — puts the statue where Fort St Nicholas now sits at the tip of the Mandraki mole. Wolfram Hoepfner (*Der Koloss von Rhodos und die Bauten des Helios*, 2003) gave that tradition scholarly backing: an upright, dignified Helios of about 30 m, gilded hair and rays, standing "at the mole head of the war harbour" as a welcome to ships. The physical hook is inside the fort: its floor contains a circle of sandstone blocks of unknown purpose, and curved marble blocks built into the fort's fabric are cut far too finely for mere fill — the argued remnant of a marble drum-base (Paul Jordan, *Seven Wonders*, 2014, summarises the case). Against it: the mole itself is a medieval Knights' work on a low rock, and Nathan Badoud's 2024 Oxford monograph judges the whole harbour placement to rest on "post-Antique depictions" rather than evidence. The medieval fort is its own story: a tower raised 1464–67 by Grand Master Zacosta over a chapel of St Nicholas (attested 1394); the first Ottoman objective in the siege of 1480 (bombardment, then infantry assaults from 9 June, repulsed by d'Aubusson in person); afterwards ringed by d'Aubusson's bastion; a lighthouse on top since the 1600s, modernised in 1863. Restoration has dragged on for fifteen years — expect to walk around it, not into it.

## Theory three: the acropolis (Vedder) and the summit (Kebric)

Ursula Vedder (2015; Getty *Artistry in Bronze*, 2017) says the sanctuary matters more than the harbour: a votive Helios belongs in Helios's own precinct. She re-identifies the big Doric temple on Monte Smith — labelled "Apollo Pythios" since the Italian digs of the 1920s–30s — as the long-lost sanctuary of Helios, "above the stadium and the odeion", and reads a ruin north-east of the temple as "the remains of the base and the workshop", its footprint comparable to the surviving base of Nero's colossus in Rome (17.60 × 14.75 m). She also argues, against Philo of Byzantium's famous description of casting the statue in horizontal courses inside a rising mound of earth (*"a huge mound of earth around each section as soon as it was completed"*), that it must have been cast in large sections in nearby pits. Robert Kebric (2019) accepts her Helios identification but pushes the statue higher still — to the limestone shelf at the very top of Monte Smith beside the Temple of Athena Polias and Zeus Polieus, where it could act as a beacon for all five harbours and where, in 167 BC, the cowed Rhodians offered Rome a second, 45-foot colossus (Polybius). Badoud rejects the acropolis outright: that hill, he thinks, belonged to Apollo and Artemis.

## Theory four: under the Grand Master's Palace (Badoud)

The newest and currently best-argued answer is the oldest part of town. Badoud (*The Colossus of Rhodes: Archaeology of a Lost Wonder*, OUP 2024) places the sanctuary of Helios on the small hill at the head of the Collachium — "the site of the later Palace of the Grand Master or its environs" — citing an inscription naming a sacred area of Helios, dedications by his priests, and a marble head of the god found there (the radiant Helios head in the Archaeological Museum is the island's most famous face). A statue here would have been visible from the Peraia, the Rhodian territory on the Anatolian coast; Badoud reads the whole monument as a piece of sympathetic magic, "fixing" Helios at the centre of the new state. Earlier twentieth-century scholars had floated the same site and dropped it; the BMCR reviewer calls Badoud's case "convincing" and the book "the definitive study of a vanished Wonder". Consensus in 2026: not the harbour mouth; probably a Helios sanctuary; Palace hill leading, Monte Smith second, the St Nicholas mole a romantic third.

## The afterlife: 900 camels

Theophanes the Confessor, writing c. 810, records under the year 653/4: *"In this year Muawiyah overran Rhodes and destroyed the Colossus of Rhodes 1,370 years after its erection. A Jewish merchant from Edessa bought it and carried off its bronze on nine hundred camels."* Constantine Porphyrogenitus made it 980 camels; Bar Hebraeus, in Syriac, had the Arabs haul the statue down with ropes and weigh out "three thousand loads of Corinthian brass" for "a certain Jew from Emesa". Lawrence Conrad (JRAS 1996) showed that every version descends from a single Syriac source behind Theophanes, that the arithmetic is wrong, and that a pagan bronze scrapheap is unlikely to have survived Christianisation and the Persian wars intact; Badoud goes further and calls the tale a fabrication modelled on Daniel's vision of Nebuchadnezzar's shattered statue — an eighth-century monk's apocalypse omen, not a bill of lading. Nine hundred camels remains, nonetheless, the best line in the story.

## Rebuilding it (nobody has)

In 2008 East German light-artist Gert Hof proposed a 60–100 m "light sculpture" at the harbour mouth, part-cast from melted weapons, €200 million. In 2015, mid-debt-crisis, the *Colossus of Rhodes Project* (architect Ari A. Palla with a Greek–Spanish–Italian–British team) unveiled a gilded, solar-skinned giant of 122–150 m bestriding two piers, with library, museum and lighthouse inside, cost $250–283 million, to be crowdfunded. Its website has gone dark. The only things that did get built are the two limestone columns at Mandraki's mouth and their bronze stag and doe, Elafos and Elafina — installed in 1939 by the Fascist governor De Vecchi's administration, modelled by Parma sculptor Renato Brozzi — marking the exact spot tradition gives the statue's feet, 62 m apart.

## Where to stand

Walk the mole to Fort St Nicholas and turn round: the deer columns and the three windmills frame de Martoni's impossible straddle. Then climb the Street of the Knights to the Palace forecourt and look north-east to the Turkish coast — Badoud's Helios faced that way. Finish, if time allows, at the re-erected columns of the "Apollo" temple on Monte Smith and the sunset ledge above it — Vedder's and Kebric's mountain of the Sun.

## Open questions
- Confirm the Scarlet Lady berth for 30 Sep 2026 (Tourist Port at Akti Sachtouri vs. Akandia) — distances above assume the Tourist Port.
- Is the interior of Fort St Nicholas open to visitors in autumn 2026? Sources conflict (kastra.eu: 'no access inside'; aegeanislands.gr: restored 'safe to be visited'). Without interior access the sandstone-block circle cannot be seen.
- Exact 30 Sep 2026 hours for the Palace of the Grand Master and the Archaeological Museum: the official site gives 08:00–20:00 for 1 Apr–31 Oct, an unofficial guide gives 08:00–19:00 for 16–30 Sept; Greek ministry hours change year to year — check hhticket.gr / archaeologicalmuseums.gr in September.
- Several guidebooks claim a Roman she-wolf (Lupa) once stood on one Mandraki column before being replaced by a deer after 1947; the 2026 Dimokratiki article on the deer says both deer went up in 1939 and stayed. Not verified either way.
- The task mentions reconstruction proposals going back to the 1970s; only the 2008 (Gert Hof) and 2015 (Colossus of Rhodes Project) schemes could be documented. Earlier Greek/municipal proposals need a source.
- Whether Vedder's 'ruin north-east of the temple' on Monte Smith is signposted or visible on the ground today (the terrace was excavated in 1938 and never fully published).
- Precise wording of Constantine VII's '980 camels' passage (De administrando imperio ch. 20–21) was not checked against an edition; the figure is taken from German Wikipedia.
- Whether the Palace's 'Ancient Rhodes' exhibition still displays the Demetrius siege stones and any Colossus material in 2026 (Kebric saw them in 2019).
- BMCR names the fabricator of the camel story as 'Theophanes of Edessa'; the German literature says the source is Theophilus of Edessa's lost chronicle — probably a slip in the review, but worth confirming in Badoud's book.
